Slides 3–8: Fed-State Fatty-Acid Synthesis

Selected figure from slide 4. ACC generates malonyl-CoA and sits at the central regulatory step of fatty-acid synthesis.

In the fed state, insulin signals that energy and carbon are abundant. Mitochondrial acetyl-CoA is exported indirectly as citrate, regenerated in the cytosol, and committed to fatty-acid synthesis by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC).

ACC is the key control point. It converts acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA, requires biotin and ATP, and is favored by the fed/anabolic state.
Essential fatty acids: humans cannot introduce double bonds beyond the Δ9 position, so linoleic and α-linolenic acids must come from the diet.

Slides 9–18: Fasting-State Mobilization and β-Oxidation

Selected figure from slide 15. The carnitine shuttle moves long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria; CPT-I is the reciprocal-control gate.

Fasting reverses the logic. Adipose triglyceride is hydrolyzed and free fatty acids are released to circulate on albumin. Long-chain fatty acids must then use the carnitine shuttle to enter the mitochondrial matrix before β-oxidation can proceed.

The molecule that prevents futile cycling
Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I. High malonyl-CoA means synthesis is ON and mitochondrial long-chain FA entry is OFF.

β-oxidation repeats four reactions—oxidation, hydration, oxidation, thiolysis—shortening the fatty acid by two carbons each round while producing acetyl-CoA, NADH, and FADH₂.

Slides 19–23: MCAD Deficiency Is a Failure of Fasting Adaptation

Selected figure from slide 21. MCAD deficiency causes fasting intolerance, impaired ketone production, and hypoglycemia.

During fasting, fatty-acid oxidation should spare glucose and provide acetyl-CoA for ketone production. MCAD deficiency blocks oxidation of medium-chain fatty acids, so the patient becomes unusually dependent on glucose just when glucose availability is falling.

Mechanism
MCAD deficiency → impaired FA oxidation → low ketones + increased glucose use → hypoketotic hypoglycemia during fasting/illness.
The lecture's SIDS association follows the same physiology: a previously unrecognized infant can decompensate during a period of poor intake.

Slides 24–25: Odd-Chain Fatty Acids Feed the TCA Cycle

Selected figure from slide 24. The final 3-carbon product of odd-chain fatty acids is propionyl-CoA, which can be converted to succinyl-CoA.

Slides 26–29: Peroxisomes Handle the Fatty Acids Mitochondria Cannot

Selected figure from slide 27. The peroxisomal disorders in the deck separate VLCFA handling, organelle biogenesis, and branched-chain α-oxidation.

X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy

ABCD1-related failure of VLCFA transport into peroxisomes → very-long-chain fatty-acid accumulation, demyelination, adrenal involvement.

Zellweger syndrome

Peroxisome biogenesis defect → broad peroxisomal failure and severe infantile multisystem disease.

Refsum disease

Defective α-oxidation of phytanic acid → retinitis pigmentosa, ataxia, anosmia, peripheral neuropathy.

Reciprocal control

The same malonyl-CoA/CPT-I relationship that prevents futile cycling is revisited here as the central regulator linking synthesis to oxidation.

Slides 31–34: Ketones Export Liver Acetyl-CoA to Other Tissues

Selected figure from slide 32. Acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone are generated from excess hepatic acetyl-CoA.

When fatty-acid oxidation is high, acetyl-CoA accumulates in liver mitochondria. If oxaloacetate is being redirected toward gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle cannot consume all of that acetyl-CoA. Ketogenesis turns it into water-soluble fuel.

Extrahepatic tissues can convert ketone bodies back to acetyl-CoA. The liver cannot, because it lacks thiophorase.
