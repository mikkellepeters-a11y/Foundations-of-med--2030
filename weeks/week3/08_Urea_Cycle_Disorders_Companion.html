Official Week 3 Objectives

1. Explain how tissues produce ammonia that is subsequently captured into urea in the liver and then excreted by the kidney.
2. Describe how, why, and where transaminases, glutamate dehydrogenase, and glutaminase detoxify/handle ammonia.
3. Discuss how defects in urea-cycle enzymes cause disease.
4. Describe the causes, symptoms, and treatment of hyperammonemia.

Slides 4–9: Collecting Nitrogen Before It Becomes Toxic

Selected figure from slide 4. Amino acids contribute a carbon skeleton for energy/metabolism while their nitrogen must be safely disposed of.

Amino acids are not stored as a dedicated reserve. Their carbon skeletons can feed glucose production, the TCA cycle, ketone formation, or specialized-product synthesis, but the nitrogen component creates the problem: free ammonia is toxic.

Transamination solves the first half of the problem. Aminotransferases move amino groups from many amino acids onto α-ketoglutarate, creating glutamate. This concentrates nitrogen onto one carrier rather than allowing free ammonia to circulate.
Selected figure from slide 7. ALT and AST illustrate the transamination pattern and the requirement for pyridoxal phosphate.
Transamination
Amino acid + α-ketoglutarate ⇌ α-ketoacid + glutamate. Aminotransferases require PLP, the active form of vitamin B6.

Glutamate then becomes the key nitrogen collector. In liver mitochondria, glutamate dehydrogenase removes its amino group by oxidative deamination, regenerating α-ketoglutarate and releasing ammonia in the organ that can immediately funnel that ammonia into the urea cycle.

ALT/AST are also clinical markers. The lecture emphasizes that cell injury releases them into plasma. It also contrasts low BUN in severe liver dysfunction with high BUN when kidney excretion fails.

Slides 10–14: Different Tissues Package Nitrogen Differently

Selected figure from slide 10. Peripheral tissues, especially brain, move toxic nitrogen into glutamine for safe transport to liver.

Brain / most tissues

Glutamine synthetase captures ammonia by converting glutamate to glutamine. Glutamine travels in blood to the liver, where glutaminase releases ammonia for urea synthesis.

Muscle

Muscle can package nitrogen in alanine. The glucose-alanine cycle moves alanine to liver, where ALT regenerates pyruvate for gluconeogenesis and transfers nitrogen back to glutamate.

Selected figure from slide 12. When ammonia rises in the brain, detoxification consumes α-ketoglutarate and threatens TCA-cycle energy production.
Why hyperammonemia injures brain
High NH₃ drives α-ketoglutarate → glutamate → glutamine. Depleting α-ketoglutarate impairs the TCA cycle and ATP generation while glutamine also accumulates.

The lecture calls the resulting neurologic syndrome hepatic encephalopathy when liver failure prevents normal ammonia disposal. Asterixis, disorientation, lethargy, and eventually coma are linked to rising ammonia.

Selected figure from slide 14. Kidney glutaminase generates ammonia that buffers urinary acid by trapping H+ as NH4+.
Kidney is different: it intentionally generates ammonia from glutamine so NH₃ can bind H⁺ and be excreted as NH₄⁺. During metabolic acidosis, renal glutaminase activity rises.

Slides 15–18: The Urea Cycle Converts Toxic Nitrogen Into an Excretable Product

Selected figure from slide 15. The first two steps occur in mitochondria; citrulline then enters the cytosol for the remaining reactions.

The cycle begins in liver mitochondria. CPS-I captures free ammonia with CO₂ to make carbamoyl phosphate. Ornithine transcarbamoylase then combines carbamoyl phosphate with ornithine to form citrulline. Citrulline exits to the cytosol, where aspartate contributes the second nitrogen and the pathway proceeds through argininosuccinate and arginine to release urea and regenerate ornithine.

StepCore eventCompartment
1CPS-I: NH₃ + CO₂ → carbamoyl phosphateMitochondria
2OTC: carbamoyl phosphate + ornithine → citrullineMitochondria
3Citrulline + aspartate → argininosuccinateCytosol
4Argininosuccinate → arginine + fumarateCytosol
5Arginine → urea + ornithineCytosol
Rate-limiting control
CPS-I is rate limiting and requires N-acetylglutamate (NAG) as an allosteric activator.

Urea then circulates to the kidneys for excretion. The deck repeatedly reinforces the organ distinction: liver makes urea; kidney excretes urea.

Slides 21–28: Urea-Cycle Disorders Are Pattern-Recognition Diseases

Selected figure from slide 24. The disorders share hyperammonemia; the differentiator is the specific substrate/product pattern around the blocked enzyme.

Most urea-cycle disorders in the lecture share poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, hypotonia, seizures, and low BUN because nitrogen cannot be converted efficiently into urea. The high-yield question is therefore not “does this patient have hyperammonemia?” but where is the block?

Treating Hyperammonemia: Reduce Nitrogen Load and Create Alternate Exit Routes

Selected figure from slide 28. The lecture emphasizes low protein intake plus nitrogen-scavenging strategies.

Reducing dietary protein reduces incoming nitrogen, but adequate calories are also important so the patient does not break down muscle protein and create more amino-acid nitrogen.

Sodium benzoate

Conjugates with glycine and permits nitrogen loss as hippurate.

Phenylacetate / phenylbutyrate

Uses glutamine as an excretable nitrogen carrier; phenylbutyrate can serve as an oral precursor to phenylacetate.
