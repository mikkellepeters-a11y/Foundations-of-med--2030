Slides 3–8: Dietary Hexoses Become Dangerous When Carbon or Phosphate Gets Trapped

Selected figure from slide 4. Galactose disorders are best learned by where the pathway is blocked and what accumulates.

Galactose and fructose are not alternative energy systems; they are entry routes into central carbohydrate metabolism. Their disorders become clinically important when an intermediate accumulates upstream of a block or when phosphate is trapped in a metabolite that cannot be processed.

Classic galactosemia

GALT deficiency causes galactose-1-phosphate accumulation. The sick infant appears after lactose-containing feeds, with feeding intolerance and hepatic injury; galactitol contributes to cataracts.

Galactokinase deficiency

Galactose cannot be phosphorylated normally and is diverted toward galactitol. Cataracts dominate; systemic toxicity is much less severe than classic GALT deficiency.

Selected figure from slide 6. Fructokinase and aldolase B deficiencies produce very different phenotypes because only one creates a dangerous phosphate trap.
Fructose mechanism
Fructokinase deficiency → benign essential fructosuria. Aldolase B deficiency → fructose-1-phosphate trapping → ↓ free phosphate and ATP → impaired glycogenolysis/gluconeogenesis → hypoglycemia.
Clinical discriminator: a child who becomes ill after fruit or juice is introduced fits hereditary fructose intolerance far better than essential fructosuria.

Slides 9–10: The Polyol Pathway Explains Osmotic Tissue Injury

Selected figure from slide 9. Aldose reductase uses NADPH to produce sorbitol or galactitol; further metabolism depends on the tissue.

Aldose reductase converts glucose to sorbitol and galactose to galactitol. When a tissue cannot efficiently metabolize the sugar alcohol further, the osmotically active product accumulates and water follows it. This is the unifying mechanism behind lens injury in galactose disorders and the lecture's diabetic polyol-pathway complications.

Connection to the next section: aldose reductase consumes NADPH, while the pentose phosphate pathway exists in part to generate NADPH.

Slides 11–17: PPP Is the RBC's Antioxidant Lifeline

Selected figure from slide 11. The pentose phosphate pathway produces NADPH and pentose sugars rather than ATP.

The oxidative arm of the PPP generates NADPH; the pathway also supplies ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis. In RBCs, NADPH is especially important because it keeps glutathione reduced, allowing peroxide detoxification.

RBC antioxidant chain
G6PD → NADPH → glutathione reductase → reduced glutathione → glutathione peroxidase → peroxide detoxification.
Selected figure from slide 16. Oxidant-damaged hemoglobin forms Heinz bodies; splenic removal of damaged material creates bite cells.

G6PD deficiency therefore does not simply cause “low ATP.” The critical problem is insufficient reducing power during oxidant stress. Hemoglobin denatures, the membrane is damaged, and episodic hemolysis follows.

Clinical pattern: the enzyme defect may be clinically silent until oxidative stress raises the burden on the glutathione system.

Slides 18–23: ROS Can Be Toxic—or Deliberately Weaponized

Selected figure from slide 19. The respiratory burst uses NADPH oxidase to generate superoxide in activated phagocytes.

The same broad chemistry that damages RBCs is intentionally used by neutrophils and other phagocytes to kill microbes. NADPH oxidase initiates the respiratory burst by producing superoxide. Downstream reactions generate additional reactive species, while antioxidant enzymes keep that chemistry from injuring the host indiscriminately.

Do not confuse the two deficiencies: G6PD deficiency = poor protection from oxidants in RBCs. NADPH oxidase deficiency = poor generation of the phagocyte oxidative burst.
