Epithelia Are Polarized, Avascular Barriers

Selected figure from slide 5. The deck emphasizes tightly packed cells with little extracellular space and no blood vessels within the epithelial layer.

Epithelium is one of the four fundamental tissue types, alongside connective, muscle, and nervous tissue. It covers external surfaces and lines internal cavities and tubes. Its defining structural feature is a continuous sheet of tightly connected cells.

Highly cellular

Cells sit close together and are physically linked by junctions, creating a barrier.

Avascular

Nutrients and oxygen must diffuse from underlying connective tissue.

Polarized

Apical, lateral, and basal domains contain different proteins and perform different jobs.

Basal lamina

ECM-rich layer anchoring epithelium to underlying connective tissue.

Selected figure from slide 6. Apical, lateral, and basal domains create directional transport and specialized function.
Polarity
Apical faces lumen/outside; basal faces basement membrane/connective tissue; lateral surfaces contact neighboring cells.

Exocrine Glands Are Specialized Invaginations of Epithelium

Epithelial cells can specialize for secretion either as isolated cells—such as goblet cells—or as multicellular glands formed by epithelial invagination into underlying connective tissue.

Selected figure from slide 33. Exocrine glands retain a duct to the surface; endocrine glands lose that connection and secrete toward blood.
ModeMechanismLecture example
MerocrineExocytosis; cell remains intactMany sweat/salivary-type secretions
ApocrineApical portion pinches off with secretionApocrine gland pattern
HolocrineEntire cell disintegrates to release contentsSebaceous glands
Secretory logic
Merocrine = exocytose • Apocrine = apex pinches off • Holocrine = whole cell dies/releases contents.

Regeneration, Metaplasia, EMT, and Cancer

Selected figure from slide 15. Stratified squamous epithelium protects against mechanical stress and is renewed from basal stem/progenitor cells.

Epithelia turn over because the barrier is constantly exposed to friction, chemical injury, and environmental stress. Stem/progenitor cells in the basal region replace lost cells. Controlled proliferation maintains the barrier; misregulated proliferation can support carcinoma.

Metaplasia is an adaptive change in epithelial identity. The lecture uses smoking-related conversion of normal airway epithelium toward stratified squamous epithelium as a maladaptive example that sacrifices specialized airway function.
Selected figure from slide 31. Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition reduces epithelial adhesion/polarity and increases motility, helping explain cancer invasion and metastasis.

EMT is a gene-expression program in which epithelial cells lose aspects of polarity/adhesion and acquire motile, mesenchymal-like properties. The lecture uses it to explain how carcinoma cells can detach and invade.
