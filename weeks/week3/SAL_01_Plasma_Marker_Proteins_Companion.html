Plasma, Serum, and Why the Difference Matters

Selected figure from slide 3. The lecture separates formed elements from plasma and connects plasma-versus-serum handling to laboratory interpretation.

Plasma is the liquid portion of anticoagulated blood and retains fibrinogen and other clotting factors. Serum is what remains after clotting, so fibrinogen and several consumed clotting factors are absent. That distinction matters when interpreting electrophoresis and laboratory marker panels.

ESR is a nonspecific inflammation marker. The lecture links faster sedimentation to factors such as increased fibrinogen/IgM and lower hematocrit, whereas dehydration/high hematocrit slows sedimentation.

Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Proteins: Functional Residents vs Damage Markers

Selected figure from slide 4. Intrinsic proteins are secreted into plasma for physiologic roles; extrinsic/marker proteins enter plasma after cell injury.

Intrinsic plasma proteins

Mostly synthesized by liver; immunoglobulins are the major exception and come from plasma cells. These proteins maintain pressure, transport ligands, support immunity/clotting, and regulate physiology.

Extrinsic / marker proteins

Normally intracellular. Tissue injury releases them into plasma, so their presence or rise can identify the injured tissue or the timing of injury.

Most intrinsic plasma proteins are glycoproteins. The deck explains clearance through progressive desialylation followed by recognition of exposed carbohydrate by hepatic asialoglycoprotein receptors.

Albumin: Oncotic Pressure, Transport, and Lab Interpretation

Selected figure from slide 10. Albumin supplies most plasma oncotic pressure and helps keep fluid inside the vascular compartment.

Albumin is the most abundant plasma protein and is responsible for most of the plasma oncotic pressure in this lecture. When albumin falls, the inward osmotic force across capillary walls falls and edema becomes more likely.

Albumin is also a carrier. It binds fatty acids, bilirubin, drugs, hormones, calcium, and other poorly soluble ligands, changing both their transport and free concentration.
Selected figure from slide 12. Total calcium falls with hypoalbuminemia because the albumin-bound pool falls, even when ionized calcium is preserved.
Pseudohypocalcemia: low albumin can lower total calcium without lowering ionized calcium. The deck specifically emphasizes direct ionized-calcium measurement as the reliable way to establish true calcium status in hypoalbuminemia.
A/G ratio
Normal lecture range ≈ 1.2–1.4. Low albumin or increased globulins lowers the ratio; low globulins can raise it. The ratio is a pattern clue, not a disease-specific diagnosis.

Acute-Phase Proteins: The Liver Changes the Plasma Proteome During Inflammation

Selected figure from slide 14. The acute-phase response changes circulating protein concentrations during inflammation, infection, trauma, infarction, and related stress.

The lecture defines acute-phase proteins by a substantial change in concentration during inflammatory stress. Positive acute-phase reactants rise; negative acute-phase reactants fall as hepatic synthesis is reprioritized.

Serum Marker Proteins: Release Kinetics Create Diagnostic Windows

Selected figure from slide 21. Marker proteins are intracellular molecules that enter plasma when cell integrity is compromised.

Marker-protein interpretation depends on more than whether a protein is present. The lecture emphasizes molecular size, cellular location, tissue specificity, the amount of tissue injured, clearance rate, and timing relative to the injury.

Marker kinetics
Small cytosolic proteins are generally released faster; membrane-associated or larger proteins appear more slowly. Faster clearance gives an earlier but narrower diagnostic window.
Selected figure from slide 25. The comprehensive metabolic panel includes markers such as ALP and transaminases, but the lecture separates tissue injury tests from true organ-function tests.
Important distinction: a tissue-damage marker can tell you cells are injured; it does not automatically measure how well the organ is functioning.

Creatine Kinase Isoenzymes: Same Enzyme Family, Different Tissue Distribution

Selected figure from slide 27. CK is used to illustrate why isoenzymes can improve tissue localization.

Creatine kinase exists as tissue-associated isoenzymes built from M and B subunits. The lecture uses these patterns to show how a rise in total CK becomes more informative when the isoenzyme distribution is known.

IsoenzymeLecture association
CK-MMPredominant skeletal-muscle form

Diagnostic principle: isoenzyme analysis increases tissue specificity compared with total enzyme activity alone.
