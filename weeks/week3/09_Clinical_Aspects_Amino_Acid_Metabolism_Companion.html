Slides 2–4: Only Learn the Classification That Changes Clinical Reasoning

The lecture explicitly says not to memorize a giant amino-acid classification table. The useful rule is simpler: glucogenic amino acids generate pyruvate or TCA-cycle intermediates that can support glucose production; ketogenic amino acids generate acetyl-CoA/acetoacetate-type products that can support ketone formation.

Lecture-specific “do not overlearn” point: keep the broad glucogenic-versus-ketogenic distinction and the selected disease-relevant amino acids, rather than memorizing every carbon fate.

Slides 5–12: Branched-Chain Amino Acids Share a Pathway—So Their Diseases Share Symptoms

Selected figure from slide 5. Valine, leucine, and isoleucine are all essential BCAAs and share the initial steps of catabolism.

Valine, leucine, and isoleucine are primarily metabolized in peripheral tissues, especially muscle. Their first step is transamination; the resulting α-ketoacids are then processed by the mitochondrial branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase complex (BCKD).

BCKD cofactors emphasized in the lecture
Thiamine (B1), lipoic acid, riboflavin (B2/FAD), niacin (B3/NAD), and pantothenate (B5/CoA).
Selected figure from slide 6. BCKD deficiency causes accumulation of BCAAs and their α-ketoacids; the E3 subunit is shared with other dehydrogenase complexes.
MSUD is therefore a block after transamination: the amino acids and their α-ketoacids accumulate. The lecture's clinical anchors are neonatal onset, sweet/burnt-sugar odor, poor feeding, lethargy, alternating hypertonia/hypotonia, seizures, and severe neurologic deterioration if untreated.
E3 clue: if BCAA ketoacids accumulate plus pyruvate/lactic acidosis, the deck points toward a shared E3-subunit problem because BCKD and pyruvate dehydrogenase both depend on that subunit.

Slides 14–23: Phenylalanine and Tyrosine Disorders Are a Pathway Map

Selected figure from slide 14. Phenylalanine hydroxylase and BH4 connect phenylalanine to tyrosine; downstream tyrosine products include pigment and neurotransmitter pathways.

Phenylalanine normally becomes tyrosine through phenylalanine hydroxylase with BH4. A block raises phenylalanine and diverts carbon toward phenylketones while making tyrosine conditionally essential.

Typical PKU

Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency. High Phe, musty/mousy odor, hypopigmentation, seizures/intellectual disability if untreated. Treatment in the deck centers on Phe restriction and Tyr supplementation.

Atypical PKU

BH4-regeneration defect (DHPR in the lecture). Because BH4 also supports neurotransmitter synthesis, the biochemical consequences extend beyond Phe accumulation.

Maternal PKU: the current lecture emphasizes that uncontrolled maternal phenylalanine can injure the fetus even when the fetus is not affected with PKU, producing congenital and neurodevelopmental abnormalities.

Slides 27–31: Methionine, Folate, B12, and Homocysteine

Selected figure from slide 28. The methionine/folate cycle links SAM methyl donation, B12-dependent remethylation, and B6-dependent transsulfuration.

Methionine is converted to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), a major methyl-group donor. After donating its methyl group, SAM ultimately yields homocysteine. Homocysteine then has two major fates: remethylation back to methionine or entry into the transsulfuration pathway toward cysteine.

Homocystinuria pattern
Elevated homocysteine + thrombosis + tall/thin habitus + downward/inward lens dislocation. The lecture contrasts this with upward lens displacement in Marfan syndrome.
