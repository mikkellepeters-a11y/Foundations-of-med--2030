Official Week 3 Objectives

1. Explain utilization of biomolecules as energy sources under different physiologic conditions and integrate pathways in liver, adipose, brain, and muscle.
2. Discuss functions of the primary metabolic organs and key hormonal regulators of intermediary metabolism.
3. Describe interorgan interactions in the fed state and relate them to insulin and glucagon levels.
4. Describe interorgan interactions in the fasting state and relate them to insulin and glucagon levels.

Slides 3–6: Fuel Choice Depends on Oxygen, Substrate Availability, and Tissue

Selected figure from slide 3. Glucose oxidation connects glycolysis, pyruvate/acetyl-CoA, TCA cycle, reducing equivalents, ETC, and ATP generation.

When glucose and oxygen are available, cells with mitochondria can use glycolysis plus the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. Cells without mitochondria—most notably RBCs—cannot do that and remain dependent on anaerobic glycolysis.

Low oxygen / impaired mitochondria

Cells rely more heavily on glycolysis and lactate production. The lecture distinguishes hypoperfusion/hypoxia-associated type A lactic acidosis from type B lactic acidosis when mitochondrial handling is impaired despite oxygen availability.

Low glucose, oxygen intact

Most tissues can increase fatty-acid oxidation; the liver can make ketone bodies. Brain cannot use albumin-bound fatty acids but can use ketones during prolonged fasting.

Selected figure from slide 5. As glucose becomes limited, fatty acids, ketone bodies, amino-acid carbon skeletons, glycerol, and nitrogen markers become integrated into the fuel map.
Integration means pathway permission, not just substrate availability. The lecture explicitly points out that a metabolite can be present but hormonal state can prevent the corresponding pathway from being active.

Slides 7–10: Each Organ Has a Different Metabolic Job

Selected figure from slide 8. The organ table is the working-memory map for integrated metabolism questions.

AdiposeStores triglyceride; releases free fatty acids and glycerol during fasting.

The lecture lists several layers of regulation—enzyme allostery, covalent modification, enzyme synthesis, compartmentalization, tissue-specific isoenzymes/transporters—and then adds organism-level control through hormones, neuronal signals, and diet.

Slides 11–17: Insulin and Glucagon Are the Main Nutritional-State Switch

Selected figure from slide 13. Insulin favors glucose use and storage plus lipid/protein synthesis—an anabolic pattern.
Insulin = fed/anabolic signal. High glucose stimulates insulin release. Across the lecture, insulin promotes glucose uptake where appropriate, glycogen formation, glycolysis, lipid synthesis/storage, and protein synthesis while suppressing fasting pathways.
Selected figure from slide 15. Glucagon acts through cAMP/PKA-dependent phosphorylation in liver and favors catabolic fuel mobilization.
Lecture mnemonic
“Phasting – Phosphorylates.” During fasting, glucagon/PKA-dependent phosphorylation tends to activate catabolic programs and turn off anabolic ones in liver.

Glucagon's major human target in this lecture is the liver. The deck also emphasizes tissue-specific regulation: catecholamine signaling can inhibit hepatic glycolysis while stimulating glycolysis in working skeletal muscle because the relevant regulatory isoforms differ by tissue.

Slides 18–21: Fed State = Use Glucose First, Then Store the Excess

Selected figure from slide 20. In the fed state, high insulin directs carbohydrate first toward immediate use and glycogen, then increasingly toward lipid storage as caloric excess grows.

After a meal, circulating glucose and amino acids rise and the insulin:glucagon ratio is high. Glucose becomes the dominant fuel. Glycogen stores are replenished first; because glycogen storage is limited, continued caloric excess is diverted into fatty-acid, triglyceride, and cholesterol synthesis.

Liver

Glucose use + glycogen synthesis; excess carbon can support lipid synthesis.

Adipose

Insulin favors glucose entry and triglyceride storage while suppressing lipolysis.

Muscle

Uses glucose, replenishes glycogen, supports protein synthesis.

Brain / RBC

Continue to use glucose; RBCs remain anaerobic.

Slides 22–26: Fasting Progresses From Glycogen → Gluconeogenesis → Ketogenesis

Selected figure from slide 22. Fasting lowers the insulin:glucagon ratio and progressively shifts the body toward catabolic fuel mobilization.

As time from the last meal increases, insulin falls and glucagon rises. Adipose begins triglyceride breakdown, so plasma free fatty acids and glycerol increase. Liver initially maintains blood glucose with glycogenolysis, then relies more heavily on gluconeogenesis as glycogen runs down.

Fasting sequence
Liver glycogenolysis → gluconeogenesis supported by FA oxidation → increasing ketogenesis as fasting continues.
Selected figure from slide 24. Most tissues shift toward fatty acids while glucose is spared for brain and RBCs; liver uses adipose-derived fatty acids to power gluconeogenesis.

The deck emphasizes that even-chain fatty acids do not provide net carbon for gluconeogenesis. Their oxidation instead supplies the ATP needed to make glucose from glycerol and amino-acid carbon skeletons.

MCAD-style integration clue: prolonged fasting with very low glucose but unexpectedly absent ketones should make you think of impaired fatty-acid degradation/ketogenesis.

Slides 25–28: Brain Ketone Use Is a Protein-Sparing Survival Adaptation

Selected figure from slide 27. The lecture quantifies why replacing part of the brain's glucose requirement with ketone bodies greatly extends survival during starvation.

During prolonged starvation, ketone concentrations rise and the brain increasingly uses ketone bodies. This is not merely an alternate fuel choice; it reduces the amount of glucose that must be produced from amino acids and therefore slows loss of essential body protein.

Protein sparing is the point. Fat stores are much more expendable than structural/functional body protein. Shifting brain metabolism toward ketones lets fat-derived carbon cover more of the body's energy needs.
