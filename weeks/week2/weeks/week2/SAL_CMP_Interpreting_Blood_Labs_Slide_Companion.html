01Lab 101: why order a test?LO 2-3
Start with the patient, not the number. The deck explicitly says a laboratory result usually does not supersede a good history and physical examination.

What a lab can do

  • Detect disease or predisposition - example: HbA1c and diabetes.
  • Reject a diagnosis - example: D-dimer in an appropriate pulmonary embolism evaluation.
  • Establish prognosis - example: a prognostic genetic marker.
  • Guide management - example: antibiotic susceptibility in sepsis.
  • Monitor therapy - examples: HbA1c or TSH.

Lab medicine mindset

The deck notes that a large proportion of clinical decisions depend on laboratory testing, but that does not mean more testing is always better. The clinical question should determine the test, and the test should change what you do.

High-yield distinction
Useful testing answers a clinical question. Unnecessary testing can generate incidental abnormal values, additional workups, cost, and patient harm.
02Reference ranges: normal is statistical, not absoluteLO 2
The deck describes reference intervals using apparently healthy reference populations and a bell-shaped distribution. The commonly used "normal" interval captures the central 95% of values, so some healthy people necessarily fall outside it.
Current-deck diagram, page 9: the central region contains most reference values. The slide emphasizes 68% within 1 standard deviation, about 95% within 2, and 97.7% within 3.

Consequences

  • For a single test, an "out-of-range" result can occur from statistical variation.
  • The slide states that with 20 or more independent tests, there is a greater than 50% likelihood that at least one will be outside the normal range just by chance.
  • Interpret a flagged result in clinical context rather than treating the reference interval as a diagnosis.

Why ranges vary

  • Age, sex, physiologic state, and population characteristics.
  • Testing method, analyzer, specimen collection, and timing.
  • The laboratory's own reference interval matters.
  • Repeat measurements have unavoidable biological and analytic variation.
Faculty emphasis from the slide
The numeric normal ranges shown later in the deck are labeled "You do not need to memorize this." Learn patterns and interpretation, not a giant list of exact numbers.
03Overuse, misuse, underuse, and harmonizationLO 3 & 5
ProblemWhat it means in this deckExample / consequence
OveruseTesting without a strong clinical reason, such as reflexive daily panels in stable patients.Cost, resource waste, and "chasing" incidental abnormalities.
MisuseRight general domain, wrong timing or wrong assay.Protein C during an acute clot can mislead; choosing the wrong molecular test can misdiagnose.
UnderuseFailing to test when the result could identify a meaningful, treatable problem.Missing significant neonatal hyperbilirubinemia can allow preventable neurologic injury.
Harmonization
Harmonization means obtaining the same clinically acceptable result and interpretation despite differences in measurement procedure, units/reference intervals, time, or location. The deck names cholesterol, HbA1c, and creatinine as examples that have undergone harmonization. It also emphasizes why universal harmonization is difficult: cost, time, and vendor/implementation barriers.
04Newborn bilirubin: the classic underuse exampleLO 4
Current-deck bilirubin diagram, page 7: red blood cell breakdown produces heme, which is metabolized to unconjugated bilirubin.

Indirect / unconjugated

Has not yet been processed by the liver. The deck describes it as toxic and albumin-bound for transport.

Direct / conjugated

Has been processed by the liver, making it water-soluble and ready for excretion.

Why screen newborns? Newborns have increased red blood cell turnover plus physiologically immature livers. A marked rise in indirect bilirubin can cause kernicterus, a permanent form of brain injury. Phototherapy can convert bilirubin into a more water-soluble form and prevent this complication.

07How the lecture turns facts into casesLO 1-6

Low intake + fatigue

Several days of poor fluid intake should push you toward a dehydration pattern: concentration-related changes plus a disproportionate BUN rise.

Myeloma + high total protein

If total protein is markedly elevated but albumin is normal, the excess protein is elsewhere in the serum protein pool.

Kidney stone pain + high glucose

Acute pain can raise glucose. The deck asks for a better longer-term diabetes assessment: HbA1c.

Pancreatitis + low calcium

Think saponification.

Alcohol-related injury

The deck's case asks which aminotransferase rises more prominently: AST.

Newborn jaundice

Severe indirect hyperbilirubinemia is dangerous because of kernicterus; phototherapy is protective.

Protein C during acute thrombosis

This is the lecture's example of misuse by wrong timing, because the result can be misleading in the acute setting.

Routine daily panels in a stable patient

This is overuse: more testing can generate noise and unnecessary follow-up.

08Rapid recall mapALL
Reference range: central 95% -> healthy people can still be flagged. More independent tests -> more chances for an incidental abnormality.
BMP vs CMP: BMP = glucose + calcium + 4 electrolytes/acid-base markers + BUN + creatinine. CMP = BMP + albumin + total protein + bilirubin + ALP + ALT + AST.
Newborn bilirubin: immature liver + higher RBC turnover -> indirect bilirubin can rise -> kernicterus risk -> phototherapy prevents harm.
Utilization: overuse = unnecessary testing; misuse = wrong test or timing; underuse = missed treatable disease.
Harmonization: same clinically acceptable result/interpretation despite method, units, place, or time; difficult because it is expensive and operationally complex.
Pattern recognition: dehydration -> BUN/albumin concentration pattern; direct bilirubin -> obstruction; indirect bilirubin -> hemolysis/metabolic/hepatocellular patterns; ALP -> bile duct; AST/ALT -> cell injury; total protein high with normal albumin -> consider excess globulin/protein.
Before you move on: Can you explain why a flagged result can be "abnormal" without representing disease, list all 8 BMP tests, name the 6 CMP additions, explain newborn bilirubin screening, and recognize the lecture's dehydration / pancreatitis / myeloma / biliary obstruction patterns?
