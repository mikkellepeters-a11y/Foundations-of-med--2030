Clinical physiology connection · Ion channelopathies

Week 13 · Lecture 3 · Disease mechanism category, not one specific diagnosis

Ion channels control selective membrane permeability and the generation of graded and action potentials. Genetic changes, autoimmune interference, and channel-targeting toxins can disrupt neuronal or muscle-cell excitability. Depending on the ion channel and tissue involved, dysfunction may cause weakness, abnormal sensation, seizures, or cardiac electrical disturbances. Recognition pearl: distinguish altered channel gating/conductance from altered extracellular ion concentration; both change excitability, but by different mechanisms. This lecture introduces channelopathies as a category without developing a specific named genetic channelopathy.

Clinical anatomy connection · Referred pain & visceral afferents

Week 13 · Lecture 6 · Slides 29–31 · Not a standalone diagnosis

Visceral afferents carry nociceptive and reflexive sensory information from viscera, glands, and blood vessels toward the CNS. Consciously perceived visceral pain is often dull and poorly localized. Above the pelvic pain line, visceral pain fibers typically parallel sympathetic pathways back to dorsal root ganglia and spinal cord segments T1–L2. Below the pelvic pain line, visceral pain fibers typically parallel parasympathetic pelvic pathways back to S2–S4. The pelvic pain line corresponds to the inferior limit of parietal peritoneum in the lecture. Somatic and visceral afferents can converge onto central neurons; the brain may consequently attribute a visceral stimulus to a somatic region. Recognition pearl: referred pain is felt away from its visceral source; trace the afferent pathway rather than assuming a lesion at the painful skin site.

Clinical anatomy connection · Autonomic pathway dysfunction

Week 13 · Lecture 6 · Slides 5–23 · Anatomy-based mechanism, not a named disorder

Autonomic motor output uses a two-neuron chain: a preganglionic neuron originating in the CNS and a postganglionic neuron associated with an autonomic ganglion. Interrupting a pathway can disrupt smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, or glandular output. For the sympathetic system, preganglionic fibers begin in the T1–L2/3 intermediolateral column and enter the sympathetic trunk via white rami communicantes (available at these levels); postganglionic fibers may rejoin spinal nerves through gray rami communicantes (all spinal levels), travel via cardiopulmonary routes, or follow arteries from prevertebral ganglia to abdominopelvic targets. For the parasympathetic system, preganglionic fibers emerge through cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X or sacral segments S2–S4, with synapses in cranial or near-organ/intramural ganglia. Recognition pearl: localize an interruption as preganglionic, ganglionic, or postganglionic based on where the route fails, without attributing a specific syndrome not taught in the lecture.

Clinical anatomy connection · Batson’s plexus

Week 13 · Lecture 2 · Not a standalone diagnosis

The valveless internal vertebral (epidural) venous plexus communicates with pelvic and other venous systems. It can provide a route for metastatic spread or infection to the vertebral column and spinal region; the handout specifically uses prostate cancer as an example. Recognition pearl: valveless epidural veins → potential spread from pelvic veins to spine.
