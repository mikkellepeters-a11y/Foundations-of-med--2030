Anterior Arm & Cubital Fossa

Lecture Companion · Mindy Johnson, PhD · Course-first explanations

← Week 14Factoid Sheet →OsteologyMusclesNervesVesselsCubital fossa
Big picture. The anterior arm is organized for flexion at the elbow and shoulder and for forearm supination. Muscle attachments explain movement, nerve routes explain neurologic deficits, and the cubital fossa explains palpation, venipuncture, and vulnerable neurovascular relationships.

Learning objectives

  1. Identify humeral, radial, and ulnar landmarks and their articulations; consider fractures and radial-head subluxation.
  2. Explain anterior and posterior fascial compartments and associated neurovasculature.
  3. Give attachments, action, innervation and functional tests for anterior arm muscles.
  4. Define boundaries, floor, roof, and contents of the cubital fossa.
  5. Trace musculocutaneous, median and ulnar nerves and assess the biceps reflex.
  6. Trace arterial branches, superficial/deep veins and elbow collateral circulation.
  7. Explain long-head biceps tendinopathy and rupture.
  8. Identify structures on imaging (a listed objective; dedicated CT/MRI views are not supplied in these slides).

1 · Osteology of the arm and elbow

Humerus

Locate the head and anatomical neck proximally, and distinguish the surgical neck below the greater and lesser tubercles. The intertubercular (bicipital) groove houses the long-head biceps tendon. The shaft features the deltoid tuberosity and posterior radial (spiral) groove.

At the elbow the capitulum articulates with the radial head and the trochlea articulates with the ulna's trochlear notch. The anterior radial and coronoid fossae accommodate structures in flexion; the posterior olecranon fossa receives the olecranon during extension. The ulnar groove lies behind the medial epicondyle.

Proximal radius and ulna

Radius: head, neck, radial tuberosity. Ulna: olecranon, trochlear notch, coronoid process, radial notch, ulnar tuberosity. The radial tuberosity anchors the biceps tendon; the ulnar tuberosity and coronoid process anchor brachialis.

BOARD / CLINICAL ENRICHMENT: Humeral surgical-neck fracture may endanger the axillary nerve; a midshaft radial-groove fracture may injure the radial nerve; a medial epicondyle fracture may injure the ulnar nerve. Radial-head subluxation (nursemaid elbow) involves displacement of the annular ligament after longitudinal traction in a young child. These details explain the listed fracture/subluxation objective but are not developed in the slide text.

2 · Fascial compartments

Brachial fascia and medial/lateral intermuscular septa divide the arm. The lateral septum extends between the deltoid insertion and lateral humeral epicondyle.

Biceps brachii, brachialis, coracobrachialis; elbow/shoulder flexion, supination. Brachial artery and accompanying brachial veins. Although radial nerve is associated with the posterior compartment, the lecture also identifies a radial contribution to brachialis innervation—an important exception to overly simple compartment rules.

Why does the biceps supinate? Its tendon inserts on the rotating radius rather than the ulna. When it contracts, the radius rotates to bring the palm anteriorly/upward. Brachialis attaches to the ulna, making it a dependable flexor independent of forearm rotation.

Bicipital myotatic reflex

With the arm relaxed, forearm passively pronated, and elbow partially extended, place the thumb over the distal biceps tendon in the cubital fossa and strike the thumb. Contraction assesses the musculocutaneous nerve and C5–C6 spinal cord levels.

Long-head biceps pathology

Tendinopathy: anterior shoulder pain and focal point tenderness at the bicipital groove. The lecture's ultrasound demonstrates inhomogeneous tendon/thickened sheath and Doppler hypervascularity with tenosynovitis. Proximal rupture: chronic wear in the bicipital groove can tear the tendon between the supraglenoid tubercle and proximal groove, allowing distal retraction of the muscle belly—the Popeye deformity.

5 · Arteries, veins and collateral circulation

Brachial artery: continuation of axillary artery beginning at the inferior border of teres major. Branches include profunda brachii (radial collateral and middle collateral), superior ulnar collateral (with ulnar nerve, behind medial epicondyle) and inferior ulnar collateral (in front of medial epicondyle). It ends in radial and ulnar arteries.

Elbow anastomosis: collateral arteries above the joint connect to recurrent arteries below the joint. This provides alternate flow routes as vessel geometry changes with elbow position or when a vessel is compressed. A brachial pulse can be felt medial to the biceps tendon at the cubital fossa.

Superficial venous network: cephalic (lateral), basilic (medial), and connecting median cubital vein. Deep venous return: radial/ulnar veins form brachial veins accompanying the brachial artery; the brachial and basilic veins unite to form the axillary vein.

Clinical connection: The median cubital vein is used for venipuncture; the bicipital aponeurosis separates it from deeper vulnerable artery and nerve. Keep this superficial/deep relationship clear.

TAN: lateral → medial
Tendon of biceps brachii → brachial Artery (with veins) → median Nerve. The radial nerve lies farther laterally, with superficial and deep branches.

The fossa is a clinically useful transition zone between the arm and forearm. The biceps tendon provides a palpable landmark for locating the brachial artery and median nerve.

Retrieval checkpoints

1. What are the lateral-to-medial structures in the cubital fossa?

Tendon of biceps → brachial artery → median nerve (TAN); radial nerve lies lateral.

2. Which anterior arm muscle flexes regardless of pronation?

Brachialis: it inserts on the ulna rather than the rotating radius.

3. Why does musculocutaneous injury affect lateral forearm sensation?

It continues distally as the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve.

4. Which artery accompanies the ulnar nerve posterior to the medial epicondyle?

Superior ulnar collateral artery.

5. What spinal levels does the biceps deep-tendon reflex assess?

C5–C6 via the musculocutaneous nerve.

6. What distinguishes long-head biceps tendinopathy from proximal rupture?

Tendinopathy causes anterior shoulder tenderness in the groove; rupture leads to a distally retracted biceps belly (Popeye).

7. Name the cubital fossa's floor and roof.

Floor: brachialis and supinator. Roof: fascia and bicipital aponeurosis.

Master these before moving on

  1. Origin/insertion predicts movement: biceps rotates radius; brachialis flexes ulna.
  2. Nerve trajectory predicts deficit: musculocutaneous pierces coracobrachialis; ulnar crosses behind medial epicondyle.
  3. Layering predicts procedural risks: median cubital vein superficial to bicipital aponeurosis; brachial artery and median nerve deep.
  4. Elbow arteries form alternative paths: collateral and recurrent branches support perfusion with changing joint position.
