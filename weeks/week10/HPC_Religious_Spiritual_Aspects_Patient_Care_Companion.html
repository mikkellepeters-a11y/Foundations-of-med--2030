Learning objectives

1. Define spirituality, faith, and religion (SFR).
2. Explain why spiritual and religious needs are part of patient- and family-centered care.
3. Perform a basic screening spiritual history.
4. Explain the role of chaplains as consultants in interdisciplinary patient care.

1. Spirituality, faith, and religion

Spirituality

The lecture frames spirituality as how a person — body, mind, and soul — relates to others and the universe. It is tied to questions of identity, purpose, origin, destiny, meaning, and suffering.

Faith

The lecture presents faith as a relationship of trust, commitment to a tradition, remembrance, belief, and knowing something as true without proof.

Religion

An organized attempt to address major questions of existence, provide authoritative teaching, pass knowledge/experience to later generations, and provide rituals around life events.

Quick distinction: spirituality is broader meaning/relationship; faith centers trust/belief/commitment; religion is the organized structure that preserves teaching, community, and ritual.

2. Spiritual care as patient- and family-centered care

PFCC core
Patient- and family-centered care recognizes the patient as a person with a unique life history and needs. The lecture connects this to compassionate, humanistic care rather than treating spiritual concerns as separate from the clinical encounter.

Why ask?

The lecture states that many patients want clinicians to ask whether spiritual or religious beliefs might influence medical decisions, especially during serious illness.

What clinicians do

Assess, understand, support, and when necessary help moderate the influence of beliefs, practices, and values on care.

Basic human needs

Kindness, respect, listening, authenticity, physical presence, timely response to requests, and relationships with family all appear in the lecture's care framework.

Reassessment matters

Spiritual needs may change during hospitalization. The lecture explicitly recommends reassessing them over time rather than treating the admission history as one-and-done.

“We care about your emotional and spiritual well-being. Do you have any needs or requests that I can help with?”

3. A basic screening spiritual history

Keep it simple and straightforward. The lecture recommends placing the spiritual history in or after the social history and before the ROS, then letting the patient’s response guide follow-up.

Opening options from the lecture

“In times of crisis, where do you find strength?”

“Does faith play a role in your home life?”

Communication behavior

Watch body language. If the patient becomes defensive, explain matter-of-factly that you are being thorough. Back off if needed.

Do not force it
If the patient is uncomfortable or does not want to discuss spirituality, the lecture tells the clinician to respect that boundary. A spiritual history should not become a projection of the clinician’s own belief system.
Escalation
If the screen uncovers complex spiritual issues, ask permission to involve the patient's clergy and/or a hospital chaplain.

4. FICA spiritual-history framework

F — Faith & Belief

Do you consider yourself spiritual or religious?

If not, what gives your life meaning?

Tell me about your faith or belief.

I — Importance & Influence

What importance does faith have in your life?

Has it influenced how you care for yourself or your illness?

What role does it play in regaining health?

C — Community

Are you part of a spiritual or religious community?

Does that community support you? How?

Who are the people or groups most important to you?

A — Address in Care

How would you like us to address these issues in your health care?

FICA is not a test of religiosity. It is a structured way to identify sources of meaning/support and whether beliefs affect care decisions.

Prayer
The lecture explicitly asks students to think in advance about how they would respond if a patient or family asks them to pray. It also lists offering to pray with a patient/family as one possible spiritual-care action, while simultaneously emphasizing autonomy and non-imposition.

6. Chaplains as interdisciplinary consultants

Lecture framing: chaplains are healthcare professionals trained to provide complex spiritual care and should be incorporated as consultants rather than treated as an afterthought.

When to consult

Complex spiritual distress, conflict between beliefs and care, need for pastoral counseling, family spiritual needs, or whenever the clinician is not comfortable or adequately trained.

What collaboration looks like

Effective emotional and spiritual care requires proactive collaboration among clinicians, chaplains, patients, and families.
