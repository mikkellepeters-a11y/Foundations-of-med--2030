Week 10 CBL Case - Quick Guide

Foundations of Medicine • CBL • Pediatric ENT Infections, Neck Masses, IEI Screening, and XLA
Master chain: recognize AOM/sinusitis correctly → choose antibiotics based on resistance risk → distinguish reactive/infectious neck masses from concerning lesions → recognize warning signs of inborn errors of immunity → screen broadly → identify the B-cell pattern of X-linked agammaglobulinemia → confirm with flow cytometry/genetics → treat with immunoglobulin replacement plus infection control.

Acute Otitis Media

Diagnostic patterns from the supplied source: moderate-to-severe TM bulging OR new otorrhea not due to otitis externa. Mild bulging also supports AOM when paired with <48 hours of ear pain or intense TM erythema.
Young children: ear tugging/rubbing/holding, crying, fever, sleep change, and behavior change are common but relatively nonspecific.

Acute Bacterial Sinusitis

PatternSource criteria
Persistent illnessNasal discharge and/or daytime cough for >10 days without improvement.
Worsening courseNew or worsening nasal discharge, daytime cough, or fever after initial improvement.
Severe onsetFever ≥39°C / 102.2°F plus purulent nasal discharge for ≥3 consecutive days.
Do not overcall viral URI: the source notes that uncomplicated viral symptoms may normally last up to 10 days.

Antibiotic Choice

Amoxicillin
First-line for uncomplicated AOM or acute bacterial sinusitis when antimicrobial resistance is not suspected.
Amoxicillin-clavulanate
Use when resistance risk is higher: recent antibiotic use, daycare attendance, age <2 years, or moderate-severe illness.
Ceftriaxone: IV/IM alternative when vomiting, unable to take oral therapy, or unlikely to adhere to oral therapy.

Bacterial Cervical Lymphadenitis

Source pattern: acute fever + tender erythematous nodes that can become fluctuant over days. About 80% are attributed to Staphylococcus or Streptococcus species.
Antibiotics: target Staphylococcus + Streptococcus. Clindamycin or an early-generation cephalosporin such as cephalexin are listed choices.
Fluctuant node: ultrasonography can help; some cases ultimately need incision and drainage in addition to antibiotics.
MRSA: specifically called out as an emerging cause that should influence antibiotic selection.

Warning Signs of Inborn Errors of Immunity

Think IEI when infections are excessive, recurrent, severe, or unusual. Additional clues from the source: prolonged diarrhea, recurrent invasive sinopulmonary infection, recurrent skin/soft-tissue infection, bacteremia/meningitis, failure to thrive, early autoimmune disease, and a concerning family history.

Initial IEI Screening

Broad screen from source: CBC with differential • serum immunoglobulins • C3, C4, CH50, AH50 • HIV testing • review TREC newborn screen.
Sometimes added: functional antibody response to primary immunizations, delayed hypersensitivity skin testing for T-cell function, and chest radiography.
Thymic shadow: newborns normally have a prominent thymic shadow; absence in a young infant is concerning for a T-cell disorder.

X-Linked Agammaglobulinemia (XLA-1)

Core Pattern

Classic source presentation: recurrent sinopulmonary infections + meningitis due to S. pneumoniae → very low immunoglobulins → absent circulating B lymphocytes on flow cytometry → BTK genetic confirmation.
Lymph nodes: patients do have lymph nodes, but they are very small because they do not form germinal centers.
Flow cytometry: absent/markedly reduced circulating B cells are central to the source’s diagnostic pattern.

Typical Infections

Encapsulated bacteria: recurrent S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae sinopulmonary infections are classic.
Additional organisms listed: Mycoplasma, Pseudomonas, Salmonella, Campylobacter jejuni, rotavirus, and Giardia intestinalis.

Inheritance

Carrier motherRisk in source
Male child50% risk of disease
Female child50% risk of being a carrier

Treatment

Cornerstone: IgG replacement from healthy donors by IVIG or subcutaneous administration.
Source intervals: IV every 3–4 weeks or subcutaneous every 1–2 weeks.
Also important: aggressive antibiotics for suspected/documented infections; prolonged courses may be needed for chronic pulmonary infection or sinusitis. Immunoglobulin replacement does not prevent every infection.
Precautions: handwashing, respiratory-droplet avoidance, and avoidance of untreated drinking water when possible.

Testing a Newborn Male in a Known BTK Family

Best simple test in the supplied case: CD19+ or CD20+ B-cell counting by flow cytometry.
Why not IgG? newborns may have normal or elevated IgG from placental transfer. Why not full BTK sequencing? the family variant is already known.
Last 30 seconds: AOM = bulging TM/otorrhea criteria • sinusitis = >10 days, double-worsening, or ≥39°C + purulence ≥3 days • amoxicillin first-line, amox-clav for resistance risk • pediatric neck mass red flags = >2 cm, firm/matted/painless/fixed/rapidly growing • IEI screen = CBC diff + Ig + complement + HIV + TREC • absent thymic shadow → T-cell concern • XLA = BTK, low Ig, absent B cells, tiny nodes/no germinal centers • IVIG/SCIG is cornerstone • newborn at known familial risk → CD19/CD20 flow cytometry.
