Learning objective 1

Membrane potential: electrochemical gradients and ion equilibrium

Membrane potential (Vm) is the voltage inside a cell compared with outside. Resting cells maintain unequal ion distributions: potassium and impermeant protein anions are relatively high inside; sodium and chloride are relatively high outside. The lipid bilayer limits free ion passage, so channels determine which gradients influence the voltage.

Potassium tends to diffuse outward down its concentration gradient; the accumulating negative charge inside pulls K⁺ back inward. At its equilibrium (Nernst) potential EK, the electrical and chemical forces balance, so there is no net K⁺ flux through available K⁺ channels. The lecture example uses 150 mM intracellular K⁺ and 5 mM outside to give EK approximately −90.8 mV.

Nernst: Eion = RT/(zF) × ln([ion]out/[ion]in). At 37°C, the equivalent base-10 expression for a monovalent cation is ≈ 61.5 mV × log10(out/in). For Cl⁻, the negative valence reverses the sign of the logarithmic term.

MUST KNOWChanging a concentration ratio changes that ion’s equilibrium potential. But the influence on actual Vm depends on membrane permeability.
EXAM TRAP · NOTATIONSlide 13 prints “61.5 × ln” whereas slide 15 uses 61.5 × log10. The general RT/zF expression uses natural log; at 37°C 61.5 applies to base-10 log for |z|=1. These are not interchangeable.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINTIf a membrane becomes exclusively permeable to K⁺, what voltage does Vm approach?
Reveal answerEK, the K⁺ Nernst potential.
Learning objective 2

Resting potential: relative permeability and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump

Ion channels provide the permeability that allows ion diffusion. The lecture describes leak channels alongside voltage-gated, ligand-gated, mechanically gated, and intracellular signal-gated channels. Each type responds to a different stimulus and changes ionic current.

At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺. The lecturer illustrates an approximate relative permeability of PK:PNa:PCl ≈ 50:1:10; consequently resting Vm is closer to EK than ENa. Increasing permeability to one ion drives Vm toward that ion’s equilibrium potential. The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation weights these concentration gradients by the relative permeabilities.

Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase: consumes ATP to pump 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in. It maintains the concentration gradients over time and is electrogenic (one net positive charge moves outward per cycle). The lecturer assigns an approximately −4 mV direct contribution to the resting potential; its more fundamental role is gradient maintenance.

COMMON CONFUSIONNernst: equilibrium voltage of ONE ion. GHK: membrane voltage influenced by MULTIPLE permeant ions. Pump: maintains gradients; it is not the rapid channel responsible for the upstroke of a neuronal action potential.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINTIf resting PNa rises sharply, which way does Vm move?
Reveal answerIt depolarizes toward ENa, a positive equilibrium potential.
Learning objective 3

Potassium changes: predict membrane voltage AND firing

Because resting membrane permeability is dominated by K⁺, changing extracellular K⁺ strongly changes resting voltage. Hypokalemia increases the outward K⁺ concentration gradient → EK becomes more negative → resting membrane becomes hyperpolarized → reaching threshold is harder, often reducing excitability. Hyperkalemia diminishes the outward K⁺ gradient → EK is less negative → membrane depolarizes.

Do not conclude that hyperkalemia always increases firing: mild depolarization can bring cells closer to threshold, but severe or sustained depolarization inactivates voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, producing impaired excitability / depolarization block. The lecturer emphasizes potential weakness, paresthesia, spasms, arrhythmias, and seizures in marked ionic imbalance.

Causes explicitly mentioned include potassium-wasting diuretics, GI loss, and excess aldosterone in hypokalemia; kidney failure, decreased aldosterone in Addison disease, and excess potassium administration in hyperkalemia. The lecture names diuretics, potassium-binding ion-exchange resins and hemodialysis when discussing hyperkalemia, but does not give modern management algorithms.

HIGH YIELD↓[K⁺]out → EK more negative → hyperpolarization. ↑[K⁺]out → EK less negative → depolarization. Severe hyperkalemia can paradoxically reduce firing via sodium-channel inactivation.
FIRST AID CONNECTIONECG changes and cardiac conduction toxicity make major potassium disturbances clinically urgent. This is supplementary context; specific ECG sequences are not provided in the lecture.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINTWhy can severe hyperkalemia cause paralysis despite making Vm less negative?
Reveal answerPersistent depolarization inactivates voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, preventing normal action-potential initiation.
Learning objective 4

From graded potentials to the neuronal and skeletal muscle action potential

Graded potentials are variable-amplitude, local changes (receptor potentials, synaptic EPSPs or IPSPs, and pacemaker potentials). They decay with distance and may summate spatially (different inputs at the same time) or temporally (repeated input over a short time). Summed depolarization at the trigger zone can bring voltage to threshold.

Action potentials are all-or-none, regenerative signals that propagate without decrement. In typical neuron and skeletal-muscle action potentials, threshold opens fast voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → Na⁺ influx and rapid depolarization → a positive overshoot may occur. Na⁺ channels inactivate while voltage-gated K⁺ channels activate more slowly → K⁺ efflux and repolarization. Persistent elevated K⁺ conductance transiently drives the membrane more negative than rest → after-hyperpolarization, followed by recovery.

Learning objective integration

Refractoriness, conduction, and action-to-cellular response

During the absolute refractory period, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are unavailable because they are open or inactivated; no stimulus can trigger a second normal action potential. During the relative refractory period, enough channels have recovered that a stronger-than-normal stimulus can generate another action potential, while the membrane may remain hyperpolarized.

Myelin reduces current leakage across internodal membrane. Voltage-gated channel clusters at nodes of Ranvier regenerate the action potential, producing saltatory conduction. Loss of myelin impairs efficient propagation; the professor’s slide 48 lists demyelinating conditions as contextual examples.

Excitation–response coupling: in neurons, a propagated action potential reaches the nerve terminal and causes Ca²⁺-dependent neurotransmitter release. At skeletal muscle, excitation is coupled to release of intracellular Ca²⁺ that permits contraction. The lecture mainly teaches the underlying ion-channel principle; the detailed excitation–contraction sequence is included only as a labeled enrichment, not presented as slide-by-slide professor content.

BOARD / CLINICAL ENRICHMENTTerminal depolarization opens presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, enabling vesicular neurotransmitter release. In skeletal muscle, T-tubule excitation triggers SR Ca²⁺ release and actin–myosin interaction; detailed molecular coupling is beyond these slides.
FIRST AID CONNECTIONDemyelination can slow or block conduction; associate myelin with fast nodal regeneration and intact neural signaling, rather than assuming the entire internode generates repeated action potentials.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINTWhy is another action potential impossible in the absolute refractory period?
Reveal answerInsufficient voltage-gated Na⁺ channels have recovered from inactivation.
Learning objective integration

Ion-channel abnormalities, toxins, and disease

The professor explicitly introduces channelopathies: diseases in which an altered channel disrupts excitability. Examples throughout the slides include genetic channel disease, autoimmune channel/receptor disorders, electrolyte-associated excitability dysfunction, and toxins that interfere with ion-channel conduction.

Slides 53–56 feature pufferfish and other toxin-bearing animals. The board-relevant mechanism for tetrodotoxin (pufferfish) is blockade of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, preventing neuronal and skeletal-muscle action potentials; the lecture’s toxin imagery is explicit, while the named molecular mechanism is a selective First Aid/board connection. Slide 58 labels apamin as blocking Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels. Slide 59 associates capsaicin with excitation of peripheral nerve endings, and slide 60 shows TRP channels in thermal and chemical sensing.

FIRST AID CONNECTIONTetrodotoxin → voltage-gated Na⁺ channel block → failure of action potentials → flaccid paralysis / respiratory failure.
COMMON CONFUSIONCapsaicin activates TRPV1 nociceptive signaling; tetrodotoxin blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ conduction; apamin inhibits certain Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels. These affect distinct channel mechanisms.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINTWhich toxin discussed through pufferfish images suppresses action potentials by targeting voltage-gated Na⁺ channels?
Reveal answerTetrodotoxin (board connection to the pufferfish examples).
MASTER THESE BEFORE MOVING ON(1) Concentration gradients plus permeability establish voltage. (2) Nernst is a single-ion equilibrium; GHK weights several ions. (3) Extracellular K⁺ shifts resting voltage, but severe depolarization can block firing. (4) Fast Na⁺ entry and delayed K⁺ exit explain the waveform. (5) Na⁺ channel recovery explains refractoriness; myelination explains fast propagation.
THE MOST USEFUL FIRST AID CONNECTIONSPotassium disturbance → excitability and cardiac risk; tetrodotoxin → Na⁺-channel blockade and paralysis; demyelination → impaired impulse propagation; channelopathy → dysfunction of normal excitable-tissue channels.
