Hantavirus Infection

Reservoir + exposure

Bunyavirus reservoir in mice. The deck emphasizes inhalation of virus in rodent saliva, urine, and feces; HCPS is associated with the Four Corners region of the U.S.

Two syndromes

HFRS: febrile illness → petechiae/thrombocytopenia → oliguria, hematuria, proteinuria, ↑BUN/Cr. HCPS/HPS: febrile phase → sudden cough/dyspnea/tachycardia/hypotension → noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, respiratory failure, cardiogenic shock.

Endothelial infection in lungs, kidneys, heart, liver, spleen → vascular leakage + thrombocytopenia/coagulation abnormalities → organ-specific HCPS/HFRS phenotype.
Deck p. 5: Hantavirus pathogenesis is presented as endothelial infection with vascular leakage; the slide specifically labels lungs, kidneys, heart, liver, and spleen.
Faculty current-event callout: the deck discusses Andes hantavirus as the only hantavirus shown with human-to-human transmission, requiring prolonged close contact, and includes a 2026 cruise-ship-associated cluster plus the 2018/19 Epuyén outbreak.

Treatment

The deck's treatment slide is intentionally simple: early recognition + supportive ICU care.

Psittacosis · Chlamydophila psittaci

Microbiology

Gram-negative intracellular pathogen. Elementary body = infectious form; reticulate body = replicative form.

Exposure

Bird reservoirs include parrots/parakeets, doves, eagles, and feral pigeons. Occupational groups shown: poultry workers, veterinarians, bird owners, pet-store workers.

Clinical pattern: abrupt fever/chills + headache + dry cough/sore throat ± diarrhea + facial Horder spots + lobar pneumonia. Sputum Gram stain: many neutrophils, no organisms.
Deck p. 13: The clinical slide combines the atypical-pneumonia syndrome with Horder spots, lobar pneumonia, and the classic many-polys/no-organisms sputum Gram-stain clue.

Treatment

Doxycycline 10–14 days; azithromycin is shown for children. The deck contrasts untreated mortality (20%) with treated mortality (1%).

Brucellosis

Species → animal

B. abortus cattle · B. melitensis sheep/goats/camels · B. suis pigs · B. canis dogs.

Acquisition

Contact with infected animals, unpasteurized dairy, inhalation, or entry through skin abrasions.

Brucella enters host cells → Brucella-containing vacuole → interaction/fusion with endosomal and ER compartments → intracellular replication; surviving organisms enter bloodstream.
Deck p. 19: Brucellosis is framed around universal fever, the relapsing “undulant” pattern, drenching night sweats with wet-hay/mold odor, and hepatosplenomegaly.

Complications

Non-erosive peripheral arthritis, sacroiliitis, spondylitis with Pons sign; neurobrucellosis can produce cranial-nerve palsies, confusion/personality change/depression, meningoencephalitis, brain abscess, and granuloma.

Diagnosis

Blood and bone-marrow cultures; Rose-Bengal test using serum + rose-stained Brucella antigen (deck sensitivity 96%).

Treatment organization shown in the deck: doxycycline-based combination therapy; gentamicin is shown for the first week in non-focal disease, prolonged therapy is emphasized for spondylitis/sacroiliitis, and neurobrucellosis is shown with doxycycline + rifampin + ceftriaxone until CSF normalizes.

RMSF · Ehrlichiosis · Anaplasmosis

Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever · Rickettsia rickettsii

Vector / season

Dermacentor variabilis (American dog tick) is the most common vector shown for the Eastern U.S.; other vectors are mapped by region. Think spring/summer.

Pathogenesis

Obligate intracellular Gram-negative coccobacillus infecting endothelial cells in capillaries, arterioles, and venules → systemic vascular injury across skin, brain, heart, lungs, kidneys, GI tract, and muscle.

Rash timing/distribution: about day 4; blanching macules → papules → petechiae → purpura; begins at wrists & ankles and spreads toward the trunk.
Deck p. 29: The faculty rash slide emphasizes day ~4 and the wrist/ankle-to-trunk progression from blanching macules to petechiae/purpura.

Labs / Dx

WBC normal to slightly ↑ with left shift, thrombocytopenia, mild AST/ALT elevation; CSF may show <100 cells/µL mononuclear pleocytosis. IFA sensitivity >90% but is often negative early. DFA on rash biopsy: sensitivity ~70%, specificity 100%.

Faculty rule

Diagnosis is primarily clinical. Severe febrile illness in spring/summer ± rash → TREAT. Delay increases fatality.

Human monocytic ehrlichiosis vs human granulocytic anaplasmosis

Deck p. 35: The contrast slide places Ehrlichia chaffeensis with monocytes/macrophages and Anaplasma phagocytophilum with granulocytic infection; rash and GI symptoms are less common with anaplasmosis.

Q Fever · Coxiella burnetii

Ozzy found the sheep barn. He would like to remind you: Q fever is the aerosol/dust exposure with “funny” pneumonia and relative bradycardia.

Microbiology

Small coccobacillus with Gram-negative-like cell wall. Large-cell variant = replicating; small-cell variant = non-replicating infectious form.

Exposure

Infected aerosols in dust or infected materials are emphasized. Raw/unpasteurized or contaminated milk is possible; tick transmission is labeled rare.

Acute Q fever: very high fever (up to 40°C, >15 d), retro-orbital headache, myalgias. Pneumonia can show rounded densities/halo sign or segmental opacities with relative bradycardia — the deck's “funny pneumonia.”
Deck p. 41: The Q-fever pneumonia slide highlights rounded/segmental opacities, respiratory symptoms, and relative bradycardia as the “funny” pneumonia pattern.

Labs

WBC normal or low in pneumonia; CRP higher than other pneumonias; ALT/AST ↑ in 30–60% with granulomatous hepatitis.

Persistent/chronic cardiac disease

Endocarditis is the most common cause of persistent C. burnetii infection, especially the aortic valve. The deck shows IgG1 >1:1600 and PET/CT as useful in detecting endocarditis.

Treatment: no valvular heart disease → doxycycline 100 mg BID ×14 days. Valvular heart disease → doxycycline + hydroxychloroquine ×12 months; the deck notes hydroxychloroquine raises vacuolar pH to preserve doxycycline activity.

Leptospirosis · Leptospira spp.

Microbiology

Highly motile aerobic spirochete with a double membrane like Gram-negative bacteria; adhesins bind host proteins such as fibrinogen/fibronectin.

Exposure / path

Rats are emphasized; urine contaminates food, soil, and water. Organisms enter through a defect in skin, then disseminate to organs including lung, liver, and kidney.

Weil disease pattern: often biphasic (leptospiremic → immune/IgM phase) + fever/chills/headache/myalgia/GI symptoms + jaundice or conjunctival suffusion + AKI with unexpectedly low K+ + pulmonary hemorrhage.
Deck p. 50: The clinical slide visually groups jaundice/conjunctival suffusion, AKI with low potassium, and pulmonary hemorrhage under the biphasic Weil-disease pattern.

Diagnosis

Darkfield microscopy (blood/serum/urine/CSF); culture is low sensitivity and slow (6–8 weeks); microscopic agglutination test = gold standard; PCR is also shown.

Treatment

Severe illness: IV penicillin G or ceftriaxone ×7 days. Mild illness: doxycycline ×7 days.

Slides 53–56 · Faculty Practice Questions

Q1. Fever, hypotension, petechiae, oliguria/hematuria and renal failure from hantavirus: where was it most likely acquired?
Answer: Oslo, Norway. The stem is the HFRS phenotype rather than HCPS.
Q2. Veterinarian with dry cough, Horder spots, lobar pneumonia, and many neutrophils but no organisms on sputum Gram stain: animal source?
Answer: parrots. This is psittacosis.
Q3. Meat-packing worker with weeks of low-grade fever, depression/confusion, malodorous perspiration, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and inflammatory CSF?
Answer: brucellosis.
Q4. Biphasic febrile illness with jaundice, red conjunctivae, pulmonary findings, AKI, and unexpectedly low K+ in a Vietnamese patient: acquisition mechanism?
Answer: contamination of an abrasion by the organism. This is leptospirosis.
