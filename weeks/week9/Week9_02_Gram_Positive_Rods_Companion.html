Official Week 9 Objectives

1. Outline the mechanism by which the toxins of various organisms cause human disease.
2. Identify classic symptoms and signs of diseases caused by these organisms.
3. Predict the natural habitat of major pathogenic Gram-positive bacilli.
4. Describe major laboratory characteristics of important Gram-positive bacilli.

How to think through this lecture
For every rod, know four things in the same order: habitat → lab identity → toxin/mechanism → clinical signature. The fastest exam recognition comes from pairing one microbiology clue with one mechanism and one syndrome.
Slides 2–3The organism frameworkLO 3–4

Four genera emphasized by the current deck

  • Bacillus: aerobic environmental rods; the deck emphasizes B. anthracis and B. cereus.
  • Clostridium/Clostridioides: anaerobic spore-forming rods; habitats include soil, sewage, marine sediment, decaying matter, and the GI tract.
  • Corynebacterium diphtheriae: pleomorphic aerobic, non-motile, non-spore-forming rod.
  • Listeria monocytogenes: small aerobic, β-hemolytic rod with distinctive motility and intracellular spread.

Slides 4–17Bacillus anthracis: morphology → toxin → syndromeLO 1–4

Laboratory features

  • Large Gram-positive bacillus with central spores and “bamboo” appearance.
  • Non-motile and non-hemolytic.
  • Raised colonies can form the classic Medusa-head appearance.
  • The capsule is explicitly described as protein, not carbohydrate.
Legacy-note enrichment
The older note names the capsule as poly-D-glutamic acid. It also labels the three anthrax toxin components as protective antigen (PA), edema factor/toxin, and lethal factor/toxin.
Toxin componentDeck mechanismClinical consequence
Protective antigen (PA)Binds cell surface and enables toxin entryDelivery component for edema and lethal toxins
Edema toxin (ET)ATP → cAMP; fluid influxMarked edema
Lethal toxin (LT)Targets MAP kinase signaling / MAPKKCell leakage and lysis
Legacy-note molecular detail
Edema factor is described as an adenylate cyclase that raises cAMP; lethal factor is described as a protease that disrupts the MAP kinase pathway.

Cutaneous

Infected animal tissue → spores enter cut/abrasion → “malignant” pustule / black eschar with painful adenopathy; dissemination is shown as rare.

Gastrointestinal

Acute abdomen, bloody diarrhea, sepsis; multiple intestinal ulcers and mesenteric adenopathy.

Injectional

Cellulitis, abscess, and necrotizing fasciitis.

Inhalational

Flu-like prodrome followed by severe dyspnea, hemorrhage, pulmonary edema, then respiratory failure/meningitis/shock. Deck states untreated mortality of 80%.

Anthrax recognition line
Non-motile + non-hemolytic + protein capsule + Medusa head + PA/ET/LT. Black eschar = cutaneous; widened mediastinum = inhalational.

Slides 22–30Clostridia & Clostridium perfringensLO 1–4

Clostridial frame

  • Obligate anaerobes; oxygen kills the vegetative organisms, while spores persist.
  • Habitats shown: soil, sewage, marine sediment, decaying plant/animal matter, and GI tract.
  • Fermentation produces gases (CO₂, H₂) and malodorous metabolic products.

Legacy-note enrichment
The older note explicitly describes alpha toxin as a lecithinase/phospholipase that degrades cell-membrane phospholipids, explaining the rapid tissue necrosis of gas gangrene.
Slides 31–42Tetanus vs botulism: same broad family, opposite paralysisLO 1–2, 4

C. tetani

Large motile rod with terminal spores → drumstick/tennis-racket appearance. Spores enter a low-O₂ wound and germinate. Tetanospasmin removes inhibitory control → spastic paralysis.

C. botulinum

Large straight motile rod with subterminal spores. Toxin blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction → flaccid paralysis.

Legacy-note molecular detail
The older note adds that both neurotoxins act through SNARE-protein cleavage: tetanospasmin blocks inhibitory neurotransmitter release in the CNS; botulinum toxin blocks ACh release at the peripheral neuromuscular junction.
Infant botulism
The current deck calls it a cause of floppy baby syndrome (poor feeding, weak cry, weak suck), shows honey/corn syrup as an exposure in 20% of cases, and lists stool culture / toxin assay plus botulism immune globulin.
Slides 43–45Clostridioides difficileLO 1–4

Deck recognition pattern

  • Long straight rods with oval subterminal spores that can survive for months.
  • Normal colon flora is shown in up to 30%; spread is emphasized on healthcare workers’ hands.
  • Clinical spectrum: mild-to-severe diarrhea, fever/leukocytosis, and toxic megacolon.
Legacy-note enrichment
The older note labels toxin A as an enterotoxin driving watery diarrhea and toxin B as a cytotoxin associated with epithelial injury/pseudomembrane formation. Those A/B labels are not explicitly written on the current deck slides.

Mechanism
Phage-mediated toxin → inactivates elongation factor 2 → stops protein synthesis → local pseudomembrane plus systemic myocarditis/neuropathy.
Legacy-note molecular detail
The older note specifies that the A-B exotoxin ADP-ribosylates EF-2.
Treatment / prevention shown
The current deck shows penicillin or erythromycin plus diphtheria antitoxin, with prevention by vaccination.
Slides 54–59Listeria monocytogenesLO 2–4

Identity & ecology

  • Small aerobic “diphtheroid” rod; β-hemolytic.
  • Ubiquitous in soil, water, plants, and animals; slide imagery emphasizes food exposures including cheese and meats.
  • Motility is shown with an umbrella-shaped agar pattern; a later deck question identifies tumbling motility as characteristic of Listeria.
Legacy-note enrichment
The older note calls Listeria a facultative intracellular organism, highlights growth in cold temperatures, and identifies newborns, pregnant patients, elderly patients, and immunocompromised patients as classic high-risk groups.
Listeria recognition line
Small β-hemolytic rod + tumbling/umbrella motility + intracellular actin rockets + pregnancy/neonate/meningitis.

Six checks before you leave

  1. Can you identify each organism from one lab clue: Medusa head, β-hemolytic motile rod, double-zone hemolysis, terminal spore, subterminal spore, black tellurite colony, or umbrella motility?
  2. Can you map PA, edema toxin, and lethal toxin to their separate roles in anthrax?
  3. Can you explain why tetanus is spastic while botulism is flaccid?
  4. Can you distinguish B. cereus emetic disease from C. perfringens diarrheal food poisoning by timing and toxin behavior?
  5. Can you explain diphtheria’s local pseudomembrane and systemic myocarditis using the toxin mechanism?
  6. Can you explain how Listeria escapes the phagolysosome and spreads cell-to-cell?
