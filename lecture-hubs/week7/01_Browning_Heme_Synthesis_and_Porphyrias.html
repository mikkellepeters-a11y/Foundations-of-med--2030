Slides 3-7Heme structure, function, Hb/Mb, and CYP450LO 1

What the slides are establishing

  • Heme is a porphyrin: a cyclic tetrapyrrole made from four pyrrole rings linked by methine bridges, with a central metal-binding core.
  • Heme functions as a prosthetic group that lets proteins bind oxygen and/or transfer electrons.
  • The faculty list includes cytochrome c (ETC), guanylate cyclase (cGMP), catalase (H2O2 degradation), tryptophan pyrrolase, CYP450, hemoglobin, and myoglobin.
  • Hemoglobin transports oxygen in blood; myoglobin stores/buffers oxygen in muscle.
  • Hepatic CYP450 proteins perform phase I metabolism and are induced by xenobiotic-sensing nuclear receptors such as PXR/CAR.
What this means
Heme is useful because the iron can participate in reversible oxygen binding and redox chemistry. That is why one small cofactor appears in proteins doing very different jobs: oxygen delivery, electron transport, peroxide handling, and drug metabolism.
Do not miss from the faculty handout
  • Heme side chains are not all equivalent: methyl/vinyl groups are relatively hydrophobic, while propionate groups are hydrophilic, helping orient heme inside an apoprotein pocket.
  • Hemoglobin/myoglobin bind oxygen to Fe2+. Oxidation to Fe3+ produces methemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen until reduced back to Fe2+.
  • Faculty handout: CYP450 enzymes are abundant in liver ER/mitochondria and inducible by lipophilic xenobiotics.
Board/clinical enrichment
Think “heme = iron chemistry.” Fe2+ is the oxygen-binding state in Hb/Mb; Fe3+ is methemoglobin. This is related to heme biology but not a major porphyria focus of this lecture.

Faculty handout detail that is easy to miss
  • Porphyrinogens are colorless. Oxidation can generate reddish porphyrins.
  • Hydroxymethylbilane can spontaneously cyclize into a nonproductive uroporphyrinogen I isomer; the enzyme-directed product for heme synthesis is uroporphyrinogen III.
  • When iron is unavailable, ferrochelatase can insert zinc, forming zinc protoporphyrin.

Common exam trap
Do not reduce the pathway to only “ALAS -> ALAD -> ferrochelatase.” The official LO says describe the pathway, so know the major intermediates and the three disease-defining enzymes: PBG deaminase, UROD, ferrochelatase, plus both lead targets.

Slides 18-19Regulation: hepatic ALAS1 vs erythroid ALAS2LO 2

ALAS1 - liver

Main purpose: supply heme for CYP450 and other hepatic hemoproteins. Controlled strongly by the regulatory heme pool and metabolic/xenobiotic signals.

ALAS2 - erythroid

Main purpose: coordinate heme production with hemoglobin synthesis. Controlled by iron availability and EPO/JAK-STAT.

Slide 18. Hepatic ALAS1: heme feedback inhibits; fasting/glucagon-PGC-1alpha and xenobiotic receptor signaling increase expression; insulin opposes the fasting program.

Hepatic ALAS1 - causal chain

Fasting -> glucagon/PKA -> PGC-1alpha -> increased ALAS1. CYP450 inducers such as phenobarbital activate xenobiotic receptors (PXR/CAR) and increase ALAS1. Heme feeds back to suppress ALAS1.

Faculty handout: heme feedback is multi-level
Excess heme can directly inhibit ALAS1, reduce transcription, reduce mRNA stability, block mitochondrial import of the precursor, and promote proteolysis. You do not necessarily need all five mechanisms to answer the LO, but understand that feedback is strong and redundant.
Slide 19. Erythroid ALAS2: EPO drives JAK/STAT5-dependent transcription; iron controls translation through an IRE/iron-regulatory protein mechanism.
Need to memorize - ALAS1 vs ALAS2
ALAS1 = liver = heme feedback + fasting/drugs.
ALAS2 = erythroid = EPO + iron availability.
Why this matters clinically
This regulation explains both AIP triggers and AIP treatment. Anything that drives hepatic ALAS1 pushes more precursor into a blocked pathway; hemin and carbohydrate loading push ALAS1 back down.

Slides 20-22Porphyria logic + Acute Intermittent PorphyriaLO 3 + LO 4
Pattern recognition
AIP = neurovisceral, no photosensitivity. Think severe abdominal pain + autonomic/neurologic/psychiatric symptoms + precipitating ALAS1 trigger.
Slide 21. AIP: partial PBG deaminase deficiency -> ALA/PBG accumulation -> neuropsychiatric/neurovisceral symptoms. The slide also shows the treatment logic.
FeatureAIP
DefectPartial deficiency of porphobilinogen deaminase (PBGD; hydroxymethylbilane synthase/HMBS), classically autosomal dominant
AccumulationALA + PBG
Target syndromeAcute neurovisceral attacks
TriggersFasting; CYP450-inducing lipophilic drugs; steroid hormones; faculty examples include phenobarbital
CluesSevere abdominal pain, tachycardia, nausea/vomiting/constipation, confusion/paranoia/psychosis, neuropathy, proximal paresis, seizures
First-line diagnostic clueElevated urinary PBG during attack; urine can darken reddish-brown after light exposure
TreatmentRemove trigger; carbohydrate/glucose; IV hemin; liver transplant in severe recurrent refractory disease
LO 4 - why glucose works
Fasting/glucagon activates PGC-1alpha and ALAS1. Providing metabolizable carbohydrate suppresses the fasting program and reduces hepatic ALAS1 drive, so fewer toxic precursors enter the blocked pathway.
LO 4 - why hemin works
Hemin supplies a heme signal that restores negative feedback on hepatic ALAS1. Less ALAS1 activity -> less ALA/PBG production upstream of the PBGD block.
Professor-specific numeric detail
Slide 21 lists glucose 400 g/day for 1-2 days and hemin 1-4 mg/kg/day for 3-14 days. Know the treatment rationale first; memorize exact dosing only if your course has emphasized dosing details.
Faculty handout nuance
AIP generally lacks anemia because the disease-causing mutation typically affects the hepatic PBGD isoform rather than the erythroid isoform. The handout also notes ALA can interfere with GABA signaling, one proposed contributor to neurologic symptoms.

Can you explain the entire vignette chain: trigger -> ALAS1 increases -> PBGD block -> ALA/PBG accumulate -> abdominal/neuropsychiatric syndrome -> urine PBG -> glucose/hemin suppress ALAS1?

Slides 23-24Porphyria Cutanea Tarda (PCT)LO 3
Pattern recognition
PCT = delayed blistering photosensitivity in adults. Think fragile sun-exposed skin, vesicles/erosions/scarring, pigment change, hypertrichosis.
Slide 24. PCT clinical phenotype and UROD block. The faculty images highlight blistering/fragility rather than the immediate burning phenotype of EPP.
FeaturePCT
DefectReduced hepatic uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase (UROD) activity
EpidemiologyMost common porphyria. Faculty handout: mostly acquired/sporadic; minority inherited AD
AccumulationUroporphyrinogen -> oxidized uroporphyrins; elevated urinary polycarboxylate porphyrins
ClinicalDelayed blistering photosensitivity, skin fragility, ulcers/erosions, slow healing/scarring, hyper/hypopigmentation, hypertrichosis
Associations/triggersAlcohol, estrogen, lipophilic chemicals; strong faculty-handout associations with hepatitis C and iron overload
ManagementRemove triggers, limit light exposure, phlebotomy to reduce iron, low-dose chloroquine
Do not miss - unusual sunscreen detail from faculty handout
PCT photosensitization is driven by porphyrins absorbing visible light (faculty handout notes an absorption peak around 405 nm). Standard sunscreen is designed mainly for UV and may not fully protect; the handout specifically recommends opaque physical blockers such as zinc/titanium oxide and protective clothing.

LO 3 checkpoint - PCT

Can you distinguish PCT from EPP using age, timing after light exposure, blistering/scarring, accumulated metabolite, diagnostic specimen, and enzyme defect?

Slides 25-28Erythropoietic Protoporphyria (EPP)LO 3
Pattern recognition
EPP = immediate painful non-blistering photosensitivity, often beginning in childhood.
Slide 26. Most protoporphyrin IX is produced by bone marrow, circulates in RBC/plasma, is taken up by liver, and is eliminated through bile/feces.
FeatureEPP
DefectReduced ferrochelatase activity
AccumulationProtoporphyrin IX in bone marrow, RBCs, plasma, and potentially liver
ClinicalBurning/stinging, itching, redness, swelling soon after visible-light exposure; no blisters; typically resolves without scarring
AgeOften infancy/childhood; faculty handout calls it the most common porphyria in children
DiagnosisTotal blood/RBC protoporphyrin; faculty notes liver biopsy can show rust-brown deposits and birefringent Maltese-cross material in hepatic disease
Why urine is not the main specimenProtoporphyrin IX is hydrophobic/water-insoluble and is excreted mainly in bile/feces
ComplicationsProtoporphyrin gallstones, cholestasis, progressive liver disease in some patients
TreatmentLight avoidance; beta-carotene; afamelanotide (MC1-R agonist) to increase melanin/pain-free light exposure
PCT vs EPP - fastest distinction
PCT: delayed + blistering/scarring + adult. EPP: immediate burning + non-blistering + childhood.
Faculty handout detail
EPP protoporphyrin is hydrophobic. The handout uses this to explain both the tissue distribution and why EPP differs from water-soluble porphyrin disorders in urinary excretion and skin injury pattern.

LO 3 checkpoint - EPP

If a child has burning and swelling within minutes of sunlight but no blisters, can you identify ferrochelatase, protoporphyrin IX, blood testing, and possible hepatobiliary complications?

Slides 29-30Lead toxicity as a heme-synthesis disorderLO 3
Slide 29. Lead hits the pathway twice: ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase.
Need to memorize cold
Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase + ferrochelatase. Therefore: urinary ALA rises and erythrocyte/zinc protoporphyrin rises.

Faculty slide symptoms

Abdominal pain, headache, numbness/tingling, weakness/fatigue, memory loss and mood/irritability changes. The slide emphasizes increased urinary delta-ALA and increased erythrocyte protoporphyrins.

Faculty handout mechanism
  • Lead can displace the zinc required by ALA dehydratase.
  • At ferrochelatase, lead favors formation/accumulation of zinc protoporphyrin rather than normal heme.
  • Blood lead level is the definitive measurement; zinc protoporphyrin reflects chronic effect on heme synthesis.
  • Heme inhibition produces microcytic, hypochromic anemia.
Board enrichment from the companion study guide
Classic board stems may additionally show basophilic stippling, sideroblastic/ring-sideroblast changes, and motor neuropathy such as wrist/foot drop. These are useful recognition clues but are not all explicitly pictured on Browning's main slides.
Common trap
Lead inhibits ferrochelatase like EPP, but lead poisoning is not the same phenotype as EPP. Faculty handout explains that zinc protoporphyrin is less phototoxic and remains associated with erythrocytes, so lead does not produce the classic immediate EPP photosensitivity syndrome.

LO 3 checkpoint - lead

Can you identify both inhibited enzymes, predict which intermediates rise, and connect heme failure to a microcytic/hypochromic anemia plus neuro/GI symptoms?
