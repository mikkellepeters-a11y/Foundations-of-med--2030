Overview: Official Week 7 Learning Objectives

LO 1. Describe primary hemostasis in terms of key components and events, including vWF, platelets, and fibrinogen.
LO 2. Understand testing of primary hemostasis: platelet count, MPV, bleeding time/PFA, and platelet aggregation studies.
LO 3. Understand disorders of primary hemostasis and their work-up, including thrombocytopenia, Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard-Soulier syndrome, and von Willebrand disease.
LO 4. Describe secondary hemostasis, including the major coagulation proteins and vitamin K-dependent proteins.
LO 5. Interpret secondary-hemostasis testing: PT, aPTT, mixing studies, factor assays, and inhibitor titers.
LO 6. Understand secondary-hemostasis disorders and work-up, including hemophilia A, hemophilia B, other factor deficiencies, and acquired factor inhibitors.
LO 7. Build the differential diagnosis of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia and know smear findings, clinical setting, work-up, and management.
LO 8. Describe common congenital and acquired thrombophilias, including Factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation, protein C/S deficiency, and related work-up.

The lecture's master framework
Primary hemostasis = platelet plug. Think mucocutaneous bleeding.
Secondary hemostasis = fibrin reinforcement. Think delayed/deep bleeding, hemarthroses, hematomas.
Thrombophilia = the same system tipped too far toward clotting.

Lecture slide 4 - full slide retained. Savage starts with vessel constriction, then the platelet plug (primary hemostasis), then fibrin stabilization (secondary hemostasis). Keep those layers separate as you move through the deck.

Slides 6-8: key players

  • GpIb binds vWF -> platelet adhesion.
  • P2Y12 binds ADP -> platelet activation/recruitment.
  • GpIIb/IIIa binds fibrinogen -> platelet-to-platelet aggregation.
  • Platelet alpha granules contain proteins including fibrinogen and vWF; dense granules contain ADP, serotonin, calcium, etc.
  • vWF comes from endothelial Weibel-Palade bodies, platelet alpha granules, and subendothelium; it also carries factor VIII.
Lecture slide 6 - full slide retained. This slide is receptor-heavy and worth memorizing: GpIb-vWF for adhesion, GpIIb/IIIa-fibrinogen for aggregation, and P2Y12-ADP for activation.
Lecture slide 7 - full slide retained. vWF links platelets to exposed subendothelium and carries factor VIII. One slide-text typo calls fibrinogen factor II; later slides correctly identify fibrinogen as factor I.
Slide typo to actively correct in your head

Slide 7 labels fibrinogen as “factor II,” but Slide 18 correctly lists factor I = fibrinogen/fibrin and factor II = prothrombin/thrombin. Treat the Slide 7 wording as a source typo.

Lecture slide 8 - full slide retained. The three A's are the core primary-hemostasis sequence: adhesion through vWF-GpIb, activation/degranulation, then aggregation through GpIIb/IIIa-fibrinogen bridges.
Need to memorize - the three A's
Adhesion: vWF - GpIb.
Activation: ADP/P2Y12, TXA2, granule release, shape change.
Aggregation: GpIIb/IIIa - fibrinogen - GpIIb/IIIa.

Slides 9-11: testing primary hemostasis

  • Platelet count: quantitative problem?
  • MPV: platelet size; larger/younger platelets can suggest peripheral destruction with marrow compensation.
  • Bleeding time: historical test; Savage notes exams may still ask it.
  • PFA: more standardized modern platelet-function screening.
  • Aggregation studies: add agonists such as ristocetin, collagen, ADP, epinephrine and measure the response.
Lecture slide 10 - full slide retained. The PFA is presented as a more standardized replacement for bleeding time. Conceptually, it tests how efficiently platelets form a plug under controlled conditions.

Slide 12: what primary-hemostasis bleeding looks like

Petechiae + mucocutaneous bleeding - epistaxis, gingival bleeding, menorrhagia, immediate superficial bleeding - should push you toward platelets/vWF before factor deficiencies.

Lecture slide 14 - full slide retained. ITP presents as isolated thrombocytopenia, often with increased MPV, antibody-mediated splenic destruction, and increased megakaryocytes in marrow. The slide emphasizes petechiae/purpura.
ITP pattern
Isolated low platelets + normal PT/aPTT + increased MPV + diagnosis of exclusion.
Lecture slide 16 - full slide retained. Glanzmann = GpIIb/IIIa aggregation defect: abnormal with most agonists but ristocetin preserved. Bernard-Soulier = GpIb adhesion defect: ristocetin response absent, with giant platelets and thrombocytopenia.

Lecture slide 20 - full slide retained. Savage's slide mnemonic: intrinsic counts backward XII, XI, IX, VIII; extrinsic is III + VII = X; common pathway is X, V, II, I.
Physiology vs test-tube cascade

The classic intrinsic/extrinsic diagram is extremely useful for interpreting PT and aPTT, but in vivo coagulation is a network centered heavily around tissue factor and thrombin amplification. Savage explicitly follows the simple cascade with a slide saying it is “really more complicated than that.”

Lecture slide 21 - full slide retained. Factor XIII acts after fibrin formation to crosslink and strengthen the clot. Because PT/aPTT stop timing once fibrin forms, XIII deficiency can bleed despite normal routine coagulation assays.

Slides 23-25: PT, aPTT, and mixing studies

  • PT/INR: extrinsic + common; especially factor VII sensitivity; used for warfarin monitoring.
  • aPTT: intrinsic + common; VIII, IX, XI, XII plus common factors; historically used for unfractionated heparin monitoring.
  • Mixing study: mix patient plasma 1:1 with normal plasma.
  • Correction -> factor deficiency.
  • No correction -> inhibitor.
  • Factor assay quantifies the suspected factor; inhibitor titer is measured in Bethesda units.
Lecture slide 25 - full slide retained. This is one of the most testable slides. Immediate correction indicates a factor deficiency; persistent prolongation indicates an inhibitor. A time-dependent VIII inhibitor may look different after incubation.
Need to memorize - coag tests
PT = VII + common.
aPTT = XII, XI, IX, VIII + common.
Mix corrects = deficiency. Mix fails = inhibitor.
Lecture slide 26 - full slide retained. Factor deficiencies cause delayed bleeding after trauma and deep bleeding into joints/soft tissues - hemarthroses and hematomas - while primary-hemostasis screening such as PFA may be normal.

Slide 27: A and B are clinically very similar

  • Hemophilia A = factor VIII deficiency.
  • Hemophilia B = factor IX deficiency.
  • Both are classically X-linked and produce secondary-hemostasis bleeding: deep tissue bleeding, muscle hematomas, hemarthroses, delayed bleeding after procedures/trauma.
  • Typical labs: normal platelet count, normal PT, prolonged aPTT.
  • If no inhibitor is present, the prolonged aPTT corrects on mixing, followed by a low factor VIII or IX assay.

Lecture slide 28 - full slide retained. Savage uses a time-dependent factor VIII inhibitor to show why some inhibitors may require incubation before the mixing study fully fails to correct.
Severity is factor activity, not the letter A/B

The slide explicitly ties disease severity to how low factor activity is. A patient with severe B can bleed far more than a patient with mild A; the factor number identifies the protein, while residual activity predicts phenotype.

Current 2026 Hemophilia B treatment landscape - external enrichment

The lecture focuses mostly on pathology/work-up, so here is the modern treatment context:

  • Factor IX replacement remains foundational, including prophylactic recombinant/extended-half-life FIX products.
  • HEMGENIX (etranacogene dezaparvovec) is an FDA-approved AAV-based gene therapy for selected adults with Hemophilia B who meet the labeled bleeding/prophylaxis criteria.
  • HYMPAVZI (marstacimab) provides once-weekly non-factor prophylaxis for patients age 12+ with Hemophilia B without FIX inhibitors.
  • ALHEMO (concizumab) is a TFPI antagonist; the current 2025 U.S. label includes age 12+ Hemophilia B with or without FIX inhibitors.

Slides 31-32von Willebrand disease bridges primary and secondary hemostasisLO 1 + LO 3 + LO 6
Lecture slide 31 - full slide retained. vWD is the most common inherited bleeding disorder. It primarily impairs platelet adhesion but can also lower factor VIII, so severe disease may prolong aPTT.
vWD dual role
Primary: vWF helps GpIb-mediated adhesion.
Secondary: vWF carries/stabilizes factor VIII.

Testing in the slide

  • vWF antigen = quantity.
  • Ristocetin activity = function.
  • RIPA = platelet-vWF response at different ristocetin concentrations.
  • aPTT / factor VIII assay can show the secondary-hemostasis effect.
  • vWF is an acute-phase reactant and tends to be lower in type O blood, so borderline type 1 disease can require repeat testing.
Lecture slide 32 - full slide retained. Type 1 is partial quantitative deficiency and most common; type 3 is severe quantitative deficiency; type 2 subtypes are qualitative and differ by multimer/receptor behavior.

vWD checkpoint

Can you explain why vWD usually looks mucocutaneous but can also raise aPTT, and distinguish a vWF quantity assay from a function assay?

Slides 42-43Natural brakes on coagulationLO 8 foundation
Lecture slide 42 - full slide retained. Antithrombin inhibits activated serine proteases with strong effects on IIa and Xa and is accelerated by heparin. Activated protein C inactivates Va and VIIIa; protein S is its cofactor.
The brake system
Antithrombin -> IIa/Xa especially.
Protein C + Protein S -> turn off Va and VIIIa.
Lecture slide 43 - full slide retained. This diagram places thrombomodulin/protein C, antithrombin, endothelial mediators, and fibrinolysis around the clot. It is the conceptual bridge from normal regulation to thrombophilia.
Board enrichment - why heparin works with antithrombin

Heparin does not simply “thin blood.” It markedly accelerates antithrombin activity against clotting proteases. This is why antithrombin deficiency can cause relative heparin resistance.

Slides 45-48Inherited and acquired thrombophiliaLO 8
Lecture slide 45 - full slide retained. Savage emphasizes that most thromboemboli are explained by common acquired risk factors rather than a rare inherited disorder. The slide uses Virchow's triad: endothelial damage, hypercoagulability, and stasis.
Faculty diagnostic restraint

Savage explicitly says most patients with thromboembolism do not require an inherited-thrombophilia work-up. Testing can be expensive, may not change inpatient management, and some assays become unreliable during acute thrombosis or anticoagulant therapy.

Factor V Leiden
Most common inherited thrombophilia -> factor V resists activated protein C.

Protein C/S and warfarin skin necrosis

Protein C and S are vitamin K-dependent and have short half-lives. Early warfarin therapy can reduce these natural anticoagulants before the procoagulant factors fully fall, creating a transient hypercoagulable period - especially dangerous in protein C/S deficiency.

Companion gap-fillHeparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)LO 8 acquired thrombophilia
Why this section is here

Savage lists HIT as an acquired thrombophilia on Slide 45 but does not develop it in the remaining deck. The uploaded companion gives the work-up and management details.

Mechanism

IgG against heparin-PF4 complexes -> Fc receptor-mediated platelet activation -> thrombosis + platelet consumption. The key paradox is that the platelet count falls while the patient becomes more thrombotic, not primarily more hemorrhagic.

4 Ts
Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes.
Classic timing is around days 5-10 unless there has been recent prior heparin exposure.

Companion work-up / management

  • High clinical probability -> stop all heparin immediately, including flushes.
  • Start a non-heparin anticoagulant rather than waiting for confirmatory testing.
  • PF4-heparin antibody immunoassay is a sensitive screen; a functional assay such as the serotonin release assay is confirmatory.
  • Do not reflexively begin warfarin during acute HIT before platelet recovery.
HIT exam answer
Low platelets after heparin + new thrombosis -> STOP heparin and anticoagulate with a non-heparin drug.
