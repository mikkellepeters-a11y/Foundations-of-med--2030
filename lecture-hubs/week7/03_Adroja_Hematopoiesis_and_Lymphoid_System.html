Overview: Official Week 7 Learning Objectives

LO 1. Describe embryonic development of the blood system and the sites of child and adult hematopoiesis.
LO 2. Understand bone marrow hematopoiesis and the concept of hematopoietic stem cells.
LO 3. Recognize the difference between T cells and B cells and know their sites of origin and maturation in bone marrow and thymus.
LO 4. Recognize normal histology of lymph nodes, tonsil, gut-associated lymphoid tissue, spleen, and thymus.
LO 5. Describe the anatomic location and biologic function of the major lymphoid structures: lymph node chains, Peyer's patches, tonsils/adenoids, spleen, and thymus.
LO 6. Describe circulation of cells, blood, and lymphatic fluid through lymph nodes and circulation of red blood cells through the spleen.

The whole lecture in one sentence
Blood cells are generated in changing developmental sites, then adult marrow maintains every lineage; lymphocytes are educated in primary organs and encounter antigen in secondary organs whose architecture is built around efficient cell and antigen traffic.
Slides 3-8Embryonic hematopoiesis and the fetal-to-adult transitionLO 1

Slide 4: primitive hematopoiesis begins with blood islands

  • At about day 18-19, yolk-sac blood islands generate primitive blood and primitive vasculature together.
  • Outer cells become endothelium; inner cells become blood.
  • This wave is transient and is dominated by nucleated erythroid cells with embryonic hemoglobins plus primitive macrophages.
Lecture slide 5 - full slide retained. This is the timing slide to learn from the current deck: yolk sac first, definitive HSC emergence from the AGM region, liver/spleen expansion, then marrow becoming dominant before birth and remaining the physiologic site thereafter.
Developmental timeline - current lecture
Day 18 -> week 8: yolk sac, primitive wave.
Week 4-6: AGM region, first true HSCs from hemogenic endothelium.
Week 6 -> birth: liver dominates; spleen/thymus are colonized.
Week 10 -> life: bone marrow begins and becomes dominant by about week 28.
Clinical correlate

When adult marrow cannot meet demand or is crowded out, fetal sites can reactivate -> extramedullary hematopoiesis with hepatosplenomegaly.

Slides 6-7: HbF and hemoglobin switching

  • HbF = alpha2 gamma2; it has higher oxygen affinity than HbA and supports placental oxygen transfer.
  • The lecture places HbF at roughly 70% at birth and under 2% by one year, while HbA becomes dominant after birth.
  • Alpha chains are needed prenatally, so severe alpha-thalassemia can present in utero.
  • Beta-chain disorders emerge after the postnatal switch, explaining delayed presentation of severe beta-thalassemia and sickle-cell disease.
  • The physiologic hemoglobin nadir at about 8-12 weeks reflects falling EPO drive after birth plus this transition.
Lecture slide 8 - full slide retained. At birth essentially all marrow cavities are red. During childhood fatty conversion proceeds from distal extremities inward. Adult hematopoiesis is concentrated in axial bones plus proximal humerus/femur; stressed yellow marrow can reconvert.
Adult red marrow
Vertebrae • sternum • ribs • pelvis • skull • proximal humerus/femur.
Posterior superior iliac spine = standard adult sampling site.

Slide 11: hematopoietic stem-cell properties

  • Self-renewal: preserves the stem-cell pool.
  • Multipotency: one HSC can reconstitute myeloid and lymphoid lineages.
  • Quiescence: most HSCs are out of cycle, limiting mutation and protecting them from cycle-active chemotherapy.
  • Rare: roughly 1 in 10,000-100,000 nucleated marrow cells in the lecture.
  • Lecture immunophenotype: CD34+ CD38- Lin-.
  • G-CSF can mobilize HSCs into peripheral blood for apheresis collection.
Lecture slide 12 - full slide retained. The lineage map separates common myeloid and common lymphoid output and anchors the major growth factors: EPO for erythroid cells, TPO for megakaryocytes/platelets, and G-CSF for neutrophil production.
Growth-factor anchors
EPO -> RBC. TPO -> platelets. G-CSF -> neutrophils.

Erythroid maturation

Lecture slide 14 - full slide retained. As erythroid cells mature, cell size falls, cytoplasm shifts blue to pink as hemoglobin accumulates, and the nucleus condenses before extrusion at the orthochromatic stage.
Erythroid sequence
Proerythroblast -> basophilic -> polychromatophilic -> orthochromatic -> reticulocyte -> erythrocyte.
Reticulocytes retain residual RNA, so the retic count is a readout of marrow response.

Granulocyte maturation

Lecture slide 15 - full slide retained. Primary azurophilic granules appear at the promyelocyte stage; specific granules at the myelocyte stage make lineage recognition easier. Division stops after the myelocyte.
Neutrophil sequence
Myeloblast -> promyelocyte -> myelocyte -> metamyelocyte -> band -> segmented neutrophil.
A left shift means immature forms are released into blood early under demand.

Megakaryocytes and platelets

Lecture slide 16 - full slide retained. Megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis: DNA replication without cell division. Their huge multilobulated polyploid nucleus makes them a useful marrow landmark.

Megakaryocyte logic

  • Polyploid nucleus: lecture gives approximately 16N-64N.
  • Mature cells abut marrow sinusoids.
  • Proplatelet processes extend through the sinusoidal wall and shed thousands of platelets.
  • TPO is produced by the liver and regulates platelet production.
Lecture slide 17 - full slide retained. The slide asks you to orient yourself using megakaryocytes, erythroid islands, granular myeloid precursors, adipocytes, and bony trabeculae.

The exam point of LO 3
B cell: born + matures in marrow.
T cell: born in marrow, matures in thymus.

Slides 23-24: B vs T cell function

  • B-cell sequence: pro-B -> pre-B -> immature B -> mature naive B; heavy chain first, light chain second, then surface IgM.
  • Strong self-reactivity in marrow triggers receptor editing or deletion: central B-cell tolerance.
  • T cells use a TCR and recognize processed peptide presented by another cell, not free native antigen.
  • Helper T cells coordinate responses; cytotoxic T cells kill infected targets.
Lecture slide 25 - full slide retained. Small B and T lymphocytes look alike on routine morphology: a dense round nucleus with a thin rim of cytoplasm. Plasma cells have eccentric clock-face nuclei, a perinuclear hof, and abundant rough ER for antibody secretion.
Why lymph nodes enlarge during infection

Secondary lymphoid organs are built to amplify rare antigen-specific clones. Antigen encounter -> activation -> proliferation -> effector cells + memory. B-cell expansion enlarges germinal centers; combined B/T proliferation produces reactive lymphadenopathy.

Dendritic-cell route

Dendritic cells sample peripheral tissues, mature after danger signals, travel through afferent lymphatics, and present antigen to naive T cells in the paracortex of the draining node. This route becomes important again when we map lymph-node architecture.

Slides 29-38Lymph nodes: zones, drainage, and two routes inLO 4-6

Why nodes exist

Nodes concentrate naive B cells, naive T cells, antigen, and antigen-presenting cells in the same place. They also filter lymph through macrophage-lined sinuses before fluid returns to blood.

Slide 32: major node chains

  • Cervical: head/neck and Waldeyer's ring.
  • Axillary: upper limb, breast, chest wall.
  • Mediastinal/hilar: lung, heart, esophagus.
  • Mesenteric: bowel.
  • Para-aortic: kidney, gonad, retroperitoneum.
  • Iliac: pelvic viscera.
  • Inguinal: lower limb/perineum/external genitalia.
Lecture slide 33 - full slide retained. The slide provides the core histology map: capsule -> subcapsular sinus -> cortex/B follicles -> paracortex/T zone with HEVs -> medulla/cords and sinuses -> hilum.
Lymph node outside -> in
Capsule -> subcapsular sinus -> cortex (B) -> paracortex (T + HEV) -> medulla (cords + sinuses) -> hilum.
ZoneDominant cells / landmark
CortexB-cell follicles; secondary follicles have pale germinal centers
ParacortexT-cell zone + high endothelial venules

Board reinforcement from the companion

In severe T-cell deficiency such as DiGeorge syndrome, the paracortex is depleted because it is the T-cell zone.

Slide 36: lymph fluid itself

  • Interstitial hydrostatic pressure generates lymph.
  • Muscle contraction helps propel it through one-way lymphatic vessels.
  • Regional chains progressively collect lymph.
  • Return to blood occurs through the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct.
  • Clinical correlate: lymph-node removal can interrupt drainage and cause chronic lymphedema.
Lecture slide 37 - full slide retained. Antigen and dendritic cells arrive through afferent lymphatics into the subcapsular sinus; resting B and T cells arrive from blood through HEVs in the paracortex. Everything exits through the efferent lymphatic at the hilum.
Two routes in, one route out
Lymphatics IN: antigen + dendritic cells -> subcapsular sinus.
Blood IN: naive T/B cells -> HEV in paracortex.
OUT: one efferent lymphatic at the hilum.
Ozzy checkpoint - click him after you can trace both routes without looking.
HEV distinction

HEVs are in lymph-node paracortex and mucosal lymphoid tissue. The spleen does not use HEVs for the same entry route because the spleen has no afferent lymphatics and samples blood directly.

Slide 38: lymphocyte recirculation

Resting cells repeatedly cycle blood -> HEV -> node -> efferent lymph -> thoracic duct -> blood. After activation they spend days proliferating, change adhesion/homing programs, then leave for inflamed tissues.

Slides 39-44MALT, Peyer's patches, appendix, and tonsilsLO 4-5
Mucosal-organ principle

Unlike lymph nodes, mucosal lymphoid tissue is generally unencapsulated and positioned immediately beneath an epithelium. Antigen can cross the epithelial surface directly, so these sites do not need afferent lymphatics.

Slide 40: the mucosal structures

  • Diffuse lymphoid tissue: scattered lymphocytes/plasma cells throughout the lamina propria.
  • Waldeyer's ring: palatine, pharyngeal/adenoid, and lingual tonsils.
  • Peyer's patches: aggregated follicles concentrated in the ileum.
  • Appendix: circumferential lymphoid follicles surrounding the lumen.
Lecture slide 41 - full slide retained. Peyer's patches are unencapsulated aggregated follicles in ileal mucosa/submucosa on the antimesenteric side. M cells transcytose luminal antigen across follicle-associated epithelium.
Peyer's patch recognition
Ileum + aggregated follicles + absent villi over dome + M cells.
Mucosal B-cell output supports dimeric secretory IgA.

Slides 42-43: appendix and Waldeyer's ring

  • Appendix: narrow irregular lumen, colonic crypts, no villi, complete ring of lymphoid follicles.
  • Palatine tonsil: paired, between tonsillar pillars, squamous epithelium with deep crypts.
  • Pharyngeal tonsil/adenoid: roof of nasopharynx, respiratory epithelium, folds rather than deep crypts.
  • Lingual tonsil: base of tongue.
Lecture slide 44 - full slide retained. The palatine tonsil is identified by nonkeratinized stratified squamous epithelium, deep branching crypts, underlying lymphoid nodules, and an incomplete capsule on the deep surface.
Palatine tonsil
Squamous epithelium + deep crypts + lymphoid follicles.
Slides 45-54Spleen: blood filtration, red/white pulp, and RBC quality controlLO 4-6

Slide 46: location and jobs

  • Left hypochondrium, deep to ribs 9-11; intraperitoneal.
  • Filters blood, not lymph.
  • Removes blood-borne antigen/immune complexes and encapsulated bacteria.
  • Tests aged/abnormal RBC deformability and recycles iron.
  • Stores a platelet reservoir and can resume extramedullary hematopoiesis under stress.
Lecture slide 47 - full slide retained. Red pulp contains cords of Billroth and venous sinuses for RBC filtration. White pulp contains T-cell PALS around central arterioles, B-cell follicles, and a marginal zone.
Spleen zoneCore function / landmark
Red pulpRBC-rich cords + sinusoids; mechanical filtration and macrophage clearance
PALST-cell zone wrapped around central arteriole
White-pulp follicleB-cell zone
Marginal zoneInterface with red pulp; macrophages + memory B cells see blood-borne antigen first
Histology differentiator

Spleen has no cortex, no medulla, and no afferent lymphatics. A central arteriole embedded in white pulp is the visual landmark that separates spleen from lymph node.

Lecture slide 50 - full slide retained. Most splenic RBC flow is open circulation: arterioles empty into cords of Billroth, and RBCs must deform through tiny slits between sinus stave cells to return to blood. Stiff cells fail this test and are removed.
Splenic RBC route
Splenic artery -> trabecular artery -> central arteriole/white pulp -> penicillar arterioles -> red pulp -> cords -> sinus -> splenic vein.
Open circulation = majority and performs the deformability test.

Slides 51-54: what happens without or with too much spleen

  • Splenectomy can reduce immune destruction in refractory ITP/AIHA and hemolysis in selected membrane/enzyme disorders.
  • After splenectomy, abnormal cells persist: Howell-Jolly bodies, target cells, acanthocytes.
  • The same smear pattern with a spleen present suggests functional asplenia, classically sickle-cell disease.
  • Vaccination against encapsulated organisms is essential before elective splenectomy; fever afterward is an emergency.
  • Splenomegaly can result from infiltration, portal congestion, chronic hemolytic workload, or extramedullary hematopoiesis.
Lecture slide 52 - full slide retained. Howell-Jolly bodies are residual nuclear fragments normally removed by splenic pitting; they persist when splenic function is absent.
Board reinforcement from the companion

Asplenia classically increases risk from encapsulated organisms, especially pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and meningococcus. Pair that clinical risk with the morphologic clue of Howell-Jolly bodies.

Slides 55-60Thymus: T-cell education, histology, and involutionLO 3-5

Slides 56-57: location and embryology

  • Anterior mediastinum behind the manubrium, over great vessels/pericardium.
  • Thymic epithelium derives from the third pharyngeal pouch; lymphocytes themselves migrate in from marrow.
  • DiGeorge syndrome links failed pouch development with thymic aplasia/T-cell deficiency, hypoparathyroidism, and conotruncal cardiac defects.
Lecture slide 58 - full slide retained. Thymic lobules have a dark cortex and pale medulla. Hassall corpuscles occur only in the medulla. There are no lymphoid follicles or germinal centers.
Thymus recognition
Lobules + dark cortex + pale medulla + Hassall corpuscles.
No follicles. No germinal centers. No afferent lymphatics.
Lecture slide 59 - full slide retained. T-cell precursors arrive from marrow, undergo positive selection in cortex for self-MHC recognition, then negative selection in medulla against strong self-reactivity. Survivors exit as single-positive CD4 or CD8 cells.
StageWhereQuestion being asked
ArrivalCorticomedullary junctionBone-marrow precursor enters as immature thymocyte
Positive selectionCortexCan the TCR interact with self-MHC at all?
Negative selectionMedullaDoes it bind self too strongly? AIRE helps display tissue-restricted antigens.
ExitMedulla -> circulationSingle-positive CD4 or CD8 mature naive T cell
Selection mnemonic
Positive = cortex = keep cells that can see MHC.
Negative = medulla = delete cells that attack self too strongly.

Slide 60: involution

The thymus reaches greatest absolute weight around puberty and then becomes progressively replaced by adipose tissue. New T-cell output falls with age, but residual thymic epithelium can regenerate a repertoire after marrow transplantation.
