Mental model before the lecture starts
Hemoglobinopathy = either the globin protein is made wrong (qualitative) or the correct globin chain is made in the wrong amount (quantitative).
Structural variants change what the chain is. Thalassemias change how much of the chain is produced.
Slides 2-6Normal hemoglobin, globin genes, and developmental switchingLO 1

Slides 2-3: start with the molecule and the fractions

  • Hemoglobin is a tetramer with 4 globin chains + 4 heme groups.
  • Normal adult hemoglobin on the lecture slide: HbA (alpha2 beta2) 96-98%, HbA2 (alpha2 delta2) 2.5-3.5%, and HbF (alpha2 gamma2) <1%.
  • The slide's fetal column shows predominantly HbF (80-85%) with some HbA already present.
  • Embryonic hemoglobins listed: Gower-1, Gower-2, Portland-1.

What this means

Developmental switching matters because a mutation can be present from conception but remain clinically quiet while the affected globin chain is not yet the dominant one. Beta-chain disorders become clinically apparent only after gamma-globin/HbF falls and beta-globin production becomes important.

Lecture slide 4 - full slide retained. Chromosome 16 contains the alpha-like cluster; chromosome 11 contains the beta-like cluster. This genomic organization underlies the deletion-heavy genetics of alpha-thalassemia versus the mostly point/splice/nonsense mechanisms of beta-thalassemia.
Need to memorize
Alpha cluster = chromosome 16, four alpha genes total.
Beta cluster = chromosome 11, two beta genes total.
Lecture slide 6 - full slide retained. Watch both things on this figure: the site of erythropoiesis migrates toward bone marrow, while gamma production falls and beta production rises after birth.
Don't miss the source discrepancy

The lecture slide later states that the fetal-to-adult gamma-to-beta switch is near-complete at about 3 months. The uploaded companion handout says beta-chain disease symptoms are delayed until about 6 months. Keep the two source statements distinct: both are pointing to the same clinical idea - HbF protects early infancy while the postnatal switch occurs over the first months of life.

Board enrichment - why HbF has higher oxygen affinity

HbF binds 2,3-BPG less avidly than HbA, so its oxygen-dissociation curve is left-shifted. That allows fetal blood to extract oxygen from maternal blood. You do not need to turn this into a giant physiology detour; the useful association is HbF = higher O2 affinity + anti-sickling effect.

Slides 7-10Qualitative versus quantitative hemoglobinopathiesLO 2 + LO 4

Slide 7 gives the classification you should use all week

  • Qualitative = Hb variants: usually missense mutations that change globin structure. Examples: HbS, HbC, HbE.
  • Quantitative = thalassemias: decreased or absent production of one or more globin chains.

Slides 8-10: why the location of the amino-acid substitution matters

  • Surface substitutions can create abnormal intermolecular interactions - HbS polymerization is the prototype.
  • Changes near the heme pocket can produce unstable hemoglobin / Heinz bodies or methemoglobinemia.
  • Interchain-contact mutations can alter stability or O2 affinity; high-affinity variants can drive compensatory erythrocytosis.
Official LO gap-fill - HbC and HbE are not developed in the slide deck

The uploaded companion handout supplies the main recognition facts:

  • HbC: beta6 Glu -> Lys; usually mild hemolytic anemia/splenomegaly; HbC crystals can be seen on smear.
  • HbE: beta26 Glu -> Lys; common in Southeast Asia; behaves as both a structural variant and a mild beta-thalassemia because the mutation can alter splicing.
Need to memorize - position 6
HbS: beta6 Glu -> Val.
HbC: beta6 Glu -> Lys.
Same position, completely different protein behavior.
Slides 11-19Sickle cell disease: mutation -> polymerization -> hemolysis + vaso-occlusionLO 4

Slide 11: the molecular lesion

  • Sickle cell anemia = homozygous HbS.
  • beta6 Glu -> Val, with the lecture giving GAG -> GTG.
  • The hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic substitution creates the abnormal interaction that permits deoxygenated HbS to polymerize.
Lecture slide 16 - full slide retained. The core disease mechanism: deoxygenated HbS forms polymers, distorting the RBC and creating the substrate for vaso-occlusion and membrane injury.
Cause -> effect

Deoxygenation / higher intracellular HbS concentration -> polymerization -> rigid sickled RBC -> microvascular obstruction + repeated membrane damage -> ischemia and hemolysis.

Lecture slide 17 - full slide retained. This is the most important mechanistic slide in the SCD block. Polymerization is central, but inflammation, adhesion, dehydration, oxidative membrane damage, endothelial dysfunction, and coagulation feed back into the disease.
Need to memorize - what promotes polymerization
Low O2, acidosis, dehydration / increased MCHC.
HbF inhibits polymerization. HbA also dilutes/inhibits HbS polymer formation.

Slides 13-15: disease genotypes and phenotype severity

  • HbSS = sickle cell anemia.
  • HbSC = compound heterozygous S/C disease.
  • S beta0-thal has no normal beta production from the thal allele and behaves more severely.
  • S beta+-thal retains some beta production and generally has a milder phenotype.
Don't miss the denominator

“Sickle cell disease” is an umbrella category. HbSS is one genotype within SCD. The lecture deliberately lists SC and S/beta-thal variants because genotype changes the hemoglobin mixture and therefore disease severity.

Slides 18-19: complications are manifestations of two core processes

  • Vaso-occlusion / ischemia: pain episodes, dactylitis, acute chest syndrome, stroke, avascular necrosis, priapism, nephropathy.
  • Chronic hemolysis: anemia, bilirubin/gallstones, NO scavenging and endothelial dysfunction.
  • Functional asplenia: infection/sepsis risk after progressive splenic injury.
  • Marrow stress: aplastic crisis can sharply worsen anemia.
Board enrichment - classic infectious clues

Functional asplenia increases risk from encapsulated organisms. In a vignette, Salmonella osteomyelitis is a classic association with SCD, while parvovirus B19 points toward aplastic crisis.

Slides 20-27HbF as a modifier, hydroxyurea, transfusion, transplant, and gene therapyLO 1 + LO 4

Slide 20 is the bridge between developmental biology and treatment

  • High HbF is beneficial in both SCD and beta-thalassemia.
  • In SCD, HbF reduces deoxy-HbS polymerization.
  • In beta-thalassemia, gamma-chain production helps compensate for missing/reduced beta chains and reduces alpha/non-alpha imbalance.
Need to memorize
Hydroxyurea -> increases HbF -> fewer sickling events.
Lecture slide 22 - full slide retained. Use this slide qualitatively: hydroxyurea therapy is tied to HbF induction and changes in the sickling phenotype. Do not waste time memorizing individual cells in this image.

Slide 21: therapeutic buckets

  • Supportive / preventive care and pain management.
  • Transfusion: episodic or chronic depending on indication.
  • Disease-modifying: hydroxyurea, L-glutamine, crizanlizumab; the slide also lists voxelotor and pyruvate kinase inhibitors.
  • Curative-intent approaches: stem-cell transplantation and gene therapy.
Current 2026 update - important correction to the slide

Voxelotor (Oxbryta) should not be treated as a current therapy. The FDA announced its voluntary market withdrawal in September 2024 because the emerging safety data changed the benefit-risk assessment. This is external/current information, not a correction silently inserted into the professor's slide.

FDA Oxbryta withdrawal notice

Lecture slide 24 - full slide retained. The slide separates gene addition from gene editing. The common strategic theme is either create an anti-sickling beta-like globin or reactivate gamma-globin/HbF.
What BCL11A editing is actually doing

BCL11A normally suppresses gamma-globin after birth. Editing its erythroid regulatory element releases that brake, allowing HbF to rise. This does not directly repair the HbS nucleotide in the common Casgevy strategy.

Current 2026 FDA update

The slide correctly highlights the 2023 approvals of Casgevy (exa-cel) and Lyfgenia (lovo-cel). FDA labeling has continued to evolve: the current Casgevy page includes SCD with recurrent VOCs and transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia, with newer 2026 approvals expanding age eligibility. Keep the mechanism distinction clear: Casgevy uses genome editing to increase HbF, while Lyfgenia adds an anti-sickling beta-globin gene.

Slides 28-32Thalassemias: alpha vs beta genetics and chain imbalanceLO 2 + LO 5

Slides 28-31: classify the production defect

  • Alpha-thalassemia: most commonly deletional. You have four alpha genes total, so phenotype follows the number and arrangement of deleted genes.
  • Beta-thalassemia: usually point/splice/transcription/frameshift/nonsense mutations affecting the two beta genes.
  • beta+ = reduced beta-globin production.
  • beta0 = absent beta-globin production from that allele.
Need to memorize - genetics shortcut
Alpha = deletions, chromosome 16, count genes.
Beta = mutations, chromosome 11, beta+ versus beta0.

Cis vs trans matters for reproductive risk

Cis (--/alpha alpha) places both deletions on one chromosome; trans (-alpha/-alpha) places one on each. The companion handout highlights cis as more common in Asian ancestry and trans as more common in African ancestry. The key clinical reason to care is the probability of producing a fetus with a severe alpha-gene deletion pattern.

Lecture slide 32 - full slide retained. This slide gives the chain-imbalance logic. Alpha-thal leaves excess beta-like chains that can form tetramers; beta-thal leaves excess alpha chains, which are very insoluble and damage erythroid precursors and RBCs.
Why beta-thal causes so much ineffective erythropoiesis

Unpaired alpha chains are poorly soluble. They precipitate in erythroid precursors, causing oxidative/membrane injury and death in the marrow. That is why beta-thalassemia combines ineffective erythropoiesis + hemolysis.

Slides 33-40Beta-thalassemia phenotype, complications, and therapyLO 5
Lecture slide 33 - full slide retained. Follow the feedback loops: excess free alpha chains drive ineffective erythropoiesis and hemolysis; anemia drives erythropoietin and marrow expansion; ineffective erythropoiesis increases iron absorption; transfusions add still more iron.

Beta-thal trait, intermedia, major

  • Trait: heterozygous beta0 or beta+; often asymptomatic or mild anemia; lecture emphasizes HbA2 around 5% and sometimes mildly increased HbF.
  • Intermedia: moderate anemia, variable transfusion requirement, hepatosplenomegaly/hypersplenism, skeletal changes, extramedullary hematopoiesis.
  • Major: homozygous or compound heterozygous severe disease, classically transfusion-dependent in early infancy with marrow expansion, hepatosplenomegaly, growth/puberty effects, and iron overload.
Need to memorize - beta-thal trait clue
Microcytosis + elevated HbA2.
The slide gives HbA2 around 5% in trait.
Why the bones and face change

Severe chronic anemia drives massive EPO stimulation. Because erythropoiesis is ineffective, the body keeps expanding marrow space without efficiently correcting the anemia. The result is skeletal remodeling and extramedullary hematopoiesis.

Slides 38-39: conventional and curative treatment

  • Regular RBC transfusion can suppress ineffective erythropoiesis and maintain oxygen delivery, but causes cumulative iron overload.
  • Iron chelation is therefore inseparable from chronic transfusion therapy.
  • HSCT can be curative in selected patients.
  • Gene therapy aims to restore effective beta-like globin production or reactivate HbF.
Current 2026 treatment updates

Zynteglo (betibeglogene autotemcel) is FDA-approved for adult and pediatric patients with beta-thalassemia who require regular RBC transfusions. Casgevy is also FDA-approved for transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia. In January 2026, FDA approved mitapivat (Aqvesme) for anemia in adults with alpha- or beta-thalassemia, making the lecture's final pyruvate-kinase slide especially current.

Why this section exists

The official Week 7 objectives explicitly require composition-analysis interpretation, but the 40-slide deck gives normal fractions and genotype descriptions rather than a full interpretation algorithm. This section is therefore clearly labeled companion/board enrichment, not something silently attributed to a slide.

First question on any hemoglobin analysis
Is HbA present? If yes, at least some normal beta chains are being produced. If no, think severe beta-chain replacement/absence (for example HbSS, beta0-thal major, or a compound variant pattern).

Exam trap

Microcytosis does not automatically mean iron deficiency. Thalassemia often produces marked microcytosis out of proportion to the degree of anemia. The companion handout also supplies the board-style Mentzer index clue: MCV/RBC count <13 favors thalassemia, while >13 favors iron deficiency.
