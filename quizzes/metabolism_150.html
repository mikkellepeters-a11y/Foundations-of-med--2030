Part II: Answer Key and Explanations

Each entry identifies the correct answer, difficulty level, reasoning pathway, and a high-value distractor distinction.

Cellular Energetics

1. A — Transfer of phosphate from phosphocreatine to ADP by creatine kinase In-house

Reasoning: Phosphocreatine serves as a rapidly available high-energy phosphate reserve. Creatine kinase transfers its phosphate to ADP, immediately regenerating ATP during abrupt energy demand.

Key distractor distinction: The reverse creatine kinase reaction stores phosphate during recovery rather than supplying ATP at the onset of contraction.

2. B — A typical mixed diet Intermediate

Reasoning: The respiratory quotient is CO₂ produced divided by O₂ consumed: 255/300 = 0.85. An RQ near 0.85 is characteristic of a mixed diet.

Key distractor distinction: Pure carbohydrate oxidation is closer to 1.0, whereas fat oxidation is closer to 0.7.

3. A — Conversion of excess carbohydrate into fatty acids Step 1 style

Reasoning: An RQ above 1.0 indicates carbohydrate overfeeding with net lipogenesis. Excess glucose is converted to acetyl-CoA and then stored as fatty acid.

Key distractor distinction: Fat oxidation lowers the RQ toward 0.7 rather than raising it above 1.0.

4. A — Succinyl-CoA In-house

Reasoning: Succinyl-CoA is a biosynthetic precursor for porphyrins and therefore heme. This illustrates the anabolic role of TCA-cycle intermediates.

Key distractor distinction: Citrate supplies carbon for fatty acid and sterol synthesis, not directly for heme.

5. C — Glutamate and purines Intermediate

Reasoning: α-Ketoglutarate is a precursor for glutamate and other amino acids and contributes to purine synthesis. Withdrawal of this intermediate therefore affects both pathways.

Key distractor distinction: Oxaloacetate, rather than α-ketoglutarate, supplies aspartate for purine and pyrimidine synthesis.

6. C — Complex IV Step 1 style

Reasoning: Complex IV transfers electrons to oxygen, the final electron acceptor. Cyanide, carbon monoxide, and azide inhibit this step and halt electron transport.

Key distractor distinction: Complex V is ATP synthase; it uses the proton gradient but does not transfer electrons to oxygen.

7. B — Decreased ATP synthesis, a progressively steeper proton gradient, and secondary slowing of electron transport Step 1 style

Reasoning: Oligomycin blocks the proton channel of ATP synthase. Protons cannot return through Complex V, the gradient becomes excessively steep, ATP synthesis falls, and electron transport slows because further proton pumping becomes difficult.

Key distractor distinction: Increased oxygen consumption with proton leak describes an uncoupler, not an ATP-synthase inhibitor.

8. C — Increased oxygen consumption, decreased ATP production, and increased heat Step 1 style

Reasoning: An uncoupler dissipates the proton gradient without ATP synthase. Electron transport and oxygen consumption increase, ATP production decreases, and the lost energy is released as heat.

Key distractor distinction: Oligomycin decreases ATP and eventually slows oxygen consumption; it does not cause a proton leak.

9. B — Nonshivering heat production in brown adipose tissue Intermediate

Reasoning: Thermogenin, or UCP1, is a physiologic uncoupler in brown adipose tissue. It allows proton flow to generate heat rather than ATP and is especially important in newborns.

Key distractor distinction: Phosphocreatine and creatine kinase provide rapid ATP regeneration in muscle.

10. E — Complex I In-house

Reasoning: Rotenone is identified in the lecture as a Complex I inhibitor.

Key distractor distinction: Antimycin A inhibits Complex III, while cyanide and carbon monoxide inhibit Complex IV.

11. C — Antimycin A Intermediate

Reasoning: Antimycin A inhibits Complex III of the electron transport chain.

Key distractor distinction: Rotenone inhibits Complex I, oligomycin inhibits Complex V, and DNP and thermogenin are uncouplers.

12. D — Amphibolic Intermediate

Reasoning: The TCA cycle is amphibolic because it participates in catabolism while also supplying intermediates for biosynthesis.

Key distractor distinction: Calling the pathway only catabolic or only anabolic ignores its simultaneous roles.

Glucose Homeostasis

13. D — It traps glucose within the cell In-house

Reasoning: Adding phosphate creates glucose-6-phosphate, which cannot freely leave through glucose transporters and is therefore trapped within the cell.

Key distractor distinction: Glucose-6-phosphate remains a metabolic crossroads and is not committed exclusively to glycolysis.

14. B — High Km, induction by insulin, and lack of product inhibition by glucose-6-phosphate Intermediate

Reasoning: Glucokinase in liver and pancreas has a higher Km, is induced by insulin, and is not inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate. These features allow substantial hepatic glucose handling after meals.

Key distractor distinction: Hexokinase has higher affinity and is inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate.

15. E — Activation of phosphofructokinase-1 Intermediate

Reasoning: AMP signals low cellular energy and activates PFK-1, the rate-limiting and committed enzyme of glycolysis.

Key distractor distinction: ATP and citrate inhibit PFK-1, whereas fructose-1,6-bisphosphate activates pyruvate kinase by feed-forward regulation.

16. A — Decreased PFK-1 activity and decreased glycolysis Step 1 style

Reasoning: ATP and citrate indicate abundant energy and inhibit PFK-1, slowing glycolysis.

Key distractor distinction: Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate activates PFK-1 and is associated with insulin rather than glucagon.

17. E — Pyruvate kinase Intermediate

Reasoning: Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate feed-forward activates pyruvate kinase, helping coordinate the final irreversible step with upstream pathway flux.

Key distractor distinction: PFK-1 produces fructose-1,6-bisphosphate and is not activated by its own product in this way.

18. B — NAD+ In-house

Reasoning: Lactate dehydrogenase oxidizes NADH to NAD+, allowing glycolysis to continue when oxidative phosphorylation cannot reoxidize NADH.

Key distractor distinction: The reaction does not directly generate ATP; ATP is produced at glycolytic substrate-level phosphorylation steps.

19. C — Increased conversion of pyruvate to lactate to regenerate NAD+ Step 1 style

Reasoning: Tissue hypoxia limits oxidative phosphorylation. Cells reduce pyruvate to lactate to regenerate NAD+, allowing continued glycolytic ATP production and causing lactic acidosis.

Key distractor distinction: Conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA requires aerobic mitochondrial metabolism and is reduced during severe hypoxia.

20. A — Formation of spiky echinocytes followed by splenic removal Intermediate

Reasoning: RBCs depend entirely on glycolysis for ATP. ATP depletion impairs ion pumps and membrane integrity, producing echinocytes that are removed by splenic macrophages.

Key distractor distinction: Heinz bodies arise from oxidative damage in G6PD deficiency, not directly from ATP depletion.

21. D — A right shift with enhanced oxygen unloading Intermediate

Reasoning: 2,3-BPG binds and stabilizes deoxygenated hemoglobin, lowering oxygen affinity and shifting the dissociation curve to the right to improve tissue unloading.

Key distractor distinction: Fetal hemoglobin binds 2,3-BPG poorly and therefore has higher oxygen affinity.

22. B — Increased 2,3-BPG and a right-shifted oxygen dissociation curve Step 1 style

Reasoning: Pyruvate kinase deficiency backs up glycolytic intermediates, increasing 2,3-BPG. This shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the right even as ATP depletion causes hemolysis.

Key distractor distinction: Hexokinase deficiency, not pyruvate kinase deficiency, reduces production of both ATP and 2,3-BPG.

23. E — Low ATP, low 2,3-BPG, and increased hemoglobin oxygen affinity Step 1 style

Reasoning: Hexokinase deficiency blocks the first glycolytic step, decreasing both ATP and 2,3-BPG. Low 2,3-BPG shifts hemoglobin left and impairs oxygen unloading.

Key distractor distinction: Pyruvate kinase deficiency causes low ATP but increased 2,3-BPG.

24. D — Phosphofructokinase Step 1 style

Reasoning: PFK deficiency, or Tarui disease, affects glycolysis in both muscle and RBCs, causing exercise intolerance and hemolytic anemia.

Key distractor distinction: McArdle disease affects muscle glycogen phosphorylase and causes exercise symptoms without hemolytic anemia.

25. E — Only liver contains glucose-6-phosphatase In-house

Reasoning: The liver expresses glucose-6-phosphatase and can convert glucose-6-phosphate to free glucose for release. Muscle lacks this enzyme and uses glycogen locally.

Key distractor distinction: Both liver and muscle contain glycogen phosphorylase and respond to relevant hormonal or contraction signals.

26. D — Dietary glucose first, hepatic glycogenolysis during the first day, then gluconeogenesis Intermediate

Reasoning: Recently absorbed glucose supplies the early postprandial period. Hepatic glycogenolysis predominates during roughly the first 24 hours, after which gluconeogenesis becomes primary.

Key distractor distinction: Muscle glycogen cannot directly supply blood glucose because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

27. A — Pyruvate to lactate and oxaloacetate to malate Step 1 style

Reasoning: Ethanol metabolism raises NADH, pushing pyruvate toward lactate and oxaloacetate toward malate. This removes gluconeogenic precursors and promotes hypoglycemia and lactic acidosis.

Key distractor distinction: The reverse reactions would support gluconeogenesis rather than inhibit it.

28. C — Glucose-6-phosphatase Step 1 style

Reasoning: Von Gierke disease is caused by glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency. Glucose-6-phosphate cannot be converted to free glucose, producing severe fasting hypoglycemia and diversion into lactate and lipid pathways.

Key distractor distinction: Debranching enzyme deficiency causes Cori disease, which is generally milder and has normal lactate because gluconeogenesis remains intact.

29. D — Debranching enzyme deficiency Intermediate

Reasoning: Cori disease results from debranching enzyme deficiency. It causes hepatomegaly and hypoglycemia but is milder than von Gierke disease because gluconeogenesis is intact, so lactate is typically normal.

Key distractor distinction: Andersen disease causes abnormal long-branched glycogen and progressive cirrhosis.

30. B — Lysosomal α-1,4-glucosidase Step 1 style

Reasoning: Pompe disease is caused by lysosomal α-1,4-glucosidase deficiency and prominently affects cardiac and skeletal muscle, causing cardiomegaly and hypotonia.

Key distractor distinction: Von Gierke disease causes severe fasting hypoglycemia and hepatomegaly rather than primary cardiomyopathy.

31. A — Muscle glycogen breakdown Intermediate

Reasoning: McArdle disease is a deficiency of muscle glycogen phosphorylase. Exercise cannot mobilize muscle glycogen, lactate fails to rise, and patients may experience a second-wind phenomenon.

Key distractor distinction: Pompe disease affects lysosomal glycogen degradation and causes infantile cardiomyopathy and hypotonia.

32. A — NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate In-house

Reasoning: The oxidative phase of the PPP produces NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate. G6PD is its rate-limiting enzyme.

Key distractor distinction: The pathway does not directly produce ATP; NADPH supports reductive synthesis and antioxidant defense.

Energy Balance and Nutrition

33. B — 370 kcal In-house

Reasoning: Carbohydrate provides 4 kcal/g, protein 4 kcal/g, and fat 9 kcal/g: 200 + 80 + 90 = 370 kcal.

Key distractor distinction: Using 4 kcal/g for fat would underestimate the meal's energy content.

34. E — Basal metabolic rate Intermediate

Reasoning: BMR is the largest component of TDEE, accounting for about 60%. TEF is roughly 10%, and physical activity is the most variable component.

Key distractor distinction: Exercise can be substantial in an individual but does not normally exceed BMR as the largest baseline component.

35. C — Thermic effect of food Step 1 style

Reasoning: Protein has the highest thermic effect, about 20-30%, because its metabolism requires energy-intensive deamination, urea synthesis, and gluconeogenesis.

Key distractor distinction: Carbohydrate has a moderate TEF and lipid has the lowest TEF.

36. E — The more muscular adult has a higher BMR Intermediate

Reasoning: Lean body mass is a major determinant of BMR. Greater muscle mass raises resting energy expenditure even when total body weight is the same.

Key distractor distinction: Equal body weight does not imply equal metabolically active tissue.

37. B — Overweight In-house

Reasoning: BMI = 90/(1.8²) ≈ 27.8 kg/m², which is in the overweight range of 25-29.9.

Key distractor distinction: Obesity begins at a BMI of 30 kg/m² in the lecture's classification.

38. C — Resistance to leptin signaling in the hypothalamus Intermediate

Reasoning: Common human obesity is associated with hyperleptinemia and central leptin resistance, so the hypothalamus does not adequately respond to the satiety signal.

Key distractor distinction: True genetic leptin deficiency produces low leptin and is rare.

39. E — Leptin replacement Step 1 style

Reasoning: Rare congenital leptin deficiency can respond dramatically to leptin replacement because the missing satiety signal is restored.

Key distractor distinction: Most obese patients have leptin resistance and would not be expected to respond this way.

40. C — Heart — primarily fatty acids, with use of lactate and ketones Intermediate

Reasoning: The heart is a continuously aerobic organ that primarily uses fatty acids but can readily use lactate, ketones, and glucose.

Key distractor distinction: The brain cannot directly use circulating free fatty acids, and RBCs cannot use ketones because they lack mitochondria.

41. C — It reduces the need for amino acid-derived gluconeogenesis and spares muscle protein Step 1 style

Reasoning: Brain ketone use lowers its glucose requirement, reducing the need to catabolize muscle protein for gluconeogenic amino acids.

Key distractor distinction: RBCs still require glucose, and the brain uses ketones rather than directly using fatty acids.

42. D — Kwashiorkor In-house

Reasoning: Kwashiorkor is characterized by protein deficiency with edema and fatty liver. Marasmus reflects global calorie deficiency with marked wasting.

Key distractor distinction: Marasmus does not classically produce the same edema pattern from isolated protein deficiency.

Minor Hexose Pathways and Reactive Oxygen Species

43. E — Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase In-house

Reasoning: Classic galactosemia is caused by GALT deficiency and presents after lactose exposure with liver disease, failure to thrive, and cataracts.

Key distractor distinction: Galactokinase deficiency primarily causes cataracts without severe hepatic or neurologic toxicity.

44. D — Galactose-1-phosphate Step 1 style

Reasoning: GALT deficiency causes toxic accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate in the liver, brain, and kidneys. Galactitol contributes particularly to lens osmotic damage and cataracts.

Key distractor distinction: A glucose-specific dipstick can be negative because the reducing sugar is galactose rather than glucose.

45. C — Galactokinase deficiency Intermediate

Reasoning: Galactokinase deficiency causes galactose to be shunted to galactitol, producing isolated infantile cataracts with little systemic toxicity.

Key distractor distinction: Classic GALT deficiency causes cataracts plus major liver and neurologic disease.

46. D — UDP-galactose 4-epimerase Intermediate

Reasoning: UDP-galactose 4-epimerase deficiency can produce a severe phenotype resembling classic galactosemia.

Key distractor distinction: Galactokinase deficiency is usually much milder and predominantly causes cataracts.

47. A — Fructokinase In-house

Reasoning: Essential fructosuria is a benign fructokinase deficiency. Fructose is not trapped in hepatocytes and is excreted in blood and urine.

Key distractor distinction: Aldolase B deficiency causes severe symptomatic hereditary fructose intolerance.

48. E — Accumulation of fructose-1-phosphate with phosphate trapping and ATP depletion Step 1 style

Reasoning: Aldolase B deficiency causes fructose-1-phosphate accumulation. Phosphate trapping depletes ATP and inhibits glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, producing severe hypoglycemia.

Key distractor distinction: Fructokinase deficiency causes benign fructosuria without phosphate trapping.

49. E — It bypasses PFK-1 and can rapidly deplete intracellular phosphate Step 1 style

Reasoning: Fructose enters glycolysis downstream of PFK-1, so carbon influx is relatively unregulated. Rapid fructose phosphorylation can transiently trap phosphate, promote purine degradation, and raise uric acid.

Key distractor distinction: Fructose does not suppress purine degradation; phosphate depletion promotes it.

50. E — Accumulation of a phosphorylated sugar that traps phosphate and lowers ATP Step 1 style

Reasoning: Both disorders accumulate a phosphorylated sugar, galactose-1-phosphate or fructose-1-phosphate. Phosphate trapping lowers ATP, inhibits glycogen breakdown, and increases reliance on anaerobic glycolysis.

Key distractor distinction: Galactitol causes cataracts in galactose disorders but is not the shared cause of hypoglycemia in both diseases.

51. A — Insufficient sorbitol dehydrogenase in the lens and Schwann cells Intermediate

Reasoning: During hyperglycemia, aldose reductase converts glucose to sorbitol. Tissues with inadequate sorbitol dehydrogenase accumulate osmotically active sorbitol and sustain cellular swelling and injury.

Key distractor distinction: High sorbitol dehydrogenase would convert sorbitol to fructose and limit accumulation.

52. D — Sorbitol is converted to fructose and increases fructose-1-phosphate formation Step 1 style

Reasoning: Sorbitol can be oxidized to fructose. In aldolase B deficiency, that fructose is converted to fructose-1-phosphate and worsens phosphate trapping and toxicity.

Key distractor distinction: Fructose itself is not a substrate for aldose reductase, which is why cataracts are uncommon in HFI.

53. D — NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate In-house

Reasoning: The PPP generates NADPH for reductive reactions and antioxidant defense and ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis. It does not directly produce ATP.

Key distractor distinction: Reduced glutathione is maintained using PPP-derived NADPH but is not a direct principal product of the pathway.

54. C — Inability of glutathione reductase to regenerate reduced glutathione because NADPH is deficient Step 1 style

Reasoning: G6PD deficiency lowers NADPH. Glutathione reductase cannot efficiently regenerate GSH, allowing ROS to denature hemoglobin and injure the RBC membrane.

Key distractor distinction: Glutathione peroxidase uses GSH to detoxify hydrogen peroxide; its substrate is not FADH2.

55. A — Glutathione peroxidase uses GSH to reduce H2O2, then glutathione reductase uses NADPH to regenerate GSH Intermediate

Reasoning: Glutathione peroxidase converts H2O2 to water while oxidizing GSH to GSSG. Glutathione reductase then uses NADPH to restore GSH.

Key distractor distinction: Superoxide dismutase converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide but does not regenerate glutathione.

56. C — NADPH oxidase Step 1 style

Reasoning: Chronic granulomatous disease results from NADPH oxidase deficiency. Phagocytes cannot generate superoxide, so the DHR test lacks fluorescence and catalase-positive organisms are especially problematic.

Key distractor distinction: MPO deficiency preserves superoxide production and therefore has a normal DHR/NBT test.

57. A — Formation of hypochlorous acid from H2O2 and chloride by myeloperoxidase Intermediate

Reasoning: MPO deficiency prevents formation of hypochlorous acid from H2O2 and chloride. Because NADPH oxidase is intact, DHR testing is normal, and most patients are asymptomatic except for increased Candida risk.

Key distractor distinction: CGD would impair superoxide production and yield an abnormal DHR test.

Fatty Acid Metabolism and Ketone Bodies

58. D — Acetyl-CoA carboxylase In-house

Reasoning: Acetyl-CoA carboxylase converts acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA and is the rate-limiting enzyme of fatty acid synthesis. The carboxylation requires biotin.

Key distractor distinction: Fatty acid synthase uses malonyl-CoA to build palmitate but is not the rate-limiting enzyme.

59. D — Dephosphorylated active ACC and inactive hormone-sensitive lipase Intermediate

Reasoning: Insulin promotes dephosphorylation and activation of ACC while dephosphorylating and inhibiting hormone-sensitive lipase. This favors synthesis and storage.

Key distractor distinction: Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I, and palmitoyl-CoA feedback-inhibits ACC.

60. E — Formation and export of citrate followed by cleavage by ATP citrate lyase Intermediate

Reasoning: Acetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form citrate, which is exported. ATP citrate lyase then regenerates cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA.

Key distractor distinction: The carnitine shuttle transports long-chain fatty acyl groups into mitochondria, not acetyl-CoA out.

61. C — Palmitate, a 16-carbon saturated fatty acid Intermediate

Reasoning: Fatty acid synthase adds two-carbon units from malonyl-CoA and uses NADPH to form palmitate, a 16-carbon saturated fatty acid.

Key distractor distinction: Humans cannot synthesize linoleic acid de novo because double bonds cannot be introduced beyond carbon 9.

62. C — Linoleic and linolenic acids In-house

Reasoning: Linoleic acid and linolenic acid are essential fatty acids. They serve as precursors for compounds including arachidonic acid, EPA, and DHA.

Key distractor distinction: Palmitate is synthesized by fatty acid synthase, and stearate can be formed by elongation.

63. B — Adipocytes require glucose to generate glycerol-3-phosphate because they lack glycerol kinase Intermediate

Reasoning: Adipocytes lack glycerol kinase and therefore depend on glycolytic intermediates from glucose to make glycerol-3-phosphate, the triglyceride backbone.

Key distractor distinction: The liver can make glycerol-3-phosphate from either glucose or free glycerol.

64. C — PKA-mediated phosphorylation and activation of hormone-sensitive lipase Step 1 style

Reasoning: Glucagon and epinephrine activate PKA, which phosphorylates hormone-sensitive lipase and promotes release of free fatty acids and glycerol.

Key distractor distinction: Insulin dephosphorylates and inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase.

65. A — Carnitine palmitoyltransferase I In-house

Reasoning: CPT-I controls entry of long-chain fatty acyl groups into the mitochondrial matrix through the carnitine shuttle.

Key distractor distinction: Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase catalyzes the first oxidation reaction after the fatty acid is already inside the mitochondrial matrix.

66. C — Inhibition of CPT-I and reduced mitochondrial fatty acid entry Step 1 style

Reasoning: Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT-I. When fatty acid synthesis is active, this blocks long-chain fatty acid transport into mitochondria and prevents simultaneous β-oxidation.

Key distractor distinction: Malonyl-CoA is a substrate for fatty acid synthesis, not an inhibitor of fatty acid synthase.

67. C — One NADH, one FADH2, and one acetyl-CoA while shortening the chain by two carbons Intermediate

Reasoning: Each β-oxidation cycle generates one FADH2, one NADH, and one acetyl-CoA and shortens the fatty acid by two carbons.

Key distractor distinction: The TCA cycle, not β-oxidation, generates three NADH, one FADH2, one GTP, and two CO2 per acetyl-CoA.

68. A — Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency Step 1 style

Reasoning: MCAD deficiency causes fasting-induced hypoketotic hypoglycemia because medium-chain fatty acids cannot be oxidized to provide acetyl-CoA and energy. Elevated acylcarnitines and dicarboxylic acids support the diagnosis.

Key distractor distinction: Von Gierke disease causes severe hypoglycemia with hepatomegaly and lactic acidosis, but ketone synthesis is not primarily blocked.

69. A — Hypoglycemia with low ketones Step 1 style

Reasoning: Failure to transport long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria impairs β-oxidation. This decreases acetyl-CoA for ketogenesis and ATP needed for gluconeogenesis, producing hypoketotic hypoglycemia.

Key distractor distinction: VLDL synthesis is a fed-state process and would not compensate for failure of fasting fatty acid oxidation.

70. E — Biotin Intermediate

Reasoning: Propionyl-CoA carboxylase requires biotin to form methylmalonyl-CoA.

Key distractor distinction: Cobalamin is required at the next step, where methylmalonyl-CoA mutase forms succinyl-CoA.

71. C — Methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA Step 1 style

Reasoning: Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, a vitamin B12-dependent enzyme, converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA. Its deficiency causes methylmalonic acidemia.

Key distractor distinction: Propionyl-CoA carboxylase deficiency causes propionic acidemia and requires biotin.

72. D — Zellweger syndrome Step 1 style

Reasoning: Zellweger syndrome is a defect of peroxisome biogenesis, causing global peroxisomal dysfunction, VLCFA accumulation, severe neurologic disease, and early death.

Key distractor distinction: X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy involves defective transport of VLCFAs into otherwise present peroxisomes and usually presents later in childhood.

73. B — Failure to transport VLCFAs into peroxisomes Step 1 style

Reasoning: X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy is caused by a transporter defect that prevents VLCFA entry into peroxisomes, leading to white-matter and adrenal-cortex injury.

Key distractor distinction: Zellweger syndrome is a global peroxisome-biogenesis defect that presents severely in infancy.

74. B — Phytanic acid Intermediate

Reasoning: Refsum disease is a defect of alpha-oxidation of phytanic acid. The classic tetrad is retinitis pigmentosa, anosmia, ataxia, and peripheral neuropathy.

Key distractor distinction: VLCFAs accumulate in Zellweger syndrome and X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy.

75. A — Thiophorase Intermediate

Reasoning: The liver lacks thiophorase, which activates acetoacetate for ketolysis. It can therefore synthesize ketone bodies but cannot consume them.

Key distractor distinction: HMG-CoA synthase is the rate-limiting ketogenesis enzyme and must be present for hepatic ketone production.

Cholesterol Metabolism and Bile Acids

76. D — HMG-CoA reductase In-house

Reasoning: HMG-CoA reductase converts HMG-CoA to mevalonate and is the committed, rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis and the target of statins.

Key distractor distinction: Cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase is rate-limiting for bile acid synthesis, not cholesterol synthesis.

77. E — Insulin-mediated dephosphorylation of HMG-CoA reductase Intermediate

Reasoning: Insulin activates a phosphatase that dephosphorylates and activates HMG-CoA reductase in the fed state.

Key distractor distinction: Glucagon promotes phosphorylation and inactivation of HMG-CoA reductase.

78. E — SREBP-SCAP moves to the Golgi, SREBP is cleaved, and the active fragment enters the nucleus Step 1 style

Reasoning: Low ER cholesterol permits the SREBP-SCAP complex to move to the Golgi. Cleaved SREBP enters the nucleus and increases transcription of HMG-CoA reductase and related cholesterol-handling genes.

Key distractor distinction: High cholesterol causes SCAP to bind INSIG and retain SREBP in the ER.

79. C — Binding of cholesterol to SCAP followed by INSIG-mediated ER retention Intermediate

Reasoning: Cholesterol binding to SCAP promotes interaction with INSIG, retaining the SREBP-SCAP complex in the ER and preventing transcriptional activation.

Key distractor distinction: Golgi transport and cleavage occur when cholesterol is low.

80. A — Cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase In-house

Reasoning: Cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase, or CYP7A1, is the rate-limiting enzyme in bile acid synthesis.

Key distractor distinction: HMG-CoA reductase controls cholesterol synthesis rather than cholesterol elimination.

81. C — Secondary — deoxycholic acid Intermediate

Reasoning: Cholic and chenodeoxycholic acids are primary bile acids made by the liver. Gut bacteria convert them to secondary bile acids, including deoxycholic and lithocholic acids.

Key distractor distinction: Chenodeoxycholic acid is a primary bile acid.

82. A — Impaired bile acid reabsorption with steatorrhea Step 1 style

Reasoning: More than 95% of bile salts are normally reabsorbed in the ileum. Resection causes bile salt loss, reducing the bile salt pool and impairing fat digestion and absorption, producing steatorrhea.

Key distractor distinction: Loss of bile acids removes feedback inhibition and may increase synthesis, but steatorrhea is the more direct clinical consequence.

83. D — Binding bile acids in the intestine and increasing their fecal loss Intermediate

Reasoning: Cholestyramine prevents enterohepatic reabsorption of bile acids. The liver must consume more cholesterol to replace them, lowering cholesterol stores.

Key distractor distinction: Inhibiting CYP7A1 would reduce conversion of cholesterol to bile acids and oppose this effect.

84. A — An increased cholesterol-to-bile salt ratio Step 1 style

Reasoning: Cholesterol precipitates when bile is supersaturated, most importantly when cholesterol is high relative to bile salts. Obesity, female sex, fertility or estrogen exposure, and age around forty are classic risk factors.

Key distractor distinction: Bilirubin is more relevant to pigment stones, not the common cholesterol stones described.

85. A — Cholesterol synthesis occurs in the cytoplasm, whereas ketogenesis occurs in mitochondria Intermediate

Reasoning: The early steps are similar, but cytoplasmic HMG-CoA is used for cholesterol synthesis, whereas mitochondrial HMG-CoA is used for ketogenesis.

Key distractor distinction: Both pathways begin with acetyl-CoA and use HMG-CoA synthase.

Lipoprotein Metabolism

86. E — Bound to albumin In-house

Reasoning: Free fatty acids circulate bound to albumin. Triglycerides, cholesterol, and cholesteryl esters require lipoprotein particles.

Key distractor distinction: Albumin transport is distinct from ApoB-containing lipoprotein transport of complex lipids.

87. E — A hydrophobic core of triglycerides and cholesteryl esters surrounded by phospholipids, free cholesterol, and apolipoproteins Intermediate

Reasoning: Hydrophobic triglycerides and cholesteryl esters occupy the core. Amphipathic phospholipids, free cholesterol, and apolipoproteins form the surface shell.

Key distractor distinction: Free fatty acids are usually carried by albumin rather than forming the main lipoprotein shell.

88. A — HDL In-house

Reasoning: HDL is the smallest, most protein-rich, and therefore highest-density lipoprotein.

Key distractor distinction: Chylomicrons are the largest, most triglyceride-rich, and least dense.

89. B — ApoC-II Intermediate

Reasoning: ApoC-II activates LPL, which hydrolyzes triglycerides in chylomicrons and VLDL.

Key distractor distinction: ApoA-I activates LCAT, while ApoE mediates hepatic remnant uptake.

90. B — ApoB-100 Intermediate

Reasoning: ApoB-100 is the structural protein of VLDL, IDL, and LDL and serves as the ligand for the LDL receptor.

Key distractor distinction: ApoB-48 is required for intestinal chylomicron assembly and does not bind the LDL receptor.

91. C — Intestine forms chylomicrons with ApoB-48, LPL removes triglyceride, and ApoE mediates remnant uptake by liver Intermediate

Reasoning: Dietary long-chain lipids are packaged into ApoB-48 chylomicrons, secreted through lymphatics, acted on by LPL, and cleared as ApoE-containing remnants by the liver.

Key distractor distinction: The endogenous pathway begins with hepatic VLDL containing ApoB-100.

92. C — VLDL → IDL → LDL Intermediate

Reasoning: LPL converts VLDL to IDL, and hepatic lipase can remove more triglyceride to produce cholesterol-rich LDL.

Key distractor distinction: LDL is the final product of the VLDL cascade rather than an intermediate before IDL.

93. E — Defective microsomal triglyceride-transfer protein Step 1 style

Reasoning: Abetalipoproteinemia results from defective MTTP, preventing assembly of chylomicrons and VLDL. The result is fat malabsorption, vitamin deficiency, low lipids, and acanthocytes.

Key distractor distinction: LPL or ApoC-II deficiency causes very high triglycerides rather than low concentrations.

94. E — Lipoprotein lipase or ApoC-II deficiency Step 1 style

Reasoning: Type I hyperchylomicronemia is caused by LPL or ApoC-II deficiency. Chylomicrons accumulate, causing marked hypertriglyceridemia and pancreatitis without the typical premature atherosclerosis of high LDL.

Key distractor distinction: LDL receptor deficiency causes severe hypercholesterolemia, tendon xanthomas, and premature atherosclerosis.

95. C — LDL receptor deficiency Step 1 style

Reasoning: Familial hypercholesterolemia, or type II hyperlipoproteinemia, is caused by defective LDL receptors and produces severe LDL elevation with premature atherosclerosis, tendon xanthomas, and corneal arcus.

Key distractor distinction: ApoE deficiency causes accumulation of remnants and IDL with mixed hyperlipidemia.

96. D — ApoE Step 1 style

Reasoning: ApoE mediates hepatic uptake of chylomicron remnants and IDL. Deficiency causes type III dysbetalipoproteinemia with remnant accumulation and premature atherosclerosis.

Key distractor distinction: ApoC-II deficiency primarily blocks LPL and causes isolated severe hypertriglyceridemia.

97. B — Activation of LCAT and esterification of cholesterol on HDL In-house

Reasoning: ApoA-I activates LCAT, which esterifies free cholesterol and traps it in the HDL core during maturation.

Key distractor distinction: ApoC-II activates LPL, and ApoE mediates remnant uptake.

98. D — ApoA-I activates LCAT to esterify the cholesterol Step 1 style

Reasoning: ApoA-I activates LCAT, which converts free cholesterol to cholesteryl ester. The ester moves into the core, making HDL spherical and mature.

Key distractor distinction: CETP exchanges cholesteryl esters and triglycerides but does not perform the esterification step.

99. E — Cholesteryl esters from HDL to VLDL or IDL in exchange for triglycerides Intermediate

Reasoning: CETP transfers cholesteryl esters from HDL to VLDL and IDL while transferring triglycerides in the opposite direction.

Key distractor distinction: LCAT creates cholesteryl esters within HDL; CETP redistributes them.

100. E — Oxidized LDL Step 1 style

Reasoning: LDL entering the arterial wall becomes oxidized and is taken up by macrophage scavenger receptors. Because these receptors are not downregulated, lipid-loaded foam cells form.

Key distractor distinction: HDL participates in cholesterol removal and is generally antiatherogenic.

Urea Cycle and Nitrogen Metabolism

101. B — Pyridoxal phosphate In-house

Reasoning: Aminotransferases require pyridoxal phosphate, the active form of vitamin B6, to transfer amino groups.

Key distractor distinction: Biotin is used by carboxylases, while cobalamin is used by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase and methionine synthase.

102. B — Pyruvate and glutamate Intermediate

Reasoning: ALT converts alanine to pyruvate while converting α-ketoglutarate to glutamate. This reaction is central to the glucose-alanine cycle.

Key distractor distinction: AST produces oxaloacetate and glutamate from aspartate and α-ketoglutarate.

103. D — Glutamate dehydrogenase Intermediate

Reasoning: Glutamate dehydrogenase performs oxidative deamination of glutamate, releasing ammonia and regenerating α-ketoglutarate in liver mitochondria.

Key distractor distinction: Glutaminase releases one ammonia from glutamine but produces glutamate rather than α-ketoglutarate.

104. B — Glutamine In-house

Reasoning: Glutamine synthetase combines ammonia with glutamate to form glutamine, a nontoxic carrier that can transport two nitrogen atoms.

Key distractor distinction: Free ammonia is neurotoxic and is not the principal circulating transport form.

105. D — Alanine Intermediate

Reasoning: In the glucose-alanine cycle, muscle transfers nitrogen to pyruvate to form alanine. Liver ALT regenerates pyruvate for gluconeogenesis and transfers the nitrogen to glutamate.

Key distractor distinction: Glutamine is the major nitrogen carrier from many tissues, but alanine is the characteristic carrier from exercising muscle.

106. A — Excretion of NH4+ with generation of new bicarbonate Step 1 style

Reasoning: Renal glutaminase releases NH3, which buffers secreted H+ as NH4+ and becomes trapped in urine. This excretes acid and effectively generates new bicarbonate.

Key distractor distinction: Urea synthesis consumes bicarbonate and occurs in liver, so it would not be the kidney's corrective response.

107. A — Consumption of α-ketoglutarate to form glutamate and glutamine, plus osmotic astrocyte swelling from glutamine Step 1 style

Reasoning: Brain detoxification of ammonia consumes α-ketoglutarate and glutamate, slowing the TCA cycle and ATP production. Accumulated glutamine acts as an osmole and causes astrocyte swelling.

Key distractor distinction: Astrocytes do not solve hyperammonemia by producing urea; the urea cycle is hepatic.

108. D — Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I In-house

Reasoning: CPS I uses ammonia, CO2, and two ATP to form carbamoyl phosphate. It is the rate-limiting urea-cycle enzyme and requires N-acetylglutamate.

Key distractor distinction: OTC catalyzes the next mitochondrial step.

109. B — Citrulline Intermediate

Reasoning: OTC combines carbamoyl phosphate with ornithine to form citrulline, which is then transported to the cytoplasm.

Key distractor distinction: Argininosuccinate is formed in the cytoplasm from citrulline and aspartate.

110. A — Argininosuccinate synthetase combines citrulline with aspartate Intermediate

Reasoning: Aspartate carries the second nitrogen and is added to citrulline by argininosuccinate synthetase in an ATP-requiring cytoplasmic step.

Key distractor distinction: The first nitrogen enters as free ammonia through CPS I.

111. C — Argininosuccinate lyase Intermediate

Reasoning: Argininosuccinate lyase splits argininosuccinate into arginine and fumarate. Fumarate can enter the TCA cycle.

Key distractor distinction: Arginase produces urea and ornithine rather than fumarate.

112. E — OTC deficiency Step 1 style

Reasoning: OTC deficiency is X-linked and causes carbamoyl phosphate accumulation, which is shunted to pyrimidine synthesis and produces orotic acid. Hyperammonemia and low BUN distinguish it from UMP synthase deficiency.

Key distractor distinction: UMP synthase deficiency causes orotic aciduria with megaloblastic anemia but normal ammonia.

113. D — CPS I Step 1 style

Reasoning: CPS I deficiency prevents carbamoyl phosphate formation, producing hyperammonemia without excess carbamoyl phosphate available for orotic acid synthesis.

Key distractor distinction: OTC deficiency produces both hyperammonemia and orotic aciduria.

114. B — Argininosuccinate synthetase Step 1 style

Reasoning: Argininosuccinate synthetase deficiency causes citrullinemia because citrulline cannot combine with aspartate to form argininosuccinate.

Key distractor distinction: Arginase deficiency instead causes high arginine and often a later presentation with spasticity.

115. B — Lactulose traps intestinal ammonia as NH4+; phenylbutyrate removes nitrogen through urinary glutamine conjugation products Step 1 style

Reasoning: Lactulose acidifies the gut and traps NH3 as poorly absorbed NH4+. Phenylbutyrate binds nitrogen through glutamine-derived conjugates that are excreted in urine.

Key distractor distinction: These treatments create alternative nitrogen-disposal routes rather than directly repairing the urea-cycle enzyme defect.

Amino Acid Metabolism

116. A — Leucine, isoleucine, and valine In-house

Reasoning: Leucine, isoleucine, and valine are the branched-chain amino acids and are metabolized predominantly in muscle.

Key distractor distinction: Phenylalanine and tyrosine are aromatic amino acids addressed in separate degradation pathways.

117. C — Branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase Step 1 style

Reasoning: Maple syrup urine disease results from BCKD deficiency, causing accumulation of branched-chain amino acids and their α-ketoacids.

Key distractor distinction: Isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency affects leucine breakdown but produces a sweaty-feet odor.

118. D — Lactic acidosis and α-ketoglutaric aciduria Intermediate

Reasoning: The E3 subunit is shared by BCKD, pyruvate dehydrogenase, and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase. A defect therefore adds lactic acidosis and α-ketoglutaric aciduria.

Key distractor distinction: MCAD deficiency causes hypoketotic hypoglycemia with dicarboxylic aciduria, not maple-syrup odor.

119. B — Isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase Intermediate

Reasoning: Isovaleric acidemia is caused by isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency in leucine degradation and produces the characteristic sweaty-feet odor.

Key distractor distinction: MSUD produces a maple-syrup or burnt-sugar odor and involves all three BCAAs.

120. A — Propionyl-CoA carboxylase and biotin Step 1 style

Reasoning: Propionic acidemia is caused by propionyl-CoA carboxylase deficiency. This carboxylase requires biotin.

Key distractor distinction: Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase requires B12 and causes methylmalonic, not propionic, acidemia.

121. B — Succinyl-CoA Step 1 style

Reasoning: Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, a TCA-cycle intermediate. The enzyme requires vitamin B12.

Key distractor distinction: Malonyl-CoA is produced from acetyl-CoA by ACC during fatty acid synthesis.

122. C — Tyrosine In-house

Reasoning: Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency prevents conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine, so tyrosine must be supplied in the diet.

Key distractor distinction: Phenylalanine remains an essential amino acid but must be restricted because it accumulates.

123. D — Tetrahydrobiopterin deficiency Step 1 style

Reasoning: BH4 is required for phenylalanine hydroxylase and for neurotransmitter synthesis pathways. Its deficiency produces malignant PKU with neurotransmitter deficits.

Key distractor distinction: Typical PAH deficiency does not directly impair dopamine and serotonin synthesis through loss of the shared cofactor.

124. E — Microcephaly, intellectual disability, and congenital heart defects Intermediate

Reasoning: High maternal phenylalanine crosses the placenta and is teratogenic, causing microcephaly, developmental impairment, and congenital heart defects even when the fetus is only a carrier.

Key distractor distinction: The maternal metabolic environment, not fetal genotype alone, determines this risk.

125. D — Homogentisic acid Intermediate

Reasoning: Homogentisic acid accumulates in alkaptonuria due to homogentisic acid oxidase deficiency. Oxidation causes black urine and connective-tissue ochronosis.

Key distractor distinction: Succinylacetone is the diagnostic metabolite of tyrosinemia type I.

126. A — Tyrosinase In-house

Reasoning: Tyrosinase deficiency impairs conversion of tyrosine to DOPA and melanin, causing albinism and reduced UV protection.

Key distractor distinction: PAH deficiency can cause fair coloring but presents as PKU with high phenylalanine and neurologic disease.

127. E — Succinylacetone Step 1 style

Reasoning: Tyrosinemia type I is caused by fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase deficiency and is diagnosed by succinylacetone. It causes severe hepatic and renal toxicity and high HCC risk.

Key distractor distinction: Homogentisic acid identifies alkaptonuria, which is much more benign.

128. E — It inhibits an earlier step and prevents formation of downstream toxic metabolites Intermediate

Reasoning: Nitisinone blocks an upstream enzyme in tyrosine degradation, reducing formation of fumarylacetoacetate-derived toxic products including succinylacetone.

Key distractor distinction: It does not restore the missing terminal enzyme directly, so phenylalanine and tyrosine restriction remains important.

129. E — Cysteine Intermediate

Reasoning: Cystathionine synthase deficiency blocks the pathway from homocysteine toward cysteine, making cysteine an essential dietary amino acid.

Key distractor distinction: Methionine is restricted because it is upstream of the accumulating homocysteine.

130. A — Downward and inward lens dislocation with thromboembolism Step 1 style

Reasoning: Homocystinuria classically causes downward-inward lens dislocation, intellectual disability, and thromboembolic disease. Marfan syndrome causes upward-outward lens displacement and aortic-root pathology.

Key distractor distinction: The inheritance also differs: homocystinuria is autosomal recessive and Marfan syndrome is autosomal dominant.

131. C — Vitamin B12 Intermediate

Reasoning: Vitamin B12 is required by methionine synthase, affecting homocysteine handling, and by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, affecting methylmalonic acid.

Key distractor distinction: Vitamin B6 is required by cystathionine synthase but does not explain methylmalonic acid elevation.

132. B — Propionic acidemia blocks propionyl-CoA carboxylase; methylmalonic acidemia blocks methylmalonyl-CoA mutase Intermediate

Reasoning: Propionyl-CoA carboxylase is biotin-dependent and deficient in propionic acidemia. Methylmalonyl-CoA mutase is B12-dependent and deficient in methylmalonic acidemia.

Key distractor distinction: Both disorders can present in infancy with high anion gap metabolic acidosis.

Integrated Metabolism: Fed, Fasting, and Starvation States

133. B — Closure of ATP-sensitive K+ channels, depolarization, opening of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and Ca2+ influx Intermediate

Reasoning: Rising ATP closes ATP-sensitive K+ channels, causing membrane depolarization. Voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open, and Ca2+ influx triggers insulin-vesicle exocytosis.

Key distractor distinction: Opening K+ channels would hyperpolarize the β-cell and suppress insulin release.

134. D — Liver In-house

Reasoning: Glucagon primarily targets the liver, stimulating hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis to raise blood glucose.

Key distractor distinction: Muscle glycogen is regulated by contraction, AMP, and epinephrine and cannot release free glucose to blood.

135. D — Glucose uptake through GLUT2, glycogen synthesis, glycolysis, and VLDL production from excess carbohydrate Intermediate

Reasoning: The fed liver takes up glucose through GLUT2, stores glycogen, performs glycolysis, and converts excess carbon to fatty acids and triglycerides that can be exported in VLDL.

Key distractor distinction: Ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis are fasting-state pathways suppressed by insulin.

136. D — Increased GLUT4 translocation, uptake of lipoprotein-derived fatty acids, and triglyceride storage Intermediate

Reasoning: Insulin recruits GLUT4, supports glycerol-3-phosphate formation, promotes fatty acid uptake, and inhibits lipolysis, favoring triglyceride storage.

Key distractor distinction: Adipose tissue lacks glucose-6-phosphatase and does not release free glucose.

137. B — Increased GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, amino acid uptake, and protein synthesis Intermediate

Reasoning: Insulin promotes GLUT4-dependent glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, amino acid uptake, and protein synthesis in muscle.

Key distractor distinction: Muscle does not respond to glucagon by releasing glucose to blood and does not perform ketogenesis or the urea cycle.

138. C — Hepatic glycogenolysis In-house

Reasoning: Early fasting blood glucose is maintained mainly by hepatic glycogenolysis. Liver glycogen stores decline and are largely depleted by about 24 hours.

Key distractor distinction: Muscle glycogen is reserved for local use because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

139. E — Lactate, alanine, and glycerol Intermediate

Reasoning: Lactate from anaerobic tissues, alanine from muscle, and glycerol from adipose lipolysis are major gluconeogenic precursors.

Key distractor distinction: Acetyl-CoA and even-chain fatty acids cannot provide net glucose in the pathway described.

140. A — Cori cycle Step 1 style

Reasoning: The Cori cycle requires hepatic gluconeogenesis to convert lactate back to glucose. High insulin after a meal suppresses gluconeogenesis and reduces this recycling.

Key distractor distinction: The glucose-alanine cycle transports both carbon and nitrogen from muscle but is not the lactate-specific pathway.

141. D — Brain and red blood cells Intermediate

Reasoning: The brain cannot directly use albumin-bound fatty acids across the blood-brain barrier, and RBCs lack mitochondria for β-oxidation.

Key distractor distinction: Heart and resting skeletal muscle readily use fatty acids.

142. B — Reduced amino acid catabolism and decreased urea production Step 1 style

Reasoning: Brain ketone utilization reduces its glucose requirement, allowing less muscle protein to be degraded for gluconeogenesis. Nitrogen delivery to the urea cycle therefore declines.

Key distractor distinction: RBCs still require glucose, so gluconeogenesis does not stop completely.

143. E — No major change in blood glucose because muscle normally lacks this enzyme Intermediate

Reasoning: Normal muscle already lacks glucose-6-phosphatase and uses its glycogen locally. Therefore, the proposed absence produces no new major systemic defect.

Key distractor distinction: Liver glucose-6-phosphatase, not muscle enzyme activity, is required for release of free glucose into blood.

144. B — Anaerobic glycolysis In-house

Reasoning: RBCs lack mitochondria and rely exclusively on anaerobic glycolysis for ATP even when oxygen and alternative fuels are available.

Key distractor distinction: The pentose phosphate pathway supplies NADPH but does not directly provide ATP.

145. A — Thiamine deficiency Step 1 style

Reasoning: Type B lactic acidosis occurs without tissue hypoxia and can result from thiamine deficiency, liver disease, or drugs and toxins. Type A is caused by inadequate oxygen delivery, as in shock or infarction.

Key distractor distinction: Thiamine is required by pyruvate dehydrogenase, so deficiency diverts pyruvate toward lactate.

146. B — Reduced fatty acid oxidation and reduced ATP supply for gluconeogenesis Step 1 style

Reasoning: Blocking long-chain fatty acid entry into mitochondria prevents β-oxidation. The liver loses both acetyl-CoA for ketogenesis and ATP needed to sustain gluconeogenesis, causing hypoketotic hypoglycemia.

Key distractor distinction: The defect does not primarily impair glycolysis or increase ketone use.

147. D — Covalent modification and induction of enzyme synthesis Intermediate

Reasoning: Phosphorylation is covalent modification and acts rapidly. Hormonal changes in gene transcription alter enzyme abundance more slowly through induction or suppression.

Key distractor distinction: Allosteric regulation involves metabolite binding rather than addition of phosphate.

148. B — The liver is the principal site of glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, urea synthesis, and ketogenesis Intermediate

Reasoning: The liver is the central metabolic hub for blood-glucose maintenance, nitrogen disposal, and ketone synthesis. Failure therefore disrupts all three functions.

Key distractor distinction: Adipose tissue is the major triglyceride store, and liver cannot use the ketones it produces because it lacks thiophorase.

149. E — It excretes H+ as NH4+ while generating new bicarbonate Intermediate

Reasoning: Renal ammoniagenesis traps secreted H+ as NH4+ and generates new bicarbonate, helping buffer ketoacidosis. Reduced reliance on urea also avoids bicarbonate consumption by the urea cycle.

Key distractor distinction: The adaptation manages acid-base balance rather than converting ketones to glucose.

150. C — Increased adipose lipolysis, hepatic β-oxidation, ketogenesis, and high anion gap metabolic acidosis Step 1 style

Reasoning: Low insulin and high glucagon mobilize adipose fat, increase hepatic fatty acid oxidation, and generate excess ketone bodies, which can cause diabetic ketoacidosis.

Key distractor distinction: Fed-state insulin actions increase GLUT4 uptake and lipogenesis and suppress ketogenesis.
