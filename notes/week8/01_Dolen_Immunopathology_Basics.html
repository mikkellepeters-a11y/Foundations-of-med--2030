Official Learning Objectives

9.1 Compare IgE-mediated Type I hypersensitivity with Types II, III, and IV, and recognize clinical examples.
9.2 Relate allergy and atopy to clinical practice.

9.5 Relate therapeutic interventions for allergic, autoimmune, and inflammatory diseases to the mechanisms driving disease.

Slide 4. Faculty caveat: mechanisms overlap in real patients. For exam-style questions, identify the predominant mechanism being tested.
Faculty wording worth remembering
Diseases can use more than one mechanism simultaneously. SLE is the lecture's classic overlap example, and even the same trigger such as penicillin can cause different hypersensitivity mechanisms in different people.
Companion-only mnemonic: ACID = Anaphylactic/Allergic I, Cytotoxic II, Immune complex III, Delayed IV. Useful memory aid, but the current deck itself does not use this mnemonic.

Slides 7-13: Allergy, Atopy, and Semantic Traps

Slide 10. The deck's preferred clinical definition: 'allergy' is an IgE-dependent Type I mechanism, not a disease or diagnosis.
Need to memorize
Allergy = mechanism, not diagnosis. In this lecture's preferred US clinical usage, allergy means IgE-dependent Type I immediate hypersensitivity. A disease such as asthma or rhinitis can still be triggered by non-IgE mechanisms.
Vocabulary trap: different specialties and the lay public use the word “allergy” differently. The lecture explicitly warns you to use the term carefully in patient communication and writeups.

Atopic / “allergic” diseases

  • The classic short list in the deck is rhinitis, asthma, and atopic dermatitis.
  • These tend to occur together in individuals and families, described in the lecture as the atopic march.
  • Other diseases can involve IgE or other mechanisms: urticaria, anaphylaxis, drug/venom reactions, eosinophilic gastroenteritis.
Slide 13. A major deck-only nuance: TSLP from epithelium can activate ILC2 cells and generate Th2-like cytokine biology without IgE. Tezepelumab targets TSLP.
Deck-only high-yield connection: the TSLP → ILC2 pathway sits outside classic Gell & Coombs. This is why “allergic disease” symptoms do not automatically prove an IgE mechanism. The treatment connection appears again on slide 46 with tezepelumab.

Slides 14-25: Type I Hypersensitivity in Depth

Step 1 - Prime the effector cell

Slide 15. FcεRI is the high-affinity IgE receptor on mast cells, basophils, activated eosinophils, and some other cells. IgE waits on the receptor until allergen cross-linking triggers activation.
Mechanism chain
IgE bound to FcεRI → allergen cross-links IgE → receptor aggregation → mast-cell activation → mediator release → immediate symptoms + inflammatory recruitment.

Step 2 - Intracellular signaling and mediator release

Slide 17. The BTK/mast-cell signaling figure links receptor cross-linking to kinase signaling, calcium-dependent exocytosis, lipid mediator synthesis, and cytokine production.
What to take from the BTK figure
You do not need to turn the entire pathway into a kinase list. The conceptual output is three parallel products: granule contents (including histamine), new lipid mediators, and cytokines. The treatment slide later mentions -brutinibs, connecting therapy back to signaling.

Mast cell vs basophil

  • Both are major Type I effectors, express FcεRI, can release histamine, and can also be activated by non-IgE mechanisms.
  • Tryptase is a clinically useful marker of mast-cell activation.
  • The deck explicitly says basophils do NOT have tryptase.

Early vs late phase

Slide 22. Early phase occurs within minutes from preformed mast-cell mediators; late phase occurs hours later from the inflammatory response.
Ozzy checkpoint - click him once you can explain why a Type I late phase reaction is still not Type IV.
Classic trap: late-phase Type I happens several hours after allergen exposure, but it remains part of an IgE/mast-cell-centered Type I response. Type IV delayed hypersensitivity is T-cell mediated and classically appears 1-3 days after antigen contact.

Selected disease factoids from slides 23-25

  • Rhinitis/asthma: IgE mechanisms are common, but ILC pathways can also contribute.
  • Atopic dermatitis: lecture phrase is the “itch that rashes”; IgE may trigger itching, scratching produces rash. “Eczema” is a physical finding, not one diagnosis.
  • Chronic urticaria is very rarely IgE-dependent; autoantibody to FcεRI can drive mast-cell activation.
  • Anaphylactic vs anaphylactoid terminology: either mechanism can be mild, severe, or fatal.
Companion-only enrichment: the Word guide adds sensitization details (Th2, IL-4/IL-13 class switching, IL-5 eosinophils), skin-prick/specific-IgE testing, and IM epinephrine physiology for anaphylaxis. These are useful clinically, but they are not shown as dedicated content in the current 49-slide deck, so they are not treated as faculty slide emphasis here.

Slides 27-30: Type II - Antibody Against a Fixed Target

Slide 28. Type II cytotoxic mechanism: IgG/IgM binds a cell-surface antigen; complement can cause direct lysis or enhance phagocytosis.
Type II recognition rule
The antigen is fixed to the cell/tissue/receptor. Ask: “What exactly is the antibody binding?” If the answer is a surface antigen or receptor, Type II rises quickly.

Cytotoxic

Penicillin-induced hemolytic anemia; hemolytic disease of the newborn/Rh incompatibility.

Stimulatory / inhibitory

Graves: stimulate receptor. Autoimmune hypothyroidism: block TSH receptor. Myasthenia gravis: block ACh receptor.

Slide 29 nuance
The deck notes that stimulatory/inhibitory Type II reactions were once colloquially called “Type V.” For this course, keep them under Type II.

Slides 31-39: Type III - Immune Complex Deposition

Slide 32. Immune-complex size depends on antigen/antibody proportions. Medium complexes can become trapped in small vessels and activate complement in situ.
Type III recognition rule
Soluble antigen + antibody → circulating immune complexes → deposition → complement/inflammation.

Slide 35. The deck divides Type III into three recognizable patterns: local Arthus reaction, systemic serum sickness, and inhaled-antigen hypersensitivity pneumonitis.

Arthus

Local tissue reaction in a sensitized person with preformed IgG; local IC deposition and complement activation. Deck example: sore arm a few hours after dT booster.

Serum sickness

Large foreign antigen exposure; rash, glomerulonephritis, arthritis, fever as antibody develops, usually about a week.

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

Inhaled antigen → IgG response → IC deposition in alveoli. Deck examples: farmer’s lung, bird fancier’s lung.

Type II vs III discriminator
Type II = antibody binds a fixed target.
Type III = soluble complexes form first, then deposit elsewhere.

Slides 40-44: Type IV - T-Cell Mediated

Slide 41. The inflammatory Th1 pathway: processed antigen is presented by MHC II; effector T cells and macrophages drive the response. Haptens such as nickel can trigger it.
Timing + mechanism
Type IV = T cells, not antibody, and typically 1-3 days after antigen contact.
Slide 43. The deck broadens Type IV beyond Th1 cytokine inflammation to include cytotoxic T-cell mechanisms that directly kill target cells.
  • DTH = delayed-type hypersensitivity when the response is pathologic/detrimental.
  • CMI = cell-mediated immunity when the same kind of T-cell biology is beneficial against pathogens.
  • Other examples in the deck: transplant rejection, resistance to fungal infection, Hashimoto thyroiditis.
  • Clinical anergy = inability to mount Type IV responses; slide 44 gives sarcoidosis as an example of general anergy.

Slides 45-47: Treat the Mechanism

Slide 46. The lecture's therapy slide: broad immunosuppression, signaling inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, mediator-specific agents, and allergen immunotherapy.

Lecture's final principle
Clinically identifying the mechanism guides management. The point of learning I-IV is not just categorization; it lets you predict what part of the immune response is rational to target.
Companion-only enrichment: the Word guide additionally names methotrexate/DMARDs, anti-TNF and anti-IL-17 biologics, and describes allergen immunotherapy as promoting tolerance/IgG4. Those details are helpful but are not on the current deck's therapy slide, so memorize the deck's own treatment list first.
