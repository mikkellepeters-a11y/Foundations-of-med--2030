Overview of the Adaptive Immune System

William K. Dolen, MD • Week 6: Foundations of Immunology • Lecture 4 • Enriched Study Guide

What this lecture is really trying to teach

This lecture introduces the two defining problems of adaptive immunity: how to create enormous receptor diversity and how to prevent that diversity from turning into autoimmunity. B cells and T cells solve the first problem with receptor-gene rearrangement, then solve the second with developmental checkpoints and central tolerance.

  • Origin: both B and T cells start from bone-marrow stem cells.
  • Destination: B cells mature in bone marrow; T cells finish development in thymus.
  • Diversity: adaptive receptors are built by rearranging germline DNA rather than being inherited as fixed receptor genes.
  • Quality control: developmental checkpoints eliminate or modify cells with failed or dangerous receptors.
  • B-cell clinical anchors: ADA deficiency, RAG-related SCID, BTK/XLA.
  • T-cell clinical anchors: DiGeorge syndrome and AIRE deficiency/APS-1.

LO 5.1 - Where B and T Cells Come From

Lymphocyte: an immune cell of the B-cell, T-cell, or related lymphoid lineages. In this lecture, the main focus is B and T cells because they form the core of adaptive immunity.

Both B and T lymphocytes begin from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. Their paths diverge during maturation:

FUN PROFESSOR BIT

B cells were named after the Bursa of Fabricius in chickens, where their final developmental stages were first described. Humans do not have a bursa. It is basically a historical coincidence that B = bone marrow is still a useful human mnemonic.

Why development needs checkpoints

Receptor generation is intentionally somewhat random. That creates diversity, but it also creates two predictable problems:

Problem 1 - Broken receptor

The rearrangement may produce nonsense DNA or a nonfunctional receptor. That cell is useless and should not mature.

Problem 2 - Self-reactive receptor

The random receptor may recognize self. If such cells escape normal control, autoimmunity becomes possible.

Developmental checkpoint: a stage at which a developing lymphocyte is tested for successful receptor construction, appropriate signaling, and/or dangerous self-reactivity.
🔥 PROFESSOR WORDING

Dolen jokes about the common idea that lymphocytes are “educated” in development: there is no education. Cells that fail checkpoints modify the receptor, become inactive, or die by apoptosis. The point is selection, not learning.

Clinical correlation - ADA deficiency

ADA deficiency: autosomal-recessive adenosine deaminase deficiency causing toxic purine metabolites to accumulate and interfere with lymphocyte and NK-cell development.
  • Dolen describes a SCID-like “T- B- NK-” phenotype.
  • Treatment options listed in the lecture: ADA replacement or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).
  • This connects directly back to nucleotide metabolism: a metabolic defect can become an immune-development defect.
Need to memorize - ADA

ADA deficiency -> toxic purine metabolites -> severe lymphocyte/NK toxicity -> SCID-like T- B- NK- phenotype.

LO 5.2 - How Adaptive Receptors Are Built

B-cell receptor (BCR): membrane-bound immunoglobulin that gives a B cell its antigen specificity.
T-cell receptor (TCR): antigen receptor on a T lymphocyte that recognizes peptide presented by MHC.
V(D)J recombination: somatic rearrangement of Variable, Diversity, and Joining gene segments during lymphocyte development to create unique antigen-receptor genes.

⭐ BOARD ENRICHMENT - receptor gene architecture

Heavy-chain-like receptor chains use V + D + J segments (for example immunoglobulin heavy chain and TCR beta), while light-chain-like chains use V + J (immunoglobulin light chain and TCR alpha). The provided sources do not ask you to memorize chromosome numbers here, so this guide does not.

⚠️ V(D)J RECOMBINATION vs LATER B-CELL CHANGES

The companion handout explicitly distinguishes V(D)J recombination, which creates the initial receptor repertoire before antigen exposure, from somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination, which occur later in activated B cells. Do not use those terms interchangeably.

RAG deficiency and SCID

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID): a group of life-threatening disorders in which adaptive immune function is profoundly impaired. The companion handout gives autosomal-recessive RAG1/RAG2 deficiency as one cause.

If RAG-mediated recombination fails, developing B and T cells cannot make functional antigen receptors, so development arrests.

🔥 PROFESSOR PRIORITY ON STAGE NAMES

Dolen says detailed developmental-stage naming is not high yield for most students outside pathology/hematology-oncology, except on certain high-stakes exams. So know the sequence and checkpoints better than every morphology label.

Central tolerance in B cells

Central tolerance: mechanisms during lymphocyte development that prevent strongly self-reactive cells from entering the mature immune repertoire.

For B cells, strong self-reactivity can lead to:

  • Receptor editing: additional rearrangement, especially of light-chain genes, to change specificity.
  • Suppression / functional inactivation: the self-reactive cell is prevented from responding effectively.
  • Deletion: apoptosis removes the dangerous clone.
Need to memorize - B-cell self-reactivity

Edit it • silence it • delete it.

FUN PROFESSOR BIT

Bruton's agammaglobulinemia is named after COL Ogden Bruton, who reported the first case. Dolen also notes that modern -brutinib drugs inhibit BTK in B-cell malignancies and other conditions - a nice example of an immunodeficiency pathway becoming a drug target.

T-Cell Development in the Thymus

T cells also begin in bone marrow, but early precursors migrate to the thymus. There they rearrange TCR genes and pass through a stricter set of selection steps because T cells must recognize antigen in the context of self-MHC.

Positive selection - “Can you use our MHC?”

Positive selection: survival of developing T cells whose TCR can bind self-MHC with appropriate weak/moderate affinity. The companion handout places this in the thymic cortex.
  • If the TCR cannot recognize self-MHC at all, the cell is useless and dies.
  • If it recognizes MHC I, the cell commits toward CD8+.
  • If it recognizes MHC II, the cell commits toward CD4+.
Need to memorize - positive selection

Positive = cortex = weak self-MHC recognition survives.
MHC I -> CD8. MHC II -> CD4.

Negative selection - “Do you love self too much?”

Negative selection: deletion of developing T cells that bind self-peptide/MHC too strongly, reducing escape of dangerous autoreactive clones. The companion handout places this mainly in the thymic medulla.
Cropped from lecture page 18. The useful concept is affinity-based selection. For exam logic, use the companion-handout framework: no self-MHC recognition fails positive selection; strong self-reactivity fails negative selection; appropriate low/intermediate recognition survives.
Need to memorize - negative selection

Negative = medulla = strong self-reactivity dies by apoptosis.

MEMORY HOOK

Positive asks “Can you recognize our ID badge?”
Negative asks “Are you dangerously obsessed with us?”

AIRE: building a thymic “library” of self

AIRE (Autoimmune Regulator): transcriptional regulator that promotes expression of tissue-specific self antigens in the thymus, helping developing T cells encounter self proteins that normally live in distant organs.

The handout gives examples such as insulin and thyroglobulin. The logic is simple: if developing T cells never see peripheral self antigens during selection, autoreactive cells could escape simply because the thymus never tested them.

Need to memorize - AIRE

AIRE makes the thymus display peripheral self antigens so dangerous self-reactive T cells can be deleted.

Clinical Correlations of T-Cell Development

DiGeorge syndrome

DiGeorge syndrome: developmental disorder classically caused by a 22q11.2 deletion, producing variable thymic, parathyroid, cardiac, craniofacial, and palate abnormalities.
Need to memorize - CATCH-22

Cardiac defects • Abnormal facies • Thymic hypoplasia • Cleft palate • Hypocalcemia / hypoparathyroidism • chromosome 22q11.2.

  • Complete DiGeorge: essentially absent functional thymus -> profound T-cell deficiency.
  • Partial DiGeorge: some thymic tissue persists, so T-cell development is reduced rather than absent.
  • Severe parathyroid involvement -> neonatal hypocalcemia, potentially seizures/tetany.
  • The lecture notes that FISH can be used to detect microdeletions.
COMPANION-HANDOUT VIGNETTE CLUE

Absent thymic shadow + hypocalcemic seizure + dysmorphic features points to DiGeorge. The handout emphasizes susceptibility to viral and fungal infections because of T-cell deficiency.

APS-1 / APECED

APS-1 / APECED: autoimmune polyendocrinopathy syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in AIRE, leading to defective thymic negative selection and escape of autoreactive T cells.
Need to memorize - APS-1 triad

Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis + hypoparathyroidism + adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease).

HANDOUT MECHANISM

The companion handout links chronic candidiasis in APS-1 to autoimmune targeting of the IL-17/Th17 axis. The main lecture simply says Th17 cytokines are involved.

Common Exam Traps

TrapCorrect logic
“B cells come from bone marrow; T cells come from thymus.”Both originate from bone-marrow stem cells. T cells merely finish development in thymus.
“B stands for bone marrow.”Useful mnemonic, but historically B = Bursa of Fabricius.
“V(D)J recombination happens after infection to improve antibodies.”No. It builds the initial receptor repertoire during development.
“Somatic hypermutation creates the initial B/T receptor repertoire.”No. The handout distinguishes it from developmental V(D)J recombination.
“XLA has no T cells.”No. BTK primarily blocks B-cell maturation; T-cell counts are preserved.
“IgM + IgD means class switching already occurred.”No. Dolen specifically states naive mature B cells can coexpress IgM and IgD before later class switching.
“Positive selection eliminates cells that bind self too strongly.”No. Positive selection asks whether a T cell can use self-MHC. Strong self-reactivity is the negative-selection problem.
“Negative selection occurs mainly in cortex.”The companion handout places negative selection in medulla and positive selection in cortex.
“AIRE deficiency causes failure to form a thymus.”No. AIRE affects the self-antigen library used for negative selection. DiGeorge affects thymic development.
“DiGeorge is mainly an antibody deficiency.”The defining immune problem is impaired T-cell maturation from thymic hypoplasia/aplasia.

30-second end-of-lecture summary

B and T cells both arise from bone-marrow stem cells, but B cells finish development in bone marrow while T-cell precursors migrate to thymus. Adaptive immunity generates receptor diversity by RAG-mediated V(D)J recombination, then uses developmental checkpoints to eliminate failed or dangerous clones. B cells rearrange heavy chain first, pass a BTK-dependent pre-B checkpoint, then rearrange light chain; immature B cells display IgM and mature naive B cells display IgM plus IgD. T cells progress from double-negative to double-positive to single-positive and undergo positive selection for useful self-MHC recognition and negative selection against excessive self-reactivity. Failures of these processes produce memorable diseases: RAG defects cause SCID, BTK causes XLA, 22q11.2 deletions impair thymic development in DiGeorge syndrome, and AIRE defects impair negative selection in APS-1/APECED.
