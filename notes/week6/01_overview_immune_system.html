Overview of the Immune System

William K. Dolen, MD • Week 6: Foundations of Immunology • Lecture 1 • Enriched Study Guide

What this lecture is really trying to give you

This is the map of immunology. Before learning individual cytokines, complement pathways, hypersensitivity types, immunodeficiencies, or lymphocyte signaling, you need to know what problem the immune system is solving and how its two major branches cooperate.

  • The immune system behaves like a sensory-response network: detect danger -> choose an appropriate response -> eliminate the threat -> shut the response down.
  • Disease occurs when the response is too weak, misdirected, excessive, or fails to resolve.
  • Innate immunity is fast, preprogrammed, pattern-based, and lacks classic antigen-specific memory.
  • Adaptive immunity uses rearranged antigen receptors, is highly specific, and creates immunologic memory.
  • The two systems are not separate armies. Innate responses often initiate and shape adaptive responses, and adaptive effectors can then recruit innate mechanisms.

He also states that his exam questions are based on principles contained in the MCG bricks and lectures, and the lecture objectives are keyed to the USMLE content outline.

LO 1.1 - What Is the Immune System For?

Immunity: the lecture opens with the historical idea of protection after exposure. The term comes from Latin immunus, and Dolen uses Thucydides' observation during the Plague of Athens to introduce the idea that prior exposure can protect against a later encounter.
FUN PROFESSOR BIT

Thucydides observed that the plague generally did not strike the same person a second time, or at least not fatally. That is an ancient clinical observation of what we would now connect to immunologic memory.

The immune system as a "sense organ"

Dolen's framing is unusually useful: think of the immune system as a sense organ that detects and responds to danger signals. This immediately gives you a four-step mental model:

Step 1
DetectRecognize a danger-associated signal.
→
Step 2
RespondRecruit cells/molecules suited to the threat.
→
Step 3
EliminateRemove pathogen, damaged cell, or abnormal cell.
→
Step 4
ResolveTurn inflammation off, repair tissue, preserve memory.

Three core jobs - supplemental handout framing

JobMeaningFailure mode
DefenseProtect against pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites.Immunodeficiency -> recurrent, severe, or unusual infections.
HomeostasisClear old, dead, or damaged cells and maintain tissue health.Accumulation of damage/debris or abnormal inflammatory consequences.
SurveillanceMonitor for abnormal host cells, including malignant cells.Failure can permit tumor cells to persist and expand.
Need to memorize - LO 1.1

Defense - Homeostasis - Surveillance. Then connect the two major disease directions: too little response = immunodeficiency; inappropriate/excess response = immunopathology such as allergy or autoimmune disease.

How immune responses cause disease

ProblemMechanismExamples in the provided sources
Too little responseFailure to mount an effective immune response.Common variable immunodeficiency, HIV-1 disease; handout also cites SCID.
Response against harmless materialImmune activation despite no dangerous pathogen.Allergy; bee-venom anaphylaxis in the handout practice set.
Response against selfAdaptive immunity targets normal self antigens, or cross-reacts with self after infection.Autoimmune disease; molecular mimicry/cross-reactivity.
Failure to shut downContinued inflammatory/effector activity after threat control.Tissue damage; the lecture's "what goes up must come down" principle.
Autoimmunity: an adaptive immune response directed against self. The lecture gives two routes: direct recognition of self antigens, or an initially appropriate anti-microbial response that also recognizes self through molecular mimicry/cross-reactivity.
Molecular mimicry / cross-reactivity: an immune receptor generated against one antigen also binds a structurally similar self antigen.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

Immunodeficiency is failure of immune protection. Autoimmunity is immune attack on self. Allergy is an inappropriate immune response to a generally harmless external antigen. These are all immune dysfunction, but the direction of failure is different.

LO 1.2 - How the Immune System Detects Danger

🔥 PROFESSOR EMPHASIS

Dolen explicitly says the "danger signal" concept has largely replaced a simple self-versus-non-self model. The reason: the immune system must tolerate many non-self commensals, attack some altered self cells such as cancer, and sometimes mistakenly attack normal self.

Why "self vs non-self" is too simple

SituationWhy the old model fails
Tumor immunosurveillanceThe target is derived from self, but may still need to be eliminated.
AutoimmunityNormal self can be misidentified as dangerous.
MicrobiomeMany non-self organisms are tolerated and may contribute to health.
Pathogen: a microorganism capable of causing disease.
Commensal organism: a microorganism that lives in or on the body without normally causing disease; many are part of the normal microbiome.
Microbiome: the community of microorganisms living in and on the body. The lecture emphasizes that immunity must control pathogens without unnecessarily damaging beneficial commensals.

PRRs, PAMPs, and DAMPs

Pattern-recognition receptor (PRR): a germline-encoded receptor used by innate immunity to recognize recurring molecular patterns associated with danger.
PAMP: pathogen-associated molecular pattern - a recurring microbial molecular feature recognized by innate PRRs.
DAMP: damage-associated molecular pattern - a danger-associated molecular signal arising from damaged host tissue or other injurious material.
Need to memorize - danger sensing

PRR recognizes PAMP/DAMP. PRRs are germline encoded and belong to innate immunity. The supplemental handout gives classic PAMP examples: LPS, peptidoglycan, flagellin, and identifies Toll-like receptors (TLRs) as a major PRR family.

SUPPLEMENTAL HANDOUT HIGH YIELD

The handout specifically uses LPS -> TLR4 as a practice-question example. It also frames DAMP-driven inflammation without infection as sterile inflammation.

Tolerance and immune privilege

Immune tolerance: the lecture uses this term broadly for situations in which antigens are present without a damaging immune response. It notes that tolerance can be beneficial for the microbiome, while some pathogens exploit mechanisms that avoid immune activation.
PRECISION NOTE

For future immunology lectures, it is useful to keep the broader textbook meaning in mind: immune tolerance is a state of specific nonresponsiveness to an antigen. Dolen's slide is using the idea functionally to emphasize that "not responding" can be either protective or exploitable.

Immune privilege: local suppressive mechanisms that reduce inflammatory immune responses in tissues where inflammation could cause disproportionate harm.
Need to memorize - immune privilege examples from lecture

Brain, parts of the eye, pregnant uterus, testicles.

MEMORY HOOK

Danger-signal thinking asks: "Should this be here, and is it harmful?" rather than simply "Is it self?"

🩺 LECTURE CLINICAL CORRELATION - DiGeorge syndrome

The lecture connects 22q11.2 deletion with abnormal embryologic development and thymic absence/hypoplasia. Because T cells develop in the thymus, severe thymic absence causes major T-cell immune deficiency. This clinical vignette is really testing the organ-cell relationship.

🔥 PROFESSOR QUESTION CLUE

Dolen's short in-class question asks what is unique to adaptive immunity. The key answer is receptors produced by rearrangement of germline genes. Pattern recognition, danger sensing, and cell-associated receptors can belong to innate immunity; antigen-receptor gene rearrangement is the adaptive signature.

LO 1.4 - Innate vs Adaptive Immunity

FeatureInnateAdaptive

GeneticsReceptors encoded directly in germline DNA.Specificity generated by gene rearrangement/recombination; lecture summary also mentions hypermutation.

Need to memorize - innate vs adaptive

Innate = FAST / FIXED / PATTERNS / NO classic MEMORY.
Adaptive = SLOWER FIRST TIME / SPECIFIC / REARRANGED RECEPTORS / MEMORY.

Humoral immunity: adaptive protection mediated by B cells and antibody, with T-cell help emphasized in the lecture.
Cell-mediated immunity: adaptive immunity mediated by T cells, with cytotoxic T cells as the lecture's major example.

SUPPLEMENTAL HANDOUT - DENDRITIC CELL ANCHOR

The handout repeatedly calls dendritic cells the key bridge between innate and adaptive immunity because they carry antigen from a peripheral site to a lymph node and are especially important for activating naive T cells.

CD4 vs CD8 - enough for this overview

CellProvided-source roleProvided-source antigen-presentation clue
CD4+ helper T cellCoordinates/"helps" activation of other immune cells.Handout explanation pairs CD4 with antigen presented on MHC II by professional APCs.
CD8+ cytotoxic T cellDirectly kills infected host cells and tumor cells.Handout explanation pairs CD8 with viral/endogenous peptides on MHC I.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP

NK cell and CD8 T cell can both kill abnormal host cells, but they are not interchangeable. The handout practice explanation emphasizes that a viral peptide presented on MHC I specifically points to CD8 cytotoxic T cells; NK cells are innate and do not require the same antigen-specific recognition.

Inflammation: the innate response you can see

Inflammation: a coordinated response that recruits cells and soluble mediators to injured or infected tissue.
Need to memorize - five cardinal signs

Rubor = redness • Tumor = swelling • Calor = heat • Dolor = pain • Functio laesa = loss of function.

SUPPLEMENTAL HANDOUT MECHANISM

In its bacterial-cut walkthrough, the handout links PAMP recognition to macrophage release of TNF-alpha, IL-1, and IL-6, causing vasodilation and vascular leak, followed by neutrophil recruitment/chemotaxis. Treat these cytokines as useful orientation points here; later lectures will give them more detailed roles.

Chemotaxis: directed movement of cells along a chemical gradient toward a signal source.
Opsonization: coating a target so phagocytes can bind and ingest it more efficiently. The handout highlights antibody/Fc-receptor interactions and C3b as two opsonization routes.

LO 1.5 - What Happens When the Response Subsides?

🔥 PROFESSOR PHRASE

"What goes up must come down or the immune system will run amok." Resolution is not passive. The body must actively reduce inflammatory signaling, remove spent cells/debris, repair tissue, and preserve the useful memory of the encounter.

Local outcomes while the response is active

Pus: in acute infection, accumulated dead neutrophils together with dead tissue and pathogens.
Abscess: a more chronic localized infection that becomes physically walled off.
Sepsis: in the lecture's overview, a disseminated/systemic infection in which there is no effective localized barrier and the immune response becomes widespread. Later courses will define sepsis more formally.

Resolution as a sequence

1
Danger fallsPathogen/damage signals diminish.
→
2
Inflammation contractsCytokine production falls; chemotaxis slows.
→
3
CleanupMacrophages ingest dead pathogens and tissue.
→
4
RepairMacrophages signal local and wound-healing cells.
→
5
Effector contractionMany plasma cells and pro-inflammatory T cells die by apoptosis; cytotoxic activity decreases.
→
6
Memory remainsLong-lived plasma/memory B and T cells preserve future responsiveness.
Apoptosis: programmed cell death. In resolution, apoptosis removes many activated effector cells once their job is finished.
Immunologic memory: persistence of antigen-experienced lymphocyte populations that support a faster/more effective response on subsequent exposure.
Need to memorize - resolution

Stop signal -> less cytokine/chemotaxis -> macrophage cleanup -> repair -> effector apoptosis/downregulation -> memory/long-lived cells remain.

SUPPLEMENTAL HANDOUT ADDITION

The handout explicitly adds regulatory T cells (Tregs) as active suppressors during resolution and states that memory T and memory B cells remain after most effector cells die. Dolen's slides emphasize the same contraction/long-lived-cell concept but describe it using plasma-cell survival and pro- versus anti-inflammatory T-cell shifts.

The companion handout provides a useful infection-to-resolution storyline. Use it as a mechanism chain, not as a list of disconnected facts:

FUN MEMORY FRAME

Think of the immune response as alarm -> dispatch -> identification -> specialized reinforcements -> cleanup -> case file. The "case file" is memory.

Clinical Correlations Dolen Wants You to Recognize

DiGeorge syndrome

22q11.2 deletion -> thymic developmental abnormality -> impaired T-cell development. The important concept for this lecture is anatomy-to-cell-function.

Reticular dysgenesis

A severe combined immunodeficiency affecting both branches: the lecture lists agranulocytosis + lymphopenia + sensorineural deafness, autosomal-recessive inheritance, and HSCT treatment.

Autoimmunity

Adaptive response directed to self, either directly or through cross-reactivity/molecular mimicry.

Anaphylaxis - supplemental handout

The practice set uses rapid severe response after re-exposure to illustrate adaptive memory plus pre-existing IgE on mast cells.

Need to memorize - likely vignette anchors
  • No/poor thymus -> T-cell problem.
  • Agranulocytosis + lymphopenia -> both innate and adaptive involvement.
  • LPS/PAMP recognition -> innate PRR/TLR.
  • Naive T-cell activation in lymph node -> dendritic cell.
  • Viral peptide on MHC I -> CD8 T cell in the handout's practice logic.
  • Rapid stronger re-exposure response -> immunologic memory.

What to Memorize vs What to Understand

Tier 1 - memorize cold

PRR/PAMP/DAMP; SALT/MALT/GALT/BALT; primary vs secondary lymphoid organs; B-marrow/T-thymus; innate vs adaptive comparison; innate/adaptive cell lists; cardinal inflammation signs; resolution sequence.

Tier 2 - understand deeply

Danger-signal model; why self/non-self is insufficient; how dendritic cells bridge the systems; how innate and adaptive responses feed back into each other; why immune responses must contract.

Tier 3 - recognize clinically

DiGeorge, reticular dysgenesis, anaphylaxis as memory, autoimmune cross-reactivity, pus vs abscess vs systemic infection/sepsis.

Tier 4 - lower priority today

Exact counts of known PRR genes/CD markers, all cytokine/CD marker names, advanced immunology texts, and details Dolen explicitly says will recur later.

🔥 DO NOT GET LOST IN THE WEEDS

The lecture deliberately labels CD markers and cytokines as bonus/reference material. Dolen says you only need to know some of them and that the high-yield ones will recur repeatedly. Do not turn this overview into a 100-cytokine memorization session.

Common Exam Traps

TrapCorrect way to think
"Non-self always triggers immunity."No. The microbiome is non-self and usually tolerated; danger-signal context matters.
"Innate and adaptive are separate pathways."No. They cooperate continuously; dendritic cells are a major bridge.
"Adaptive receptors are pre-made in the germline."No. Specific adaptive receptors are generated by rearrangement of germline DNA.
"Fast killing of an infected cell must be CD8."Not automatically. NK cells can also kill abnormal cells. Antigen-specific MHC I recognition points toward CD8.
"A response ending means the immune system stops doing anything."No. Resolution includes active suppression, cleanup, tissue repair, effector contraction, and memory formation.
"Tolerance is always pathologic."No. Tolerance is essential for avoiding damaging responses to appropriate antigens/commensals, although pathogens can exploit nonresponsiveness.
"All plasma cells disappear after infection."No. Most contract, but the lecture describes a population receiving survival signals and persisting long term.

30-second end-of-lecture summary

The immune system is a danger-sensing network that must recognize the right threats, respond with the right intensity, and shut itself down when the job is done. Innate immunity detects shared PAMP/DAMP patterns through germline-encoded PRRs and responds rapidly through phagocytosis, inflammation, cytotoxicity, cytokines, and complement. Adaptive immunity generates highly specific receptors by gene rearrangement, expands more slowly on first exposure, and creates memory through T- and B-cell responses. Dendritic cells help convert innate detection into adaptive activation. After clearance, inflammatory signaling contracts, macrophages clean and repair tissue, most effectors disappear, and memory/long-lived cells remain.
