Antibody Function

William K. Dolen, MD • Week 6: Foundations of Immunology • Lecture 6 • Enriched Study Guide

What this lecture is really trying to teach

Lecture 5 taught you how antibodies are built. Lecture 6 asks the more important clinical question: once an antibody binds antigen, what can it actually do? The answer depends heavily on the antibody's Fc region and isotype.

  • Neutralize: physically prevent microbes or toxins from binding their targets.
  • Opsonize: coat a target so phagocytes can grab it through Fc receptors.
  • Direct cytotoxicity: use IgG to recruit NK cells for ADCC.
  • Activate complement: connect adaptive recognition to complement-mediated opsonization, inflammation, immune-complex clearance, and lysis.
  • Sensitize mast cells: IgE arms Fc epsilon receptors so later antigen exposure can trigger rapid mediator release.
  • Transport matters too: IgG crosses placenta via FcRn; dimeric IgA crosses epithelium to protect mucosa.
Need to memorize - master list
  1. Five major antibody functions: neutralization, opsonization, ADCC, complement activation, mast-cell/basophil sensitization.
  2. Neutralization: IgG in tissues/blood; IgA at mucosal surfaces.
  3. Opsonization: IgG, especially IgG1 and IgG3.
  4. ADCC: IgG1/IgG3 on target + NK-cell Fc gamma RIII (CD16) -> apoptosis.
  5. Mast-cell sensitization: IgE on high-affinity Fc epsilon RI; allergen cross-linking activates mast cell.
  6. Complement activation: IgM and IgG, but not IgG4; IgM is especially potent.
  7. Placental transport: IgG through FcRn.
  8. FcRn also protects IgG from endosomal degradation; IgG half-life is about 21 days.
  9. Term infant IgG should be equal to or higher than maternal IgG.
  10. Epithelial transport: dimeric IgA; mucosal defense.
  11. IgM: pentamer + J chain, first primary-response antibody, strong complement activator.
  12. IgA: monomer in serum, dimer in mucosa; dimer uses J chain.
  13. IgG: most abundant serum isotype; neutralization, opsonization, complement, placenta.
  14. IgE: mast cells/basophils, allergy/anaphylaxis, parasite defense, eosinophil activation.
  15. IgD: B-cell co-receptor; serum function unclear in the lecture.
  16. Immune-complex cleanup: C3b coats complexes -> CR1 on RBCs -> spleen/liver phagocyte removal.
  17. Serum tryptase: useful marker of mast-cell activation in anaphylaxis/mastocytosis.

LO 6.1 - The Five Major Functions of Antibody

Cropped from Dolen lecture page 3. This is the whole lecture in one picture: antibody can neutralize directly, recruit phagocytes through Fc receptors, recruit NK cells for ADCC, or activate complement to promote opsonization, inflammation, and lysis.
Need to memorize - the five

Neutralize • Opsonize • ADCC • Complement • Mast cells.
Everything else in the lecture is a mechanism, clinical example, or isotype assignment underneath those five functions.

1. Neutralization - Block the Interaction

Neutralization: antibody binds a pathogen or toxin in a way that prevents it from binding to, entering, or damaging its target host cell.

Neutralization is the most direct antibody function because the antibody itself can stop the threat without requiring a phagocyte, NK cell, or complement protein to finish the job.

Need to memorize - neutralization

CLINICAL EXAMPLE - toxin neutralization

Dolen specifically names tetanus and diphtheria toxins. The companion handout adds the treatment concept of pre-formed antitoxin antibodies: passive antibody can immediately bind circulating toxin and prevent additional cell injury.

BOARD ENRICHMENT - passive vs active immunity

Passive antibody gives immediate protection but does not create the patient's own memory response. Active immunization takes time but creates immunologic memory. A toxin vignette can therefore ask whether you need immediate neutralization, long-term prevention, or both.

EXAM TRAP

Neutralization is not the same as killing. If an antibody merely blocks viral entry or toxin-receptor binding, that is neutralization even if the bound material is later cleared by another mechanism.

2. Opsonization - Put a Handle on the Target

Opsonization: coating a target with molecules that make it easier for phagocytes to recognize, bind, ingest, and destroy it.
PROFESSOR MEMORY BIT

Dolen labels opsonization as "steak sauce": the target was already there, but coating it makes it much more attractive to the cell that is going to eat it.

Fc receptor: receptor on an immune cell that binds the constant (Fc) portion of an antibody. Fc receptors are class-specific and connect antibody binding to cellular effector functions.
PROFESSOR PRIORITY

Dolen shows Fc gamma receptor nomenclature such as Fc gamma RI and Fc gamma RII but explicitly says you do not need structural detail. Know the functional concept: the Fc receptor tells the immune cell what kind of antibody is coating the target and what to do next.

COMPANION-HANDOUT CLINICAL LINK - asplenia

Asplenic patients have impaired clearance of opsonized encapsulated bacteria, especially Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria meningitidis. The spleen is a major site where macrophages remove these antibody/C3b-coated organisms from blood.

BOARD ENRICHMENT - why capsules matter

Polysaccharide capsules make direct phagocyte attachment difficult. Opsonization solves that problem by giving the phagocyte a recognizable Fc or complement-receptor handle.

CLINICAL / PHARMACOLOGY CONNECTION

The companion handout uses monoclonal-antibody cancer therapy to illustrate ADCC: an antibody binds a tumor-cell surface antigen, and NK cells recognize the exposed Fc region. Examples discussed in the handout include rituximab and trastuzumab logic.

COMMONLY CONFUSED

Opsonization typically ends with phagocytosis by neutrophils/macrophages. ADCC ends with cytotoxic killing by an NK cell. Both can start with IgG coating the target, so the identity of the effector cell matters.

4. Complement - Antibody Calls in a Protein Cascade

Dolen's antibody-function lecture revisits complement from the adaptive side. Antibody can trigger the classical pathway, and complement then contributes to opsonization, inflammation, lysis, and immune-complex clearance.

COMPANION-HANDOUT MECHANISM

The handout adds the classical-pathway initiation detail: C1q binds nearby Fc regions on antigen-bound antibody. IgM is especially efficient because its pentameric structure can provide the needed Fc geometry with one bound IgM molecule; IgG usually requires multiple nearby molecules.

Need to memorize - complement activation by antibody

IgM + IgG activate complement; IgG4 does not.
IgM is a particularly potent activator.
Handout mnemonic: "GM makes classics."

SLE CONNECTION

Dolen emphasizes two opposite ways complement and lupus can intersect. In active SLE, very large immune-complex burden can consume complement faster than it is replaced. Conversely, inherited deficiencies of some early complement components impair normal immune-complex clearance and can produce lupus-like disease.

EXAM TRAP

Complement opsonization with C3b is an innate function and does not require antibody. But antibody-triggered classical complement activation is one way adaptive immunity recruits that innate effector system.

5. IgE, Fc epsilon RI, and Mast Cells

Mast-cell sensitization: binding of IgE to high-affinity Fc epsilon RI on mast cells/basophils, leaving the cell armed for rapid activation when a later exposure cross-links adjacent IgE molecules.

Fc epsilon RII: lower-affinity IgE receptor found on eosinophils, platelets, and dendritic cells in Dolen's lecture; associated with receptor-mediated endocytosis.
CLINICAL EXAMPLE - anaphylaxis

On re-exposure to an allergen such as bee venom or peanut protein, cross-linking of pre-existing mast-cell-bound IgE can rapidly cause urticaria, wheeze, edema, hypotension, and other manifestations of Type I hypersensitivity.

TRYPTASE

Dolen highlights elevated serum tryptase as a useful biomarker of mast-cell activation in conditions such as anaphylaxis and mastocytosis.

LECTURE CONNECTION - BTK shows up again

BTK is not only a B-cell-development protein. Dolen notes that it also participates in mast-cell activation, and he mentions -brutinib BTK inhibitors in chronic spontaneous urticaria and ongoing mast-cell-related research.

IMPORTANT NUANCE

Dolen explicitly notes that mast cells can also be activated by IgE-independent mechanisms. Therefore, "mast-cell activation" does not automatically mean "allergy," even though IgE/Fc epsilon RI is the high-yield allergic pathway.

Special Transport Functions - FcRn and IgA

FcRn: keeps IgG alive and moves it across placenta

FcRn (neonatal Fc receptor / Brambell receptor): Fc receptor that protects IgG from endosomal degradation and actively transports maternal IgG across the placenta.
Need to memorize - FcRn

FcRn = IgG rescue + placental transport.
Defective FcRn -> shortened IgG serum half-life.
Term newborn IgG should be equal to or higher than maternal IgG.

MECHANISM ENRICHMENT

FcRn binds internalized IgG in acidic endosomes and redirects it away from lysosomal destruction, then releases it after recycling to the cell surface. This explains why IgG has a long serum half-life and why FcRn is a major pharmacologic target in diseases driven by pathogenic IgG.

Transient hypogammaglobulinemia of infancy

Cropped from Dolen lecture page 12. Maternal IgG falls after birth while infant IgG production rises. If the infant response matures slowly, total IgG can dip transiently before recovering.
Transient hypogammaglobulinemia of infancy: temporary low total IgG caused by the normal decline in maternally acquired antibody plus delayed maturation of the infant's own antibody production.
Need to memorize - infant IgG

IgG half-life about 21 days. A physiologic IgG nadir occurs at a few months of age; Dolen notes that transient hypogammaglobulinemia resolves with time.

Dimeric IgA at mucosal surfaces

Need to memorize - IgA

Serum IgA = monomer.
Mucosal IgA = dimer + J chain.
Dimeric IgA protects respiratory, GI/GU, ocular, and mammary mucosal surfaces.

COMPANION-HANDOUT DETAIL

The handout adds that dimeric IgA binds the poly-Ig receptor for epithelial transcytosis. Part of that receptor remains attached as the secretory component, helping protect IgA from enzymatic degradation in mucosal secretions.

Common Exam Traps

TrapCorrect logic
"Antibody bound the pathogen, so it must be opsonization."If the antibody simply blocks entry or toxin binding, it is neutralization. Opsonization means tagging for another cell to ingest.
"ADCC means CD8 T-cell killing."In this lecture, ADCC means antibody-coated target + NK-cell Fc gamma RIII/CD16.
"All IgG subclasses activate complement equally."Dolen's summary explicitly says IgG activates complement but not IgG4.
"IgA crosses the placenta."No. IgG crosses placenta via FcRn. IgA is the mucosal/epithelial-transport antibody.
"Breast-milk IgA is the main systemic antibody protecting the newborn."Breast-milk IgA protects mucosal surfaces; systemic passive protection comes primarily from placentally transferred maternal IgG.
"Any mast-cell activation proves IgE allergy."Dolen says mast cells also have IgE-independent activation mechanisms.
"Low complement in lupus always means inherited complement deficiency."Active immune-complex disease can consume complement. Separately, some inherited early-component deficiencies can predispose to lupus-like disease.
"C3b immune-complex binding means RBCs phagocytose the complexes."RBCs act as transport vehicles via CR1; spleen/liver phagocytes remove the complexes.

Five antibody functions; isotype-function table; IgG1/3 opsonization + ADCC; CD16; Fc epsilon RI; FcRn; IgM/IgG complement; IgG4 exception; placental IgG; dimeric IgA; tryptase.

Tier 2 - understand deeply

Why Fab alone can neutralize but Fc recruits effector mechanisms; how Fc receptors connect adaptive recognition to innate cells; why immune-complex clearance protects small vessels/kidneys.

Tier 3 - recognize clinically

Asplenia/encapsulated bacteria; anaphylaxis; SLE complement consumption; early-complement lupus-like disease; terminal complement/Neisseria; transient hypogammaglobulinemia.

Tier 4 - lower priority

Detailed Fc-gamma-receptor structural subunits, every Fc receptor isoform, fine IgA1 vs IgA2 functional differences, and advanced mast-cell signaling architecture.

30-second end-of-lecture summary

Antibodies do much more than bind antigen. They can directly neutralize microbes and toxins, opsonize targets for phagocytes, recruit NK cells for ADCC, activate complement, and arm mast cells through IgE/Fc epsilon RI. The Fc region and isotype determine which downstream mechanism is recruited. IgG and IgA are the major neutralizers; IgG1/IgG3 are strong opsonizing and ADCC antibodies; IgM and most IgG can activate complement; IgE drives mast-cell sensitization; IgG crosses placenta through FcRn; and dimeric IgA protects mucosal surfaces. Complement also clears immune complexes through C3b and RBC CR1, explaining important links to SLE. If you can match isotype -> receptor -> effector cell/pathway -> clinical vignette, you understand the lecture.
