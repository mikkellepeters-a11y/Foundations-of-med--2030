What this SAL session is really trying to teach

This session is less about memorizing isolated radiology facts and more about building a safe, repeatable way to orient yourself to an image and choose the right modality language. The highest-yield skill is recognizing what type of study you are looking at, which side of the patient you are seeing, what “bright” and “dark” mean in that modality, and what each modality is good or bad at showing.

  • Orientation first: know right vs left before interpreting pathology.
  • Modality language matters: radiopaque/radiolucent for x-ray, attenuation/Hounsfield units for CT, echogenicity for ultrasound, signal for MRI.
  • Physics drives appearance: each modality creates contrast differently.
  • Risk/benefit matters: ionizing radiation, magnetic-field hazards, cost, speed, and contrast risks influence modality choice.
  • Quiz prep: orientation, density/signal vocabulary, modality comparisons, HU anchors, contrast basics, and obvious modality-selection questions are the most likely targets.
Need to memorize - master list
  1. Axial CT/MRI: view as if standing at the patient's feet -> patient's right appears on image left.
  2. Coronal: face-to-face orientation -> patient's right appears on image left.
  3. Sagittal: side view.
  4. X-ray: ionizing radiation, low cost, fast, excellent for chest and bone.
  5. X-ray brightness order: air/gas darkest -> fat -> fluid/soft tissue -> bone -> metal brightest.
  6. Radiopaque/opacified = bright. Radiolucent/lucency = dark.
  7. Attenuation: how much an x-ray beam is blocked as it passes through tissue.
  8. CT: ionizing x-rays + cross-sectional reconstruction; excellent density discrimination; higher radiation than plain radiography.
  9. HU anchors: air ~ -1000, fat ~ -100, water 0, soft tissue tens of HU, bone ~ +1000.
  10. Ultrasound: reflected sound waves; no ionizing radiation.
  11. Hyperechoic = bright. Hypoechoic = darker. Anechoic = black/no reflected echoes.
  12. MRI: strong magnetic field + radiofrequency signals; no ionizing radiation; slower and expensive.
  13. MRI brightness is sequence-dependent. Do not call MRI structures “radiopaque” or “radiolucent.”
  14. Nuclear medicine: radiopharmaceutical emits signal from within patient; primarily functional rather than high-resolution structural imaging.
  15. PA vs AP CXR: name direction of x-ray beam; know which side faces detector.
  16. Plain x-ray is 2D: usually need at least two views to localize a 3D structure.
  17. ABCDE-FGH CXR check: Airway, Bones, Cardiac, Diaphragm, Effusions, Fields, Gastric bubble, Hardware.
  18. Contrast: iodine/barium for x-ray/CT; gadolinium commonly for MRI; purpose is to increase conspicuity of vessels/tissues/pathology.
  19. MRI safety: magnet is always on; screen implants/metal for MRI compatibility.

Official Learning Objectives

SOURCE-DERIVED OBJECTIVES
  • Compare and contrast x-ray, MRI, CT, ultrasound, and nuclear medicine, including principles, image acquisition, benefits/limitations, ionizing radiation, cost, risk, and indications.
  • Define lucency, opacity, density, attenuation, and signal.
  • Describe how air, soft tissue/fluid, vasculature, fat, bone, and metal appear across x-ray, CT, and MRI.
  • Use correct conventions for reading radiographs and axial/coronal/sagittal CT/MR images and correctly distinguish patient right from left.
  • Discuss contrast use and its diagnostic benefits.

1. Orientation Comes Before Interpretation

Lecture p. 10. Axial images are viewed “foot to head,” coronal images “face to face,” and sagittal images from the side.
Need to memorize - image orientation

Axial = standing at feet.
Coronal = standing face-to-face.
Sagittal = side view.

🔥 FACULTY EMPHASIS

The lecture explicitly warns that students lose exam points by mixing up right and left and that wrong-sidedness is a real medical-error risk. Always identify the orientation marker before interpreting pathology.

Axial right-left logic

For standard axial CT/MRI display, imagine looking up from the patient's feet. That means the patient's right is on your left and the patient's left is on your right.

MEMORY HOOK

Axial = ankles looking upward. If you are standing at the patient's feet, your left faces their right.

2. Plain Radiography

Radiography / x-ray: imaging produced by passing ionizing x-rays through the body to a detector. Tissues that attenuate more x-rays appear relatively brighter.

Why things look bright or dark

Lecture p. 20. The lecture emphasizes density as the main determinant of x-ray brightness, with thickness and exposure also affecting appearance.
MaterialX-ray appearanceWhy
Air/gasBlack / radiolucentVery little attenuation
FatDark grayLow attenuation
Water/soft tissueIntermediate grayModerate attenuation
BoneWhiteHigh attenuation
MetalVery whiteVery high attenuation
Need to memorize - x-ray vocabulary

Opacity/radiopaque = bright.
Lucency/radiolucent = dark.
Attenuation = how much x-ray is blocked.

⚠️ TERMINOLOGY TRAP

The slide uses “hyperdense/hypodense” while discussing radiographs, but hyperdense/hypodense are most naturally used with CT. For plain x-ray, preferentially think radiopaque vs radiolucent.

PA vs AP chest radiograph

Lecture p. 13. PA means the beam travels posterior-to-anterior; AP means anterior-to-posterior.
Need to memorize - projection names

PA: beam enters posterior, exits anterior toward detector.
AP: beam enters anterior, exits posterior toward detector.

⭐ BOARD ENRICHMENT - HEART MAGNIFICATION

Portable AP chest radiographs can make the heart look larger because the heart is farther from the detector. A standard upright PA study gives a more reliable estimate of heart size.

Why at least two views?

A radiograph compresses a 3D structure into a 2D image. A second orthogonal view helps localize depth and avoid mistaking overlapping structures for pathology.

Systematic CXR reading

Need to memorize - ABCDEFGH

Airway • Bones • Cardiac • Diaphragm • Effusions • Fields (lungs) • Gastric bubble • Hardware.

Fluoroscopy

⭐ MODALITY ENRICHMENT

Fluoroscopy uses x-rays to create real-time or near-real-time moving images. Think contrast GI studies, angiography, catheter/device guidance, and dynamic joint/procedure imaging. It uses ionizing radiation.

3. Ultrasound

Ultrasound: imaging based on transmission of high-frequency sound waves and detection of returning echoes.
Lecture p. 28. Bright and dark on ultrasound refer to echo strength, not x-ray density.
Need to memorize - ultrasound words

Hyperechoic = bright / strong reflection.
Hypoechoic = darker / weak reflection.
Anechoic = black / essentially no internal echoes.

⚠️ DO NOT SAY “RADIOPAQUE” ON ULTRASOUND

Ultrasound appearance is described using echogenicity, not radiographic density/lucency terminology.

Why ultrasound is useful

  • No ionizing radiation.
  • Portable and relatively inexpensive.
  • Real-time motion and procedure guidance.
  • Excellent for fluid-filled structures, pregnancy, many abdominal structures, vascular flow with Doppler, and bedside POCUS.

Major limitations

  • Operator dependent.
  • Air and bone limit penetration/visualization.
  • Body habitus can reduce image quality.
  • Lower cross-sectional anatomic detail than CT/MRI in many applications.
⭐ BOARD ENRICHMENT - DOPPLER

Doppler ultrasound evaluates motion/velocity of blood flow. It is commonly used to assess vascular patency, stenosis, thrombosis, and flow direction.

4. Computed Tomography

Computed tomography (CT): cross-sectional imaging reconstructed from many x-ray measurements acquired around the patient.
Lecture p. 35. CT uses rotating x-ray acquisition to create slice-based images with much better density discrimination than plain radiographs.

High-yield advantages

  • Fast.
  • Excellent for trauma and acute emergency imaging.
  • Good visualization of bone, lung, hemorrhage, many abdominal processes, and vessels with contrast.
  • Cross-sectional imaging removes much of the superimposition problem of plain radiography.

Main costs/limitations

  • Ionizing radiation, at a substantially higher dose than a typical plain x-ray exam.
  • Moderate/high cost compared with plain radiography.
  • Contrast may be needed for some vascular/soft-tissue questions.

Hounsfield Units

Lecture p. 36. HU quantify relative CT attenuation. Water is the reference at 0 HU; air is about -1000 HU and bone is strongly positive.
Need to memorize - HU anchors

Air ≈ -1000 • fat ≈ -100 • water = 0 • soft tissue = positive tens • bone ≈ +1000.

Window/level: display settings that determine which HU range is emphasized. The same CT dataset can be displayed with lung, soft-tissue, bone, or other windows.
⭐ BOARD ENRICHMENT - CT DENSITY LANGUAGE

On CT, hyperdense means relatively higher attenuation/brighter and hypodense means lower attenuation/darker. These terms are relative to the surrounding/reference tissue.

5. Magnetic Resonance Imaging

MRI: imaging that uses a strong magnetic field, radiofrequency excitation, and detected tissue signal to generate images. It does not use ionizing radiation.
Lecture p. 42. The magnet is always on; screening for ferromagnetic objects and device compatibility is fundamental MRI safety.

What determines brightness?

Unlike x-ray/CT, MRI does not have a single “density brightness” rule. Signal depends on the pulse sequence and magnetic properties of tissue.

High-yield starting ruleTypical appearance
T1-weightedFat relatively bright; simple fluid relatively dark.
T2-weightedWater/fluid relatively bright.
DWIRestricted diffusion becomes bright; especially useful in acute ischemic stroke.
Air/cortical boneUsually very low signal/dark on routine sequences.
Lecture p. 43. The same ischemic process looks different across CT, T1, T2, and DWI; this is why MRI descriptions must include the sequence.
Need to memorize - MRI language

Use “signal”: high signal / low signal, hyperintense / hypointense. Do not automatically transfer x-ray words such as radiopaque/radiolucent to MRI.

Advantages

  • No ionizing radiation.
  • Excellent soft-tissue contrast.
  • Highly useful in CNS, spine, joints/soft tissue, marrow, pelvic imaging, and many vascular applications.

Limitations/safety

  • Expensive and relatively slow.
  • Motion can degrade images.
  • Claustrophobia can be limiting.
  • All implants/devices/metallic foreign bodies must be screened for MRI compatibility.
⭐ SAFETY ENRICHMENT

MRI devices are not simply “metal = never.” Modern practice distinguishes MR Safe, MR Conditional, and MR Unsafe equipment. The core student rule is to verify compatibility rather than assume.

6. Nuclear Medicine

Nuclear medicine: imaging in which a radiopharmaceutical is administered to the patient and emitted radiation is detected to map physiology or molecular activity.
Lecture p. 44. Nuclear medicine differs from x-ray/CT because the radioactive source is the administered tracer rather than an external beam passing through the patient.
Need to memorize - nuclear medicine

Tracer in patient -> functional signal out. Lower spatial resolution, but powerful physiologic/metabolic information.

PET concept

⭐ BOARD ENRICHMENT - PET

PET commonly uses radiotracers such as 18F-FDG, a glucose analog. Tissues with high glucose uptake may show increased tracer accumulation, which is why PET is useful for many cancers and some neurologic/cardiac applications. Increased uptake is not automatically cancer; inflammation can also be metabolically active.

Need to memorize - contrast pairing

Iodine/barium -> x-ray/CT.
Gadolinium -> MRI.
Radiotracer -> nuclear medicine.

⭐ CONTRAST SAFETY ENRICHMENT
  • All contrast agents can cause adverse reactions; clinically relevant reaction history should be identified before administration.
  • Renal function may affect contrast decisions in susceptible patients.
  • Gadolinium-based agents require special consideration in severe kidney disease because of the rare risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis.
  • The choice to use contrast is a risk-benefit decision based on the diagnostic question.

30-second SAL summary

Start every image by identifying the modality, plane/projection, and patient side. Plain x-ray and CT use ionizing radiation and display differences in attenuation; x-ray is fast and cheap, while CT gives rapid cross-sectional anatomy and quantitative HU values. Ultrasound uses sound echoes and is described as hyper-, hypo-, or anechoic. MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals, has no ionizing radiation, and is described by sequence-dependent signal intensity. Nuclear medicine administers a radiotracer to display physiology/function. Contrast improves tissue/vessel conspicuity but the contrast agent and safety considerations depend on modality. For the separate SAL quiz, orientation and modality-specific vocabulary should be automatic.
