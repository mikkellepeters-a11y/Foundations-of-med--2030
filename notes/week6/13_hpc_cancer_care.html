Holistic Patient Care in Cancer

Lauren Bigham, PhD • Week 6 Holistic Patient Care Session • Enriched Study Guide

What this session is really trying to teach

A cancer diagnosis is not only a biologic disease process; it can disrupt a patient's physical function, cognition, identity, relationships, finances, future plans, emotional health, and sense of meaning. The clinical task is to recognize distress early, normalize discussion of it, connect the patient to the right level of support, and preserve patient autonomy while doing so.

  • See the whole patient: physical, emotional, social, practical, behavioral, and spiritual concerns all affect care.
  • Expect distress to fluctuate: it often peaks at predictable transition points in the cancer journey.
  • Screen routinely: the companion handout frames distress as a “6th vital sign.”
  • Match intervention to need: psychoeducation, counseling/CBT, specialty psycho-oncology/psychiatry, plus practical interdisciplinary support.
  • Communicate collaboratively: use Elicit-Provide-Elicit instead of reflexively imposing a referral.
Need to memorize - master list

  2. Common psychosocial disruptions: pain, physical limitations, cognitive changes, body image, employment/finances, loss of social roles/identity, intimacy, uncertainty, threatened future goals.
  3. Distress is common: the slides cite 35-80% of patients experiencing distress.
  4. High-risk distress transitions: suspicious symptom, diagnosis, staging/diagnostic workup, starting treatment, changing/ending treatment, longer surveillance intervals, recurrence/progression, poor prognosis, advanced illness, end of life.

  6. Distress Thermometer: 0-10 scale + problem list. Companion handout teaches ≥4 as a positive screen.
  7. Positive screening ≠ automatic desire for referral. Follow with a conversation.
  8. Intervention ladder: psychoeducation -> counseling/behavioral therapy -> specialty psycho-oncology/psychiatry when severity/complexity warrants.
  9. CBT: helps patients identify and modify maladaptive thoughts/behaviors and build coping/self-efficacy.
  10. Elicit-Provide-Elicit: ask permission -> give clear/manageable information -> check interpretation/response.
  11. Holistic team examples: PT, OT, social work, pastoral care, dietitians, palliative care, psycho-oncology.

1. Psychosocial Implications of a Cancer Diagnosis

Psychosocial distress: a broad unpleasant experience involving mental, emotional, social, physical, practical, or spiritual concerns that can interfere with coping with cancer, symptoms, or treatment.

CLINICAL ENRICHMENT - FINANCIAL TOXICITY

Financial toxicity is the material and psychological burden created by the cost of illness and its effects on work, insurance, transportation, housing, food, and caregiving responsibilities. It is clinically relevant because practical barriers can directly alter adherence and access to treatment.

SESSION METAPHOR

Bigham opens with a patient's description of cancer as feeling like being “hit by a freight train”. The point is not merely emotional intensity; it is the sense that multiple domains of life are disrupted at once.

2. Frequency and Course of Distress

Need to memorize - distress hot spots

Suspicion -> diagnosis/staging -> treatment start -> treatment change/end -> surveillance -> recurrence/progression -> poor prognosis -> advanced disease/end of life.

Why the end of treatment can be stressful

The companion handout explicitly warns against assuming that distress falls when treatment ends. Patients may lose the frequent-contact “safety net,” become more focused on recurrence, and feel uncertain about how to transition back into ordinary life.

⚠️ COMMON EXAM TRAP

Clear scans do not guarantee low distress. A patient who just finished treatment can be highly anxious because uncertainty and fear of recurrence may become more salient once active treatment stops.

Distress can be new or an exacerbation

The slide deck notes that distress may become chronic or worsen a pre-existing psychological problem. That means a clinician should ask about both current symptoms and pre-cancer psychiatric/coping history.

3. Why Distress Screening Belongs in Cancer Care

Central principle

Psychosocial functioning and cancer diagnosis/treatment influence each other. Distress can affect treatment engagement, healthcare utilization/costs, quality of life, and clinical outcomes.

The slide deck cites professional standards calling for psychosocial needs to be integrated into routine cancer care and for cancer centers to identify patients with distress and triage them to appropriate resources.

COMPANION-HANDOUT HIGH YIELD - “6TH VITAL SIGN”

The companion handout teaches distress screening as a 6th vital sign: something that should be assessed routinely rather than waiting until distress becomes a crisis.

Need to memorize - purpose of screening

Normalize -> identify early -> clarify source -> connect to appropriate help.

4. Distress Thermometer and Problem List

Slide p. 8. The Distress Thermometer is a 0-10 rating paired with a broad problem list spanning physical, emotional, social, practical, and spiritual/religious concerns.
Need to memorize - companion-handout threshold

The companion handout teaches Distress Thermometer ≥4 = positive screen, prompting follow-up conversation and possible intervention.

SOURCE-SPECIFIC NOTE

The slide PDF displays the NCCN tool but does not itself state a numeric cutoff. The ≥4 threshold comes from the companion handout, so treat it as the source-specific exam point for this session rather than silently attributing it to the slide image.

CLINICAL REASONING

A high distress score tells you that the patient is struggling; the problem list helps tell you why. Two patients with the same score may need completely different interventions.

6. Psycho-Oncology

Psycho-oncology: the clinical field focused on the psychological, behavioral, social, interpersonal, and symptom-related impact of cancer and its treatment.

What Bigham's psycho-oncology service addresses

PsychologicalDepression, anxiety/worry, panic/fear, trauma, anger, grief/loss, denial, suicidal/homicidal ideation, treatment decisions
BehavioralHealth behavior change, adherence, substance use, risky decisions, healthcare overutilization

SAFETY NOTE

Suicidal or homicidal ideation is not just “routine distress.” It requires immediate safety assessment and escalation according to clinical protocol.

LevelWhat it looks likeWho may provide it

2. Counseling/behavioral therapyStructured coping skills, problem solving, CBT, adjustment workMental-health/behavioral-health clinicians

⚠️ NOT EVERY POSITIVE SCREEN NEEDS A PSYCHIATRIST

A positive screen should trigger assessment and conversation. The intervention should match severity, cause, patient preference, and safety needs.

8. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

Slide p. 11. CBT links thoughts, emotions, and behaviors and uses active skills to facilitate adjustment.
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): a structured, skills-based therapy that helps patients recognize and modify maladaptive thoughts and behaviors while building more effective coping responses.

Bigham's CBT tasks

  • Psychoeducation.
  • Managing emotional reactions.
  • Paced breathing, imagery, progressive muscle relaxation.
  • Distress-tolerance and present-moment skills.
  • Coping with limitations and changes.
  • Problem-solving, interpersonal skills, assertiveness.
  • Building control/self-efficacy in disease management.
  • Modifying maladaptive appraisals.
Need to memorize - CBT exam answer

CBT = identify and modify maladaptive thoughts/behaviors + build coping skills and self-efficacy.

COMMON DISTRACTOR

CBT is not primarily about prescribing medication, uncovering repressed childhood memories, or simply allowing unstructured emotional ventilation.

Elicit permission/perspective -> Provide manageable information -> Elicit understanding/response.

🔥 CENTRAL COMMUNICATION POINT

Bigham explicitly states that screening positive for anxiety does not equal a desire for help. Referral should follow a conversation with the patient.

Respecting autonomy does not mean ignoring serious distress. It means engaging the patient collaboratively, explaining options and urgency honestly, and distinguishing ordinary referral preferences from situations requiring immediate safety intervention.

Common Exam Traps

TrapCorrect logic
Distress means psychiatric illnessNo. Distress is broader and may be practical, social, physical, spiritual, or emotional.
Ending treatment always lowers anxietyNo. End-of-treatment transition can increase distress and recurrence fears.
A positive screen means the patient wants psychotherapyNo. Positive screen -> conversation, clarification, and collaborative next step.
A high score tells you which referral to makeNo. The problem list/source of distress determines the most appropriate support.
CBT is mainly medication managementNo. CBT is skills-based modification of thoughts and behaviors.
“Provide” in EPE means giving a long lectureNo. Provide information clearly and in manageable doses, focusing on patient priorities.
Holistic care = psycho-oncology aloneNo. Rehab, social work, nutrition, spiritual care, palliative care, and other services may be central.
Patient autonomy means doing nothing after SI/HI disclosureNo. Safety concerns require clinical risk assessment and escalation.

What to Memorize vs Understand

Tier 1 - memorize cold

Psychosocial domains; distress hot spots; 35-80%; companion-handout “6th vital sign” and ≥4 threshold; intervention ladder; CBT purpose; Elicit-Provide-Elicit; team/referral roles.

Tier 2 - understand deeply

Why distress changes over time, why screening requires follow-up rather than automatic referral, and how the source of distress determines the intervention.

Tier 3 - clinical integration

Financial toxicity, patient autonomy, safety escalation for SI/HI, matching rehab/social/spiritual/palliative/psycho-oncology resources to patient need.

Tier 4 - lower priority

Memorizing institutional referral logistics/contact details. Know the referral principles and service roles unless your instructor specifically tests local workflow.

30-second end-of-session summary

Cancer-related distress is broad, common, and dynamic. It can involve physical symptoms, cognition, body image, social roles, relationships, finances, practical barriers, emotional symptoms, and existential concerns. Distress often rises at predictable transitions such as diagnosis, treatment changes, surveillance, recurrence, poor prognosis, and end of life. Routine screening helps normalize and identify needs, but a positive screen should be followed by a conversation rather than an automatic referral. The clinician should clarify what is driving the distress, assess severity and safety, then connect the patient to appropriate interdisciplinary resources. CBT is a high-yield structured intervention, and Elicit-Provide-Elicit is the core communication framework for discussing sensitive concerns and referral options while preserving patient autonomy.
