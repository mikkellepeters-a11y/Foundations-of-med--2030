Genetic Testing for Familial Cancer Syndromes

John W. Henson, MD • Monday Lecture 2 • Week 5: Foundations of Cancer Biology

🔥 Professor-emphasis map

  • Germline vs somatic: tumor sequencing can suggest a hereditary syndrome, but tumor tissue is not the same as germline testing.
  • Tumor suppressor genes and the second hit: hereditary cancer commonly begins with one inherited inactivating hit followed by somatic loss of the remaining functional allele.
  • Lynch = DNA mismatch-repair failure → mutagenesis / MSI-high / hypermutator.
  • FAP = APC/Wnt/β-catenin mitogenic signaling → classical polyposis and comparatively lower tumor mutation burden.
  • HBOC = homologous-recombination / double-strand DNA repair defect, especially BRCA1/BRCA2.
  • Variant classification matters: pathogenic and VUS are not clinically interchangeable.
  • Family testing (cascade testing) follows identification of a germline pathogenic variant.

Learning Objectives

  1. Identify the three “red flags” that indicate possible hereditary cancer predisposition.
  2. Identify the most common cancers seen in Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC) syndrome and Lynch syndrome.
  3. Describe the difference between Pathogenic, Variant of Uncertain Significance, and Benign variants.
  4. Explain the use of GeneReviews and National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines.

Foundations — What Makes a Cancer Hereditary?

TermLecture meaning
Germline variantPresent from early development in essentially all cells and potentially transmissible through gametes.
Somatic variantArises in an individual cell after development; present in the tumor lineage, not necessarily throughout the body.
Sporadic cancerDisease arising from acquired/somatic alterations without an identified inherited predisposition.
Familial cancerClustering in a family without an identified causative germline variant; may reflect shared environment, chance, unknown genes, or technical limitations.
Hereditary cancerA germline variant increases disease risk and can be transmitted to offspring.
Lecture pp. 2–5.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Two-hit hypothesis

Most classic hereditary cancer syndromes in this lecture involve tumor suppressor genes. A person inherits one nonfunctional allele (first hit), then a susceptible cell acquires somatic loss/inactivation of the remaining normal allele (second hit). The predisposition is inherited in an autosomal-dominant pattern at the person level, while loss of tumor-suppressor function is recessive at the cellular level.

LO1 — The Three Red Flags for Hereditary Cancer Predisposition

The lecture teaches these mainly through cases and pedigrees rather than as a single numbered slide. The following three-category framework fills the LO using standard cancer-genetics risk-assessment language.
Red flagWhat to noticeExamples
1. Unusually early cancerCancer occurring substantially younger than expected for the population.CRC in a young adult; premenopausal breast cancer.
2. Multiple / bilateral / unusual primary tumorsMore than one primary cancer in one person, bilateral paired-organ tumors, multifocal disease, or a rare tumor pattern.Breast + ovarian cancer in one person; bilateral breast cancer; multiple Lynch-associated primaries.
3. Family clustering across generationsSeveral close relatives with the same cancer or a recognizable syndrome-associated cancer combination.Breast + ovarian + pancreatic/prostate pattern; colorectal + endometrial pattern.
🔥 LECTURE CONNECTION

The Lynch, FAP, and HBOC case pedigrees repeatedly use young onset, multiple affected relatives, and syndrome-specific cancer clustering to trigger germline evaluation and cascade testing.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

A negative family history does not rule out hereditary cancer. Small families, incomplete penetrance, limited family information, adoption, early deaths, sex-specific cancer risk, and de novo variants can obscure the pedigree.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — BRCA

BRCA1/2 are tumor suppressors involved in homologous recombination repair of double-strand DNA breaks. Loss of BRCA function creates dependence on alternative repair pathways; this provides the biologic basis for synthetic lethality with PARP inhibition.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Lynch

Lynch syndrome is an autosomal-dominant hereditary cancer syndrome caused by germline loss-of-function in mismatch-repair pathway genes. Defective mismatch repair produces microsatellite instability and a high mutation burden.

Lynch Syndrome — Case-Based Testing Logic

MMR immunohistochemistry

The lecture’s Lynch case shows loss of MLH1 and PMS2 nuclear staining. MLH1 and PMS2 function as a heterodimer, so loss of one partner can destabilize the pair. Board-style pairing to remember: MLH1–PMS2 and MSH2–MSH6.

Lecture p. 14 — MMR proteins work in pairs; paired loss patterns help direct evaluation.

Tumor test ≠ germline test

  • Tumor NGS in the case showed MSI-high, MMR loss, and high TMB, suggesting possible Lynch syndrome.
  • The slide explicitly warns: “THIS IS TUMOR TISSUE, not germline.”
  • Germline testing used leukocyte/blood DNA and identified a pathogenic MLH1 variant.
Lecture p. 18 — the same variant is demonstrated in blood, establishing germline status.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — dMMR is not automatically Lynch

A tumor can acquire mismatch-repair deficiency somatically. Tumor findings can trigger hereditary evaluation, but germline testing is required to establish an inherited pathogenic variant.

Lecture p. 9 — repeated professor comparison: Lynch = DNA repair/mutagenesis; FAP = APC/β-catenin/mitosis.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — FAP / Gardner pattern

APC loss permits β-catenin accumulation and transcription of proliferative genes. Classic extracolonic clues include desmoid tumors, osteomas, and congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium (CHRPE).

LO3 — Pathogenic vs VUS vs Benign

Lecture p. 45 — ACMG/AMP five-tier sequence-variant classification.
ClassMeaningClinical implication
Pathogenic (P)Evidence supports that the variant causes disease / increases syndrome-specific risk.Can establish a molecular diagnosis in the proper clinical context; may guide syndrome-specific surveillance, risk reduction, treatment, and cascade testing.
Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)Current evidence is insufficient or conflicting; cannot determine whether the variant is disease-causing.Do not treat it like a pathogenic variant. Management should be based on personal/family history and established risk factors while the variant may later be reclassified.
Benign (B)Evidence supports that the variant is not disease-causing.Does not explain a hereditary cancer syndrome and is not used for syndrome-specific predictive testing.
Lecture p. 45; definitions supplemented from ACMG/AMP variant-interpretation standards and board-style genetics resources.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — VUS

VUS ≠ positive test. Do not recommend prophylactic surgery or label relatives as genetically “affected” based on a VUS alone. Reclassification can occur as population, segregation, functional, and computational evidence accumulates.

Why the BRCA1 case was pathogenic

The lecture’s BRCA1 intronic variant created an abnormal splice acceptor and altered RNA splicing. This is a useful reminder that intronic does not automatically mean benign; an intronic change can be pathogenic when it disrupts normal splicing.

Cascade testing

Lecture p. 49 — after a familial pathogenic BRCA1 variant is established, relatives can undergo targeted cascade testing.

Best logic: first identify a pathogenic/likely pathogenic germline variant in an affected family member when possible, then test at-risk relatives for that known familial variant.

LO4 — How to Use GeneReviews and NCCN Guidelines

ResourceWhat it is best forHow to use it
GeneReviewsExpert-authored, peer-reviewed disease chapters covering diagnosis/testing, clinical features, inheritance, management, surveillance, and counseling.Use when you need a syndrome overview: What genes cause it? What findings suggest it? How is it inherited? What testing methods matter? What should relatives know?
NCCN GuidelinesVersioned clinical-practice guidance for cancer risk assessment, genetic testing indications, surveillance, and risk-reduction/management.Use when you need a clinical action pathway: Does this patient meet testing criteria? What surveillance/risk-reducing options are recommended for this gene/risk level? Always check the current version.
🩺 Practical distinction

GeneReviews answers “What is this hereditary syndrome and how does it work?”
NCCN answers “What should I do clinically for this patient now?”

Rapid Step 1 Review

Hereditary red flags

Early onset; multiple/bilateral primaries; family clustering of related cancers.

Two-hit model

Inherited TSG loss + somatic loss of remaining functional allele.

Lynch genes

MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2; EPCAM deletion can affect MSH2.

Lynch cancers

Colorectal + endometrial are the core pair; also ovarian, gastric/upper GI, urothelial and others.

HBOC genes

BRCA1/BRCA2 are the classic high-yield genes; homologous-recombination repair.

HBOC cancers

Breast, ovarian, prostate, pancreatic.

FAP

APC loss → β-catenin accumulation → polyposis and CRC predisposition.

MSI

Signature of mismatch-repair failure; high mutation burden can predict checkpoint-inhibitor sensitivity.

Pathogenic

Disease-causing evidence; clinically actionable in context.

VUS

Uncertain; do not use alone for irreversible management decisions.

GeneReviews

Expert syndrome overview: diagnosis, testing, inheritance, management, counseling.

NCCN

Current clinical criteria and management pathways; check latest version.
