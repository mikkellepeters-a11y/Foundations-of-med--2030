Palliative Care in Cancer Care

Pamela Tipler, DO, FACP, DTM&H • Thursday Lecture 1 • Week 5

What this lecture is really about

  • Palliative care is active medical care aimed at reducing suffering and improving quality of life; it can coexist with cancer-directed treatment.
  • Hospice is narrower than palliative care. It is an end-of-life model used when comfort becomes the dominant goal and burdens of life-prolonging therapy exceed expected benefit.
  • Referral should be triggered by needs: high symptom burden, progressive disease, functional decline, repeated crises, or difficult goals-of-care decisions.
  • Communication is part of treatment: anticipate, plan, communicate clearly, validate, and follow up.
  • Symptom treatment starts by identifying the mechanism of the symptom and matching intervention to the patient's goals.

Learning Objectives

  1. Define palliative care.
  2. Determine who should be considered for palliative care.
  3. Have open communication when discussing palliative care/hospice.
  4. Identify ways to manage end-of-life issues.
  5. Explain management of common end-of-life symptoms.

1. Palliative Care vs Hospice

Palliative careHospice
Can begin at any stage of a serious illness and can occur alongside chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, or other disease-directed therapy. End-of-life care model in which comfort becomes the primary goal and burdens of further life-prolonging therapy outweigh likely benefit.
⚠️ COMMON MISCONCEPTION

"There is nothing more we can do" is the wrong framework. Even when cure is not possible, clinicians can still treat pain, breathlessness, nausea, delirium, anxiety, family distress, and uncertainty.

2. Who Should Trigger Palliative Involvement?

  • Progressive, life-limiting disease such as incurable cancer or advanced organ failure.
  • High symptom burden: pain, dyspnea, nausea, fatigue, delirium, recurrent obstruction/effusions, etc.
  • Declining function or performance status.
  • Repeated hospitalizations or crises despite appropriate disease-directed care.
  • Need for help with goals, prognosis, family communication, or treatment-burden decisions.
🩺 PERFORMANCE STATUS

The lecture uses ECOG and Karnofsky as examples of tools that summarize function. They help communicate prognosis and treatment tolerance, but palliative care should not be reduced to a numerical threshold.

3. Communication: A Practical Framework

Before

  • Anticipate likely decline, loss of oral medication route, dyspnea, delirium, pneumonia, congestion, agitation, or sudden change.
  • Plan needed medications, supplies, family presence, and "what to do when..." scenarios.
  • Set the stage: sit down, reduce distractions, use plain language.

During

  • Explore the patient's understanding of illness.
  • Ask about hopes, fears, priorities, and cultural/spiritual factors.
  • Be direct about prognosis when asked; avoid vague euphemisms.
  • Do not assume silence means the patient has no questions.
  • Reassure the patient that pain, dyspnea, and agitation can usually be treated.

After

  • Check understanding and invite further questions.
  • Offer social work, pastoral/spiritual care, and family support.
  • Make a concrete follow-up plan.

4. End-of-Life Decision-Making

⭐ GOAL-CONCORDANT DECISION FILTER

Is there a reversible cause? If yes, ask whether treating it would meaningfully improve comfort or another patient-defined goal, and whether the burden of treatment is acceptable.

This is the practical meaning of "fix the fixable" without automatically treating every abnormality simply because it is technically treatable.

5. Symptom Management

Pain

TypeTypical descriptionManagement logic
Somatic nociceptiveLocalized aching/sharp pain; often worse with movement.Non-opioids, opioids, local therapy when appropriate, plus adjuvants.
Visceral nociceptivePoorly localized aching/cramping; may be referred.Opioids plus treatment of the visceral cause when consistent with goals.
NeuropathicBurning, tingling, electric, shooting, lancinating.Often benefits from anticonvulsant/antidepressant adjuvants plus analgesics.
Cropped lecture figure: escalate analgesia according to pain severity; adjuvants can be used at any step.

Opioid strategy

  • Persistent pain generally needs a scheduled baseline regimen.
  • Breakthrough pain needs a short-acting rescue medication.
  • Prevent constipation proactively when opioids are started.
  • Exact equianalgesic conversion, especially methadone, should use a current clinical reference.
⚠️ TOLERANCE vs DEPENDENCE vs ADDICTION

Tolerance = higher dose needed for same effect. Physical dependence = withdrawal if abruptly stopped. Addiction = compulsive use despite harm. These are not interchangeable.

Dyspnea

  • Simple measures: upright/semi-reclined positioning, calm reassurance, fan/open window.
  • Treat a reversible disease-specific cause when appropriate.
  • Oxygen is most useful when hypoxemia is present.
  • Appropriately titrated opioids can reduce the sensation of air hunger.

Other common symptoms

SymptomHigh-yield approach
ConstipationLook for opioids, immobility, hypercalcemia, obstruction; start a bowel regimen proactively with opioids.
Nausea/vomitingTreat the cause when appropriate; use regular antiemetic therapy matched to mechanism/context.
FatigueNot necessarily relieved by rest; use education, energy conservation, activity/rehab when feasible.
AnorexiaAppetite stimulants may improve intake/weight without improving survival; nutrition interventions should be individualized.
DeliriumQuiet familiar environment, fix reversible contributors when consistent with goals, relieve refractory distress.
Terminal secretionsOften distress family more than patient; reposition and use antisecretory agents when needed.
