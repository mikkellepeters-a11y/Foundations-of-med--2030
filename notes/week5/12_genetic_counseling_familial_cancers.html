Genetic Counseling for Familial Cancers

Charlaine Conger, NP-C • Wednesday Lecture 4 • Week 5: Foundations of Cancer Biology

🔥 Professor-emphasis map

This lecture is clinically oriented rather than pathway-heavy. I prioritized the recurring decision points: who should be referred/tested, what must be discussed before testing, how results are interpreted, and how those results change management.

  • Genetic counseling is more than ordering a test: it includes pedigree collection, risk assessment, inheritance education, informed discussion of implications, support, test coordination, and result interpretation.
  • Know the hereditary-cancer “red flags.” The lecture gives a detailed list including early diagnosis, rare tumors, multiple primaries/bilateral tumors, family clustering, excessive benign lesions, suggestive tumor testing, known familial variants, and high-risk ancestry.
  • Some cancer presentations should prompt panel testing even without an obvious syndrome. The lecture highlights early breast cancer/TNBC, ovarian-fallopian-primary peritoneal cancer, endometrial cancer, pancreatic cancer, metastatic prostate cancer, and selected colorectal cancer.
  • Patient factors matter: goals, family-history knowledge, psychological factors, and concerns about insurance/genetic discrimination can all influence decision-making.
  • Pathogenic ≠ VUS ≠ benign. A pathogenic variant can change surveillance and family counseling; a VUS generally should not guide care or predictive testing of relatives.
  • HBOC is caused by pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants and is associated with breast, ovarian, prostate, pancreatic, and melanoma risk in the lecture.
  • Use the right resource for the right question: NCCN for testing/management criteria; GeneReviews for diagnosis, management, and genetic counseling; ClinVar/VarSome for variant-level information.
  • Cascade testing is part of hereditary cancer care. A confirmed pathogenic germline variant has implications for biologic relatives, not just the proband.

LO1 - When and How to Identify Individuals for Cancer Genetics Risk Assessment

Core idea: the job is not to test everyone indiscriminately. The clinician identifies a pattern suggesting inherited predisposition, builds a pedigree, estimates risk, and decides whether germline testing and genetics counseling are appropriate.

What genetic counseling actually includes

Lecture p. 3 - counseling includes detailed personal/family history, genetic risk assessment, inheritance/testing education, support, test coordination, and result interpretation.
StepPurpose

Sporadic, familial, and monogenic cancer

Lecture p. 4 - most cancers are sporadic; a smaller subset shows familial clustering, and an even smaller subset reflects a recognizable monogenic hereditary syndrome.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Hereditary predisposition vs inherited tumor

Most hereditary cancer syndromes transmit a predisposition, not a fully formed cancer. The patient inherits a germline pathogenic variant that raises cancer risk; additional somatic events are usually required before a tumor develops.

Inheritance pattern

Lecture p. 5 - the pedigree diagram illustrates vertical transmission of a predisposition allele followed by tumor development in selected carriers.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Autosomal dominant predisposition

Many classic hereditary cancer syndromes are autosomal dominant at the person level: inheriting one pathogenic allele creates increased risk. For tumor-suppressor syndromes, complete loss of function commonly requires a second somatic hit at the cellular level.

Who should be tested? - Lecture “always test” scenarios

Lecture p. 7 - examples the lecturer labels as warranting panel genetic testing even without an obvious syndromic phenotype.
  • Breast cancer at age 50 or younger and/or triple-negative breast cancer.
  • Ovarian, fallopian-tube, or primary peritoneal cancer.
  • Endometrial cancer.
  • Pancreatic cancer.
  • Prostate cancer metastatic at presentation.
  • Colorectal cancer with mismatch-repair deficiency or diagnosis at age 50 or younger.
🔥 LECTURE-SPECIFIC TESTING CRITERIA

These are the testing triggers exactly as presented in this lecture. Genetic-testing criteria evolve over time, so for the course use the lecturer's framework and use current guideline resources such as NCCN in actual practice.

“Red flags” of hereditary cancer

Lecture p. 9 - this is the key referral/risk-assessment slide.
  • Early age at diagnosis.
  • Rare cancers or tumors.
  • Multiple primary cancers or bilateral tumors.
  • Tumor testing suggests a possible germline variant.
  • Excessive benign lesions, such as a large polyp burden.
  • Multiple close relatives with the same or related cancers.
  • Known pathogenic variant in a family member.
  • High-risk ancestry/ethnicity, with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry used as the lecture example.
⭐ COURSE CONNECTION - The “three red flags” shorthand

If an exam compresses this list into three broad buckets, think: early/unusual cancer, multiple/bilateral primary tumors, and family clustering across close relatives/generations. The Conger lecture gives the fuller real-world list above.

Pedigree assessment

Lecture p. 11 - a pedigree integrates cancer types, ages, lineage, and ancestry to decide whether a hereditary pattern is plausible.
🩺 CLINICAL CONNECTION - Test the most informative person

When possible, testing an affected family member is usually more informative than beginning with an unaffected relative. If the affected person has a pathogenic variant, relatives can then undergo focused cascade testing for that known familial variant.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - BRCA biology

BRCA1/2 participate in homologous recombination repair of double-strand DNA breaks. Loss of this pathway creates genomic instability and explains the therapeutic rationale for PARP-inhibitor synthetic lethality in selected BRCA-deficient tumors.

LO2 - Considerations Before Genetic Testing: Ethical, Legal, and Social Implications

Core idea: genetic testing can affect medical decisions, family relationships, anxiety, insurance concerns, and relatives who never asked to be tested. Pretest counseling should prepare the patient for all plausible result categories and downstream consequences.

Patient factors

Lecture p. 10 - the lecturer emphasizes that patient goals may differ from clinician goals, family-history knowledge may be incomplete, psychological factors matter, and insurance concerns can affect decisions.
  • Patient goals may differ from clinician goals.
  • Family-history information may be incomplete or inaccurate.
  • Testing can provoke anxiety, guilt, uncertainty, or family conflict.
  • Genetic information has implications for relatives who may or may not want to know their risk.
  • The lecture specifically flags GINA 2008 as an insurance/legal consideration.
⚠️ LEGAL / ETHICAL CAUTION

The lecture names GINA as a counseling consideration. For exams, know the general principle that genetic information raises privacy and discrimination concerns. Do not assume every type of insurance or every legal context is covered identically; real-world counseling may require current policy-specific guidance.

How germline testing is performed

Lecture p. 13 - germline DNA can be obtained using blood, saliva, or buccal-swab specimens.

The specimen source is not the same thing as the clinical interpretation. Blood, saliva, and buccal cells can all provide germline DNA; what matters is what genes/variants are analyzed and how the result is interpreted in the context of personal and family history.

Result interpretation: benign vs VUS vs pathogenic

Lecture p. 14 - management differs sharply across benign, VUS, and pathogenic findings.
ResultMeaningClinical response in lecture
BenignNo evidence of pathogenicity; normal variation.No syndrome-specific action based on the variant.
VUSUncertain whether the variant is benign or pathogenic.Manage based on personal/family history; await possible reclassification.
PathogenicDisease-causing / predisposes to disease.Gene-specific medical management and discussion of family risk.
Lecture p. 15 - a VUS can confuse patients; the slide states that, with rare exception, it should not guide patient care or be used to assess cancer risk in relatives.
🔥 PROFESSOR EMPHASIS - VUS

A VUS is not a positive result. With rare exception, do not use a VUS alone to change surveillance, justify prophylactic surgery, or perform predictive testing in relatives. Management should continue to follow the patient's phenotype and family history while the variant awaits reclassification.

⭐ STEP 1 / COURSE HIGH YIELD - Cascade testing

Once a pathogenic germline variant is identified, biologic relatives can be offered targeted testing for that familial variant. A relative who tests negative for the known familial variant may avoid unnecessary syndrome-specific surveillance; a carrier can enter appropriate surveillance/risk-reduction pathways.

Communicating risk

The lecture notes that cancer risk is commonly presented as lifetime risk. Counseling should distinguish absolute risk from relative risk and should explain that penetrance can be incomplete: carrying a pathogenic predisposition variant does not guarantee that cancer will occur.

⭐ COMMONLY TESTED CONCEPT - Penetrance

Penetrance is the proportion of people with a genotype who express the associated phenotype. Many hereditary cancer syndromes show incomplete penetrance, which is why a seemingly unaffected relative can still transmit a pathogenic variant.

Lecture p. 18 - NCCN hereditary-cancer guidelines are used to translate a pathogenic result into surveillance and risk-management recommendations.
Lecture p. 21 - GeneReviews is presented as an online resource providing clinically relevant information for inherited conditions.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - NCCN vs GeneReviews

NCCN is especially useful for current testing criteria and clinical management algorithms. GeneReviews is an expert disease/syndrome overview that integrates diagnosis, inheritance, clinical features, surveillance, and counseling.

Pathogenic variant → management is gene-specific

Lecture p. 20 - the PALB2 example demonstrates that management depends on the specific gene and organ-specific risk profile rather than on the generic label “positive genetic test.”
🩺 CLINICAL CONNECTION

A positive hereditary-cancer result can change screening frequency, age at screening initiation, imaging modality, consideration of risk-reducing surgery, systemic treatment options, and counseling/testing of relatives. The exact plan is gene- and syndrome-specific.
