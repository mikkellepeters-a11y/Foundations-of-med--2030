LO1 - Molecular Basis of Carcinogenesis

Core idea: cancer develops when mutations allow a damaged cell to proliferate, evade growth suppression, resist apoptosis, and/or accumulate additional mutations because DNA repair is impaired.

Mutation is the central event

The lecture simplifies carcinogenesis to one word: mutation. Normal cells that sustain DNA damage should either repair the damage or undergo apoptosis. If these safeguards fail, the abnormal DNA can be passed to daughter cells and additional driver mutations can accumulate.

Lecture pp. 4-6.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Driver vs passenger concept

A driver mutation gives the tumor cell a selective growth/survival advantage. Passenger mutations accumulate during genomic instability but do not themselves drive the malignant phenotype. Board questions usually focus on recognizable driver pathways.

Oncogenes

Lecture definition: an oncogene is the mutated/activated form of a normal growth-promoting proto-oncogene.

Proto-oncogene → oncogene

  • Proto-oncogenes normally participate in regulated cell growth and division.
  • An activating mutation can cause the growth signal to remain “on.”
  • At the cellular level, oncogenes usually behave as gain-of-function, dominant mutations: one activated allele can be sufficient.
Lecture p. 7; board-style dominance language is supplemental.
Lecture p. 9 - HER2/NEU amplification, MYC amplification, BCR-ABL translocation, and BRAF V600E are the professor’s examples.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Oncogene activation mechanisms

Common ways to activate proto-oncogenes are point mutation (e.g., RAS, BRAF), gene amplification (e.g., HER2, MYC/N-MYC), and chromosomal translocation (e.g., BCR-ABL or MYC).

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

Proto-oncogene = normal gene that supports regulated growth.
Oncogene = abnormally activated version that promotes uncontrolled growth.

Tumor Suppressor Genes

A tumor suppressor normally holds the cell in check by slowing proliferation, enforcing checkpoints, repairing damage, or promoting death of damaged cells. Cancer develops when these brakes are lost.

Lecture p. 10 - the professor explicitly teaches the two-hit model.

Two-hit model

  • Cells have two copies of most tumor-suppressor genes.
  • Complete loss of function generally requires inactivation of both alleles.
  • Hereditary cancer syndromes often begin with one inherited defective copy, so only one additional somatic “hit” is required in a susceptible cell.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Oncogene vs tumor suppressor

Oncogene = accelerator stuck on (gain-of-function, one allele often enough).
Tumor suppressor = brakes fail (loss-of-function, both alleles typically must be lost in the tumor cell).

Genes Regulating Apoptosis

Cancer can arise by either increasing anti-apoptotic signaling or losing pro-apoptotic signaling. The lecture emphasizes that the normal balance between these pathways can be tipped in either direction.

Lecture p. 13 - t(14;18) places BCL2 near the immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus in follicular lymphoma, causing persistent anti-apoptotic signaling.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - BCL2 pathway

BCL2 is anti-apoptotic. Overexpression allows abnormal cells to survive when they should die. In the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, pro-apoptotic proteins such as BAX/BAK favor cytochrome c release; BCL2 opposes this process.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - What BCL2 does

BCL2 does not primarily make follicular lymphoma cells divide faster. It makes them survive longer by blocking apoptosis.

Genes Involved in DNA Repair

DNA-repair defects do not necessarily provide a direct growth signal. Instead, they create a mutator phenotype: unrepaired DNA damage persists, increasing the probability of mutations in oncogenes, tumor suppressors, and apoptosis regulators.

Lecture p. 15 - UV damage is repaired by NER; inherited NER failure causes xeroderma pigmentosum with extreme UV sensitivity and skin-cancer risk.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Additional repair pathway

BRCA1/2 participate in homologous recombination repair of double-strand DNA breaks. Loss of this pathway is a classic board-level DNA-repair defect associated with hereditary breast/ovarian and related cancers.

LO2 - Carcinogens: Radiation, Infections, and Chemicals

Core idea: carcinogens cause or promote mutations and cellular changes that ultimately affect the same growth-control, apoptosis, or DNA-repair pathways described above.

Radiation

Ultraviolet radiation

Lecture p. 19 - the yellow “WELDERS!” callout is explicit professor emphasis.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - UV DNA lesion

UVB → pyrimidine dimers → NER. Failure of NER in xeroderma pigmentosum causes markedly increased risk of UV-induced skin malignancy.

Ionizing radiation

  • Includes electromagnetic radiation such as x-rays and particulate radiation such as alpha/beta particles, protons, and neutrons.
  • Causes DNA damage; if repair fails but the cell survives, mutations can be inherited by daughter cells.
  • Lecture examples include atomic-bomb survivors, Chernobyl-associated thyroid cancers, early occupational x-ray exposure, and radiation-induced second malignancies.
  • A specific lecture example is post-radiation angiosarcoma of the breast years after therapeutic irradiation.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - Asbestos

Asbestos is associated with both mesothelioma and bronchogenic carcinoma. In asbestos-exposed smokers, the risk of bronchogenic carcinoma rises markedly; smoking does not show the same synergistic effect for mesothelioma.

LO3 - Epidemiology of Common Cancers

Core idea: distinguish incidence (how commonly a cancer occurs) from mortality (how often it causes death). The most common cancer is not necessarily the most lethal.

Cancer as a cause of death

The lecture’s CDC figures from 2022, 2023, and provisional 2024 data show cancer as the second leading cause of death in the United States after heart disease.

Lecture pp. 31-33. These are lecture-era epidemiologic data and are intentionally preserved rather than replaced with newer statistics.

Adult cancer incidence

Lecture p. 40 - explicit stars: men: prostate is most common; women: breast is most common.
🔥 PROFESSOR EMPHASIS - Most common

Men: prostate cancer.
Women: breast cancer.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Nonmelanoma skin cancer

Nonmelanoma skin cancers are extremely common but are often excluded from major cancer-registry incidence tables. That is why “most common cancer” questions can depend on whether NMSC is being counted.

Adult cancer mortality

Lecture p. 42 - explicit stars: lung cancer is the most deadly cancer in both men and women.
🔥 PROFESSOR EMPHASIS - Most deadly

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in both men and women in the lecture dataset.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - Incidence vs mortality

Most common ≠ most deadly. Breast and prostate lead incidence in women and men, respectively, while lung cancer leads mortality in both sexes in the lecture data.

Pediatric cancer

  • The lecture states that cancer is the most common cause of death from disease after infancy in ages 0-19.
  • A larger proportion of pediatric cancers reflects inherited predisposition because children have had less cumulative carcinogen exposure.
  • B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is identified as the most common pediatric cancer and has an excellent cure rate in the lecture.
Lecture p. 44 - leukemia is the largest pediatric incidence category in the displayed 0-19 year data.
Lecture p. 45 - brain/CNS tumors lead mortality in the displayed pediatric dataset, followed by leukemia.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Pediatric pattern

Leukemia is the most common childhood malignancy overall, especially ALL. Pediatric tumors also have a higher relative contribution from germline cancer-predisposition syndromes than typical adult cancers.
