Introduction to Neoplasia

Terminology & Tumor Biology • Zach Ramsey, MD • learning-objective–based summary with USMLE Step 1 enrichment

🔥 Professor-emphasis map

This lecture uses color and repeated comparisons to signal important concepts. Those points are intentionally elevated in this summary.

  • Malignant nomenclature by cell origin: epithelial → carcinoma, mesenchymal → sarcoma, hematopoietic → lymphoma/leukemia, melanocytic → melanoma.
  • Clonality: tumors arise from a single precursor cell; “monoclonality” is especially used for lymphoid neoplasms, while reactive/non-neoplastic proliferations are often polyclonal.
  • Benign vs malignant morphology: differentiation/anaplasia, nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, dysplasia, growth rate, local invasion, and metastasis.
  • Basement membrane status matters: dysplasia/carcinoma in situ remains above an intact basement membrane; invasive carcinoma breaches it.
  • Metastasis is a defining malignant behavior and strongly worsens prognosis.
  • TNM staging: Tumor, Node, Metastasis; grading instead refers primarily to microscopic appearance.

LO1 — Define the Nomenclature for Neoplasia

Core idea: tumor names communicate the tissue/cell of origin, morphology, and usually benign versus malignant behavior.

Basic definitions

  • Neoplasia = “new growth.”
  • Oncology = study of tumors.
  • Cancer = common term for malignant neoplasms.
  • Neoplasm = an abnormal mass whose growth exceeds and is uncoordinated with normal tissue and persists after the original stimulus is removed.
Lecture p. 3.

Two basic components of a tumor

  • Neoplastic/proliferating tumor cells = the actual transformed cell population.
  • Stroma = supporting extracellular matrix and blood vessels.

The lecture emphasizes that the stroma helps determine tumor consistency:

  • Scant stroma → soft/fleshy tumor.
  • Desmoplasia/sclerosis → firm or hard tumor.
Lecture p. 5.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Desmoplasia

A desmoplastic reaction is dense fibrous stromal response around a tumor. It can make an invasive carcinoma feel hard and is a common pathology clue for stromal reaction to malignancy.

Benign tumor naming

Benign tumors often use the tissue or pattern name plus the suffix -oma.

Lecture pp. 6–9.

Adenoma

  • Can be gland forming histologically, e.g., tubular adenoma of the colon.
  • Can also arise from a gland even if it does not form glands histologically, e.g., adrenal cortical adenoma.

Polyp

A polyp is a gross description: a lesion that protrudes into the lumen of a mucosa-lined organ. It does not by itself specify benign vs malignant histology.

Papilloma

A papilloma is defined by papillary architecture. Papillomas can occur in different organs and be lined by different types of epithelium.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — “-oma” does not always mean benign

The lecture itself includes malignant names such as melanoma and lymphoma. For Step 1, also remember classic malignant “-oma” exceptions such as seminoma and mesothelioma.

Tumors with divergent differentiation

Pleomorphic adenoma of the salivary gland contains epithelial/myoepithelial cells plus chondromyxoid-appearing matrix, but the lecture emphasizes that this can arise by divergent differentiation from a single cell line.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Pleomorphic adenoma vs teratoma

Pleomorphic adenoma may look “mixed,” but it is a single-clone salivary neoplasm with divergent differentiation. A teratoma instead arises from germ cells and can contain mature/immature tissues representing multiple germ layers.

Teratoma

  • Arises from germ cell rests.
  • Can form tissues representing ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm.
  • Classically found in the ovary and testis.
Lecture p. 18.

Why nomenclature matters clinically

A precise tumor diagnosis conveys expected histology, prognosis, and treatment implications. The lecture uses different testicular tumors as an example to show that not all tumors in the same organ behave or respond to therapy the same way.

Tumor Biology — Clonality, Evolution, and Kinetics

Clonality

  • Benign and malignant neoplasms arise from a single precursor cell and are therefore clonal.
  • Monoclonality is a term commonly used in lymphoid neoplasia.
  • Reactive/non-neoplastic proliferations are often polyclonal, arising from several precursor cells.
Lecture p. 21. “Clonal,” “monoclonality,” and “polyclonality” are highlighted on the slide.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Monoclonal vs polyclonal lymphocytes

A monoclonal lymphoid population supports a neoplastic process, whereas a polyclonal population is more consistent with reactive proliferation. In B cells, clonality can be assessed by immunoglobulin light-chain restriction or gene-rearrangement studies.

Clonal evolution and tumor heterogeneity

Although a tumor begins from one clone, ongoing genetic instability produces subclones. Selection favors subclones with survival advantages such as faster growth, invasiveness, metastatic ability, or resistance to therapy.

Lecture p. 22.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Why relapse can be different

Tumor heterogeneity helps explain why treatment can shrink most of a tumor while a resistant subclone survives and later drives recurrence or progression.

Tumor cell kinetics

  • The lecture links growth rate with degree of differentiation.
  • Classic cytotoxic chemotherapy acts preferentially on cells that are actively cycling/dividing.
  • Slow-growing tumors may be relatively less responsive to such chemotherapy.
  • Fast-growing tumors can be clinically aggressive but may respond well to cytotoxic chemotherapy.
  • Rapidly dividing normal tissues are also injured, including skin/hair and GI epithelium.
Lecture p. 23.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Cytotoxic toxicity pattern

Classic chemotherapy commonly harms tissues with high turnover: bone marrow → cytopenias, GI mucosa → mucositis/diarrhea, and hair follicles → alopecia.

LO2 — General Properties of Benign vs Malignant Tumors

Core idea: no single microscopic feature is perfect, but differentiation, nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, growth rate, local invasion, and metastasis together define tumor behavior.

Differentiation and anaplasia

  • Differentiation = how closely tumor cells resemble the normal cells/tissue from which they arose.
  • Benign tumors and well-differentiated malignant tumors often resemble normal tissue.
  • As a tumor becomes poorly differentiated, the tissue of origin becomes harder to recognize.
  • Anaplasia = lack/absence of differentiation.
Lecture pp. 25–27.
Lecture p. 27 — well-differentiated adenocarcinoma forms recognizable glands; poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma may show few or no obvious glands.

Nuclear features of malignancy

FeatureBenign tendencyMalignant tendency
N:C ratioNear normalOften increased
MitosesFew and normalIncreased; may be atypical
PleomorphismMinimalVariation in nuclear/cell size and shape
HyperchromasiaNot prominentDarkly staining nuclei

Lecture pp. 28–30.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Anaplasia pattern

Think pleomorphism + hyperchromatic enlarged nuclei + high N:C ratio + prominent nucleoli + atypical mitoses + loss of polarity. These are classic morphologic clues to poorly differentiated malignancy.

Dysplasia

  • Disordered epithelial growth.
  • Loss of normal orientation and maturation.
  • Changes remain confined to the epithelium.
  • No breach of the basement membrane.
Lecture p. 31.
Lecture p. 31 — dysplastic squamous epithelium remains above the basement membrane.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — Dysplasia, carcinoma in situ, invasive carcinoma

Dysplasia: atypical/disordered epithelial maturation, no invasion.
Carcinoma in situ: severe/full-thickness epithelial dysplasia with an intact basement membrane.
Invasive carcinoma: malignant cells have breached the basement membrane and entered underlying stroma.

Growth rate

  • Benign tumors are generally slow growing.
  • Malignant tumors have a variable growth rate but high-grade/anaplastic tumors often grow rapidly.
  • The lecture contrasts a slow-growing leiomyoma with a rapidly growing leiomyosarcoma.
Lecture pp. 32–33.

Local invasion and tumor margins

  • Most benign tumors do not invade and are grossly well-circumscribed.
  • Malignant tumors often invade surrounding tissue and appear infiltrative/ill-defined.
  • There are exceptions, so margin appearance alone is not absolute.
Lecture pp. 34–35.
Lecture p. 35 — benign tumors tend to be well-defined/circumscribed; malignant tumors tend to be ill-defined/infiltrative.
🔥 LECTURE EMPHASIS — Invasion is one of the strongest malignant features

The lecture states that, next to metastasis, invasion is the most reliable feature distinguishing benign or premalignant epithelial lesions from malignancy.

Lecture p. 36 — carcinoma in situ retains an intact basement membrane; invasive carcinoma breaches it.

Metastasis and Dissemination of Cancer

Why metastasis matters

  • Metastasis generally identifies a tumor as malignant.
  • Metastatic disease markedly reduces the likelihood of cure.
  • It substantially increases cancer morbidity and mortality.
Lecture p. 37.

Three routes of dissemination in the lecture

  1. Direct seeding of body cavities and surfaces — lecture example: ovarian cancer with peritoneal seeding.
  2. Lymphatic spread — lecture example: breast carcinoma metastasis to lymph nodes.
  3. Hematogenous spread — lecture example: distant lung involvement via blood-borne spread.
Lecture pp. 38–39.
Lecture p. 39 — the three major dissemination routes illustrated with ovarian, breast, and hematogenous examples.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Typical route patterns

Carcinomas commonly spread via lymphatics, while sarcomas commonly spread hematogenously. Many tumors can use more than one route, so these are patterns rather than absolute rules. Ovarian carcinoma is a classic example of transcoelomic/peritoneal seeding.

LO3 — Clinical Presentation, Staging, and Grading of Cancer

Core idea: tumors may be detected by screening, symptoms, or incidentally; once diagnosed, stage describes anatomic extent while grade describes microscopic aggressiveness/differentiation.

How tumors come to medical attention

RouteLecture examples / principles
ScreeningUseful when earlier detection reduces mortality, screening itself has acceptable risk, and the disease is treatable. Methods may include laboratory testing, radiology, pathology, and clinical examination.
Symptoms“B symptoms” such as fever/night sweats, obstructive symptoms, bleeding, and weight loss.
IncidentalTumor discovered while imaging/examining a patient for another reason.
Lecture pp. 40–43.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — B symptoms

The classic lymphoma “B symptoms” are unexplained fever, drenching night sweats, and significant unintentional weight loss. In lymphoma they can contribute to staging/prognostic classification.

Staging

  • The lecture identifies the AJCC as defining staging for most cancers.
  • Distinguishes clinical stage from pathologic stage.
  • Most solid tumors use a version of TNM staging.
Lecture p. 44.
TNM componentWhat it measures
T — TumorPrimary tumor size and/or depth/extent of local invasion; exact criteria are cancer-specific.
N — NodeRegional lymph-node involvement; may incorporate number of nodes, size of deposits, and/or extranodal extension.
M — MetastasisPresence of distant metastatic disease. The lecture presents distant metastasis as the key “M” variable.
Lecture pp. 45–47.
Lecture p. 48 — colorectal cancer illustrates how T, N, and M are combined into an overall AJCC stage.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Stage vs grade

Stage = where the cancer is / how far it has spread.
Grade = what the tumor looks like microscopically / how differentiated or aggressive it appears.
For many solid cancers, stage is the strongest general predictor of prognosis.

Clinical vs pathologic stage

  • Clinical stage is based on information available before definitive surgery, including history, examination, imaging, endoscopy, and biopsy.
  • Pathologic stage incorporates examination of the resected tumor and lymph nodes after surgery and is often more anatomically precise.
The lecture explicitly distinguishes clinical from pathologic stage on p. 44; the explanatory detail above is Step 1 enrichment.

Grading

  • Grade usually refers to microscopic appearance of tumor cells.
  • Not every tumor type has a clinically meaningful or standardized grading system.
  • Some tumors do have well-defined grading systems that affect prognosis or management.
  • Examples in the lecture include FIGO grading for endometrial cancer, Nottingham grade for breast cancer, and Fuhrman grade for kidney cancer.
Lecture p. 49.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — Grade is not stage

A small, poorly differentiated tumor can be high grade but low stage. A well-differentiated tumor that has spread widely can be low grade but high stage. These describe different dimensions of disease.
