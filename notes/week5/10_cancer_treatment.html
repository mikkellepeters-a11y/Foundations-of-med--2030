Framework - Three Pillars of Cancer Treatment

Lecture p. 3 - surgery and radiation are primarily local/regional therapies, whereas pharmacotherapy is systemic.
ModalityTypical reachTypical role
SurgeryLocal/regionalMajor primary treatment for many early-stage solid tumors; diagnostic, curative, preventive, palliative, and reconstructive uses.
RadiationLocal/regional, with selected systemic exceptionsDefinitive, adjuvant, neoadjuvant, palliative, emergent, or combined-modality treatment.
PharmacotherapySystemicPrimary treatment for hematologic malignancies and metastatic solid tumors; adjunct in early-stage disease.
🩺 CLINICAL CONNECTION - Treatment intent

Treatment may aim for cure, prevention, control/prolongation of life, or palliation. The lecture emphasizes multidisciplinary care that weighs tumor factors, treatment factors, patient factors, and psychological factors.

LO1 - Surgery: Rationales and Indications

Core idea: surgery is most effective when disease can be meaningfully controlled by removing a local or regional tumor burden.
Lecture p. 7 - slow-growing, lower-growth-fraction tumors with low metastatic potential are emphasized as especially amenable to curative surgery.

Major types of cancer surgery

Lecture p. 9 - curative lumpectomy, diagnostic core biopsy, palliative surgery for a fungating tumor, and reconstructive surgery.
TypePurposeLecture / board example
ProphylacticReduce future cancer risk in a high-risk organ or syndrome.FAP colectomy; risk-reducing breast/ovarian surgery in selected BRCA carriers.
DiagnosticObtain tissue for cytology/histology.FNA, core biopsy, incisional biopsy, excisional biopsy.
Definitive / curativeRemove localized tumor with adequate margins.Resection of an early-stage solid tumor.
CytoreductiveReduce bulky tumor to improve control by other modalities.Selected advanced tumors.
PalliativeRelieve symptoms rather than cure.Control bleeding, pain, obstruction, or a fungating mass.
Reconstructive / rehabilitativeRestore function or appearance after cancer therapy.Breast reconstruction.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - Negative biopsy

The handout emphasizes that a positive biopsy is definitive, whereas a negative biopsy may reflect either no cancer or a nonrepresentative sample. Persistent high clinical suspicion can require repeat sampling.

⭐ STEP 1 CONNECTION - Local vs systemic disease

Surgery works best when cancer is anatomically resectable and primarily local. Widespread metastatic disease is generally a systemic problem, although surgery can still have selected palliative, cytoreductive, or oligometastatic roles.

LO2 - Radiotherapy: Rationale, Indications, and Biological Effect

How ionizing radiation kills cancer cells

Lecture p. 12 - ionizing radiation causes direct DNA damage and indirect ROS-mediated injury.
  • Direct effect: ionization damages DNA, producing single- and double-strand breaks.
  • Indirect effect: radiation interacts with water and other molecules to generate free radicals and reactive oxygen species, which then damage DNA, proteins, and membranes.
  • The handout identifies double-strand DNA breaks as especially lethal.
  • Radiation ultimately causes DNA damage, genomic instability, loss of reproductive capacity, and cell death.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Oxygen effect

Oxygen enhances radiation injury by stabilizing free-radical DNA damage. Hypoxic tumor cells are relatively radioresistant; this helps explain why reoxygenation during a fractionated course improves treatment effectiveness.

Radiosensitivity and the cell cycle

Lecture p. 14 - marked HY. Late G2 and M show greatest radiosensitivity; repair by homologous recombination contributes to relative resistance in later S/early G2.
  • Rapidly proliferating tissues are generally more radiosensitive than quiescent tissues.
  • Late G2 and M phase are the most radiosensitive phases emphasized in the handout.
  • Late S / early G2 are relatively radioresistant, with homologous recombination available for accurate double-strand break repair.

Common indications

  • Definitive / curative: e.g., selected lung cancers treated with SBRT.
  • Adjuvant: improve local control after surgery, such as close/high-risk margins.
  • Neoadjuvant: improve operability or organ preservation.
  • Concurrent with chemotherapy: synergy and organ preservation in selected cancers.
  • Palliative: relieve cancer pain or other local symptoms.
  • Emergent: spinal cord compression.
  • Preventive/prophylactic: selected settings such as prophylactic cranial irradiation.

LO3 - Radiation Modalities, Fractionation, and Side Effects

Radiation modalities and terms

Lecture p. 15 - EBRT, brachytherapy, SRS/SBRT, TBI, systemic radioisotopes, and WBI.
ModalityCore concept
EBRTRadiation source is outside the patient; most commonly delivered with a linear accelerator.
BrachytherapyRadioactive source is placed in or near the tumor, allowing a concentrated local dose.
SRS / SBRTHighly precise treatment delivering a large dose to a defined target while sparing surrounding tissue.
TBITotal-body irradiation used in selected stem-cell-transplant conditioning regimens.
Systemic radiationTargeted radioisotopes such as iodine-131 in thyroid disease or bone-seeking radioisotopes for skeletal metastases.

Fractionated radiotherapy - HIGH YIELD

Lecture p. 16 - marked HY. The handout specifically says to understand the rationale rather than memorize the names of the “R’s.”
🔥 PROFESSOR EMPHASIS

Do not merely memorize “5 R’s.” Understand why dividing the total radiation dose into smaller fractions can improve the therapeutic ratio: maximize tumor injury while allowing more recovery of normal tissue.

ConceptWhy fractionation helps
RepairNormal cells can repair sublethal DNA damage between fractions more effectively than many tumor cells.
RedistributionCells move through the cell cycle; a tumor cell resistant during one fraction may enter a more radiosensitive phase before the next.
ReoxygenationKilling better-oxygenated peripheral cells can improve oxygen delivery to previously hypoxic, radioresistant tumor cells.
RepopulationNormal tissue recovery reduces toxicity, but excessive treatment delay can also permit tumor-cell regrowth.
RadiosensitivityDifferent tissues and cell states have different intrinsic sensitivities to ionizing radiation.

Radiation adverse effects

Lecture p. 18 - acute vs late toxicity varies by organ and by the tissue included in the treatment field.
⭐ GENERAL RULE

Rapidly proliferating tissues tend to show earlier toxicity: skin, mucosa, GI epithelium, and bone marrow. Late effects can appear months to years later and may include fibrosis/scarring, vascular injury, organ dysfunction, infertility, cognitive effects, and rarely secondary malignancy.

LO4 - Chemotherapy: Rationales and Indications

Core idea: systemic chemotherapy exploits differences in growth and survival between malignant and normal cells, but has a relatively narrow therapeutic window.
Lecture p. 21 - treatment should be individualized using tumor features, predictive/prognostic factors, performance status, symptoms, and patient goals.

Common treatment roles

RoleMeaning
CurativeChemotherapy is capable of eradicating selected malignancies, especially some hematologic tumors and germ-cell tumors.
NeoadjuvantGiven before local therapy to shrink tumor or improve operability.
AdjuvantGiven after local treatment to eradicate micrometastatic residual disease and reduce recurrence risk.
ConcurrentGiven with another modality, often radiation, to increase efficacy.
InductionInitial intensive treatment to produce remission or major disease reduction.
ConsolidationAdditional therapy after response/remission to deepen or maintain disease control.
Palliative / salvageImprove symptoms, quality of life, progression-free survival, or overall survival when cure is not expected.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

Palliative treatment is not the same as hospice. Palliative anticancer therapy can still be active disease-directed treatment intended to reduce symptoms or extend life.

LO5 - Pulsed Intermittent Chemotherapy, Drug Classification, and Toxicities

Pulsed intermittent chemotherapy - HIGH YIELD

Lecture p. 22 - marked HY. Cycles are separated by recovery intervals for normal tissue.
  • Cytotoxic drugs often work best against rapidly dividing cells.
  • Repeated treatment cycles are needed because one treatment does not eradicate every malignant cell.
  • Intervals allow normal cells, especially marrow and GI epithelium, to recover.
  • The lecture emphasizes using the highest tolerable doses at the shortest effective intervals, because modest dose reductions or prolonged delays can reduce tumor cell kill.
  • Traditional cytotoxic dosing is commonly based on body surface area (mg/m²).

Log cell-kill model

Lecture p. 23 - a chemotherapy dose kills a constant fraction of susceptible cells, not a fixed number.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Constant fraction

If a regimen produces a 3-log kill, it reduces the susceptible tumor population by 99.9% each time. For example, 10¹² cells → 10⁹ → 10⁶ with repeated equally effective cycles. This is why multiple cycles are required.

Cell-cycle-specific vs cell-cycle-nonspecific agents

Phase-nonspecificAlkylating agents, anthracyclines/selected antitumor antibioticsCan act across multiple phases including G0; classically more dose dependent.
Lecture p. 27 - marked HY. Major mechanisms map onto nucleotide synthesis, DNA damage, topoisomerases, microtubules, signaling, and differentiation.

Combination chemotherapy

Lecture p. 29 - choose active drugs with different mechanisms/resistance patterns and non-overlapping dose-limiting toxicities.
  • Each agent should have meaningful single-agent activity against the tumor.
  • Use different mechanisms of action and ideally different resistance mechanisms.
  • Choose agents with non-overlapping dose-limiting toxicities when possible.
  • Combination therapy can increase fractional cell kill and reduce the likelihood that one resistant clone dominates.
⭐ STEP 1 CONNECTION - Resistance

Resistance may be primary (innate), secondary (acquired), or multidrug. Common board mechanisms include altered drug targets, increased DNA repair, decreased drug activation, increased drug efflux, or apoptosis resistance.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - Carboplatin vs cisplatin

The lecture groups platinum toxicities together. For Step 1, cisplatin is the classic association with nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, while carboplatin is generally more associated with myelosuppression.

General toxicity principles

  • Cytotoxic chemotherapy has a narrow therapeutic window.
  • Rapidly dividing normal tissues are common collateral targets: marrow, GI mucosa, reproductive tissues, and hair follicles.
  • Toxicity depends on drug, dose, schedule, route, organ function, and patient characteristics.
  • The lecture introduces CTCAE grading from Grade 1 mild → Grade 5 death.

Oncologic Emergencies - Explicitly High Yield

1. Neutropenic fever

Lecture p. 34 - marked HY. Febrile neutropenia is treated as an oncologic emergency because infection can progress rapidly in a patient with impaired neutrophil defenses.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD

In a chemotherapy patient with fever and significant neutropenia, obtain cultures promptly and give empiric broad-spectrum antipseudomonal antibiotics without waiting for culture results in a high-risk presentation.

2. Tumor lysis syndrome

Lecture p. 35 - marked HY. Rapid tumor-cell breakdown causes the characteristic electrolyte pattern.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Prevention/treatment concept

High-risk patients need aggressive hydration and uric-acid control. Allopurinol prevents formation of new uric acid; rasburicase lowers existing uric acid by converting it to a more soluble metabolite.
