Foundational Molecular Techniques

The lecture begins with several core laboratory processes that underlie nearly every technique that follows.

Nucleic acid isolation

  • DNA and RNA normally interact with proteins inside cells.
  • Isolation involves cell disruption, separation of nucleic acids from proteins using denaturing/chaotropic conditions, extraction of proteins into an organic phase such as chloroform, and alcohol precipitation of nucleic acids from the aqueous phase.
  • Nucleic acids can be quantified by absorbance at 260 nm.
Lecture p. 3

Restriction endonucleases

  • Restriction enzymes recognize short DNA sequences, typically about 4–6 nucleotides long.
  • Recognition sites are often palindromic.
  • They cleave phosphodiester bonds in both DNA strands and can generate:
    • Blunt ends – straight cuts.
    • Sticky ends – staggered cuts producing 3′ or 5′ overhangs.
  • Complementary sticky ends from DNA fragments cut with the same enzyme can base-pair, allowing recombinant DNA formation.
Lecture p. 3
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Restriction enzymes

Restriction sites are useful because a sequence change can create or destroy a restriction site. That principle is the basis of RFLP analysis. Also remember: restriction enzymes cut DNA, whereas DNA ligase seals the phosphodiester backbone after compatible fragments base-pair.

Gel electrophoresis

  • Nucleic acids are separated mainly by size/length in an electric field.
  • Shorter fragments migrate faster through the gel than longer fragments.
  • Agarose is used routinely for larger DNA fragments and RNA.
  • Polyacrylamide can resolve fragments differing by as little as a single nucleotide, which is useful for sequencing.
  • DNA bands can be visualized with intercalating dyes such as ethidium bromide under UV light.
Lecture p. 4
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

Agarose = routine separation of larger nucleic-acid fragments. Polyacrylamide = much higher resolution, including one-nucleotide differences.

Reverse transcription, transfection, and transduction

  • Reverse transcriptase is an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase that copies RNA into DNA, producing cDNA.
  • cDNA made from mature mRNA does not contain introns and also lacks genomic regulatory regions such as promoters and enhancers.
  • Reverse transcriptase requires a primer. A poly(dT) primer can anneal to the poly(A) tail of many mRNAs.
  • Transfection = deliberate introduction of purified/recombinant “naked” DNA into eukaryotic cells.
  • Transduction = viral delivery of nucleic acid into cells. The lecture emphasizes that this is generally more efficient and can permit tissue-specific delivery.
Lecture p. 4
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Why cDNA matters

Bacteria cannot perform normal eukaryotic intron splicing. Therefore, if the goal is to express a human protein in bacteria, the preferred template is usually cDNA made from mature mRNA, not genomic DNA.

Molecular cloning

Molecular cloning amplifies a DNA sequence by placing the desired DNA into a replicating vector and allowing a host cell, usually a bacterium, to copy it.

  1. Obtain the foreign DNA: either a genomic DNA fragment or cDNA.
  2. Cut the foreign DNA and vector with the same restriction enzyme.
  3. Complementary sticky ends base-pair.
  4. DNA ligase covalently joins the fragments to produce recombinant DNA.
  5. Introduce the vector into a host cell.
  6. As the host replicates, the vector and inserted DNA are amplified.
Lecture p. 6

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

PCR is an in vitro method for amplifying a specific DNA segment. The nucleotide sequence at the two ends of the target region must be known so that primers can be designed.

StepWhat happensLecture temperature range
1. DenaturationDouble-stranded DNA separates into single strands.High temperature
2. AnnealingShort primers hybridize to complementary sequences flanking the target.~45–72°C
3. ExtensionThermostable DNA polymerase extends primers to synthesize new DNA.~68–72°C
  • The two primers must flank the sequence with their 3′ ends directed toward each other.
  • A thermostable polymerase from a thermophilic bacterium is used because the reaction repeatedly reaches high temperatures.
  • Very small quantities of starting DNA are sufficient, making PCR useful in clinical and forensic samples.
  • PCR products can be used for sequencing, cloning, RFLP analysis, STR analysis, and other downstream tests.
Lecture p. 7
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — PCR essentials
  • DNA polymerase synthesizes DNA 5′ → 3′ and therefore extends from the primer’s free 3′-OH.
  • Classic PCR uses a thermostable polymerase such as Taq polymerase.
  • Under ideal conditions, the target amount approximately doubles each cycle, giving exponential amplification.
  • If primers face away from each other, they will not amplify the intended intervening segment.

RT-PCR

Reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) begins with RNA. The RNA is first reverse transcribed into cDNA, and that cDNA is then amplified by PCR. The lecture emphasizes its use in detecting or measuring gene expression.

mRNA → reverse transcriptase → cDNA → PCR amplification

Lecture p. 7
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — RT-PCR vs qPCR

RT-PCR refers to reverse-transcription PCR: RNA is converted to cDNA first. qPCR refers to real-time quantitative PCR, which monitors amplification during cycling. A test can combine both approaches and be called RT-qPCR.

Reporter gene assay

  • Used to study cis- and trans-acting regulatory elements and the effect of regulatory mutations on gene expression.
  • A fusion gene is created by combining the regulatory region of the test gene with the coding region of a reporter gene.
  • Reporter expression is measured visually or by an enzyme assay.
Lecture p. 8

LO2 — Hybridization and Blotting Techniques

Objective: Understand how complementary base pairing and labeled probes are used to detect specific nucleic acids or proteins.

Hybridization principle

  • Hybridization = complementary nucleic-acid strands base-pair with each other.
  • A probe is a labeled nucleic-acid strand that can identify its complementary target.
  • Probes can be labeled with radioactivity such as 32P or with fluorescent dyes.
  • Probes may be cDNA, genomic DNA fragments, or synthetic oligonucleotides.
Lecture p. 10

High temperature + low salt = high stringency. This favors highly specific, nearly perfect complementarity. Low temperature + high salt = low stringency, allowing more mismatches.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — SNoW DRoP

A classic mnemonic: Southern = DNA, Northern = RNA, Western = Protein.

Clinical integration from the lecture

The lecture uses allele-specific oligonucleotide probes in a cystic fibrosis family. One probe recognizes the normal CFTR sequence and another recognizes the common ΔF508 mutant sequence. A person binding only the normal probe is normal for that tested allele, a person binding both probes is heterozygous/carrier, and a person binding only the mutant probe is homozygous for the tested mutation.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Northern blot and alternative splicing

Different mRNA band sizes from the same gene can reflect alternative splicing. That is why one gene may produce multiple transcripts and protein isoforms.

LO3 — Sanger Sequencing and Methylation Analysis

Objective: Understand chain termination and how bisulfite treatment distinguishes methylated from unmethylated cytosine.

Sanger chain-termination sequencing

The lecture describes classic Sanger sequencing using DNA polymerase, a primer, normal dNTPs, and labeled 2′,3′-dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs).

  1. DNA polymerase begins extending a primer on the template.
  2. A ddNTP occasionally competes with the corresponding normal dNTP and is incorporated.
  3. Once a ddNTP is incorporated, elongation stops.
  4. This generates DNA fragments of many lengths, each ending at a particular base.
  5. Fragments are separated by high-resolution polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis.
  6. For the classic four-lane gel, read the bands from bottom to top to obtain the newly synthesized strand in the 5′ → 3′ direction.
Lecture p. 14
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Why ddNTPs terminate DNA synthesis

A ddNTP lacks the 3′-OH needed to form the next phosphodiester bond. No 3′-OH means no further nucleotide can be added, so the chain terminates.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — Which strand are you reading?

The bands directly give the sequence of the newly synthesized strand in the 5′ → 3′ direction. If the question asks for the template strand, take the reverse complement.

Whole-genome vs whole-exome sequencing

MethodWhat is sequenced?Key implication
Whole-genome sequencingThe entire genomeCan detect variants in coding and noncoding regions
Whole-exome sequencingProtein-coding exons (~2% of genome, as emphasized in lecture)Efficient for coding mutations but may miss pathogenic regulatory/noncoding variants

The lecture also notes that exome sequencing can identify actionable mutations in cancer that may be targeted therapeutically.

Lecture p. 14

Bisulfite sequencing for DNA methylation

Standard Sanger sequencing does not inherently distinguish methylated from unmethylated cytosine. Bisulfite treatment creates a chemical difference that can be detected by sequencing.

Unmethylated C → bisulfite treatment → U → read as T during sequencing

Methylated C (5-methylcytosine) → resistant to conversion → remains C

Comparing treated and untreated DNA reveals methylation patterns.

Lecture p. 15
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — DNA methylation
  • Promoter-region CpG methylation generally decreases gene transcription.
  • Methylation is central to genomic imprinting, in which expression depends on whether an allele was inherited from the mother or father.
  • A methylated cytosine remains read as C after bisulfite treatment; an unmethylated cytosine becomes read as T.

LO4 — RFLP, VNTR, STR, Disease Diagnosis, Paternity, and Forensics

Objective: Understand how inherited DNA sequence variation changes fragment sizes or repeat lengths and how those differences can identify alleles or individuals.

Polymorphisms, SNPs, and INDELs

  • A polymorphism is one of multiple DNA-sequence variants at a locus.
  • A SNP is a single-base variation.
  • INDELs are insertions or deletions.
  • Some sequence variants create or destroy a restriction enzyme recognition site; these are detectable by RFLP.
Lecture p. 16

Restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP)

RFLP detects sequence changes that alter a restriction site, producing different-sized restriction fragments.

  1. Isolate or PCR-amplify genomic DNA.
  2. Digest with the relevant restriction enzyme.
  3. Separate fragments by gel electrophoresis.
  4. Perform Southern blotting with a gene-specific probe.
  5. Interpret the fragment-size pattern.

The lecture uses sickle-cell allele genotyping as an example.

Lecture p. 16
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — RFLP limitation

RFLP only works directly when the sequence change creates or abolishes a restriction site. Not every SNP or INDEL does this.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Paternity logic

At each autosomal STR locus, an individual inherits one allele from each parent. A candidate parent must be able to account for the child’s allele that did not come from the known parent. Multiple independent STR loci are combined to make the identification highly specific.

Forensic application in the lecture

The lecture uses STR analysis of the Romanov family remains as an example of how inherited repeat-length patterns can be used to establish biological relationships and identity.

LO5 — Gene Therapy and Vectors

Objective: Have an overall understanding of how gene therapy works and know the major vector categories. The lecturer specifically states not to memorize minute details for this objective.

Basic concept of gene therapy

Gene therapy introduces or manipulates genetic material in a patient’s cells to treat or prevent disease. Approaches described in the lecture include:

  • Replacing a defective gene with a functional copy.
  • Inactivating/knocking out a harmful mutant gene.
  • Adding a new functional copy of a defective gene.
  • Exon-skipping therapy to alter RNA splicing.

Exon-skipping therapy

Splice-site mutations can cause abnormal exon recognition and disrupt the reading frame. Exon-skipping therapy uses antisense oligonucleotides (AONs) to bind specific pre-mRNA sequences and alter splice-site recognition.

Frame-disrupting transcript → AON masks selected splice region → extra exon is skipped → reading frame restored → shorter but potentially functional protein

  • AONs are short, single-stranded nucleic-acid sequences that bind complementary RNA.
  • The goal is not necessarily to restore the full-length protein. It is to create an in-frame truncated transcript that retains important functional domains.
Lecture pp. 21–23
Duchenne muscular dystrophy example

The lecture’s page 26 diagram shows how exon skipping can convert an out-of-frame dystrophin transcript into an in-frame transcript. This permits production of a shorter, partially functional dystrophin, producing a phenotype conceptually more like Becker muscular dystrophy than classic Duchenne.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Duchenne vs Becker mechanism

Duchenne: typically out-of-frame or severe loss-of-function dystrophin mutation → little/absent functional dystrophin. Becker: often in-frame mutation → shortened but partially functional dystrophin. Exon-skipping therapy exploits this reading-frame principle.

Professor emphasis — low priority

The lecture explicitly states that the detailed newer splicing regulatory elements on p. 27 are not required for the quiz or Step I. Know the basic exon-skipping concept rather than memorizing ESE/ESS/ISE/ISS details.

Vectors for gene therapy

A therapeutic gene generally requires a vector for efficient delivery. The lecture divides vectors into nonviral and viral types.

  • Nonviral vectors: lower transfection efficiency.
  • Viral vectors: high delivery efficiency and sometimes tissue specificity.
  • Viral vectors are engineered to remove virulence/replication functions while retaining the ability to deliver the therapeutic sequence.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Vector pattern recognition
  • Integrating retroviral/lentiviral vectors can provide durable expression but raise concern for insertional mutagenesis.
  • Adenovirus is strongly immunogenic and typically gives more transient expression because it remains episomal/extrachromosomal.
  • AAV is less immunogenic and useful for long-term expression but has a small packaging capacity.

Risks of viral gene therapy

  • Difficulty restricting the vector to the desired tissue.
  • Insertional mutagenesis if integration occurs in a harmful location.
  • Potential oncogenesis.
  • Excessive expression of the therapeutic gene.
  • Host immune response against the vector or transduced cells.

The lecture gives the historical example of X-linked SCID patients who developed T-cell leukemia after retroviral insertional mutagenesis.

Lecture p. 30
Professor emphasis — overall understanding only

The lecture explicitly says: do not spend time memorizing minute details for the final learning objective. Know the overall logic of gene therapy, the major vector differences, and the major risks.

CRISPR/Cas9 — supplemental lecture material

The lecture labels CRISPR/Cas9 as additional information that is not tested. The basic concept is that guide RNA directs Cas9 to a complementary DNA sequence, where Cas9 creates a double-strand break. Repair by NHEJ can create insertions/deletions, whereas a supplied homologous template can support homology-directed repair.
