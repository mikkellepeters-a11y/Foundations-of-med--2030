Cancer Screening and Diagnosis

Giri Raval • Wednesday Lecture 1 • Week 5: Foundations of Cancer Biology

🔥 Professor-emphasis map

I treated the lecture's repeated concepts, boxed definitions, comparison slides, and emphasized screening examples as priority material.

  • A good screening test must improve meaningful outcomes, especially cancer-specific mortality, rather than simply detect disease earlier.
  • Sensitivity and specificity are core screening-test characteristics. Poor sensitivity produces false negatives; poor specificity produces false positives.
  • Know PPV and NPV and how prevalence changes predictive value.
  • Lead-time bias and length-time bias are the major limitations of screening emphasized in the lecture.
  • Know the major screening modalities for breast, lung, colorectal, cervical, and prostate cancer as presented in the lecture.
  • Low-dose CT is the lung-cancer screening modality for eligible high-risk patients.
  • Clinical presentation matters: the official objectives specifically require breast, lung, colon, and prostate cancer.
  • Cancer diagnosis is confirmed through pathology, with IHC, molecular testing, liquid biopsy, and emerging AI/NGS approaches adding classification and treatment information.
  • Diagnosis and staging are different: diagnosis tells you what the tumor is; staging determines how far it has spread.

LO1 - Risk/Reward of Screening & How to Assess Efficacy

What makes a disease suitable for screening?

Lecture p. 6 - principles of population screening.
  • A recognizable latent or early symptomatic stage should exist.
  • A suitable screening test should have sufficient accuracy.
  • There should be an accepted treatment for disease found early.
  • Screening should change the disease trajectory, ideally reducing cancer-specific mortality.
  • The test should be acceptable to the population.
  • Confirmatory diagnostic and treatment facilities must be available.
  • Cost should be reasonable relative to overall healthcare benefit.
  • Screening is a continuous process, not a one-time intervention.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD

Screening is applied to asymptomatic people to detect possible disease early. Diagnostic testing follows symptoms, signs, or an abnormal screening test and attempts to establish whether disease is actually present.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV

Lecture p. 8 - sensitivity and specificity definitions.
MeasureMeaningWhen low
SensitivityAmong people who truly have disease, proportion testing positive.More false negatives.
SpecificityAmong people who truly do not have disease, proportion testing negative.More false positives.
PPVIf the test is positive, probability the patient truly has disease.Lower when prevalence is low.
NPVIf the test is negative, probability the patient truly does not have disease.Lower when prevalence is high.
⭐ STEP 1 MEMORY AID

SnNout: highly sensitive test + negative result helps rule OUT disease.
SpPin: highly specific test + positive result helps rule IN disease.

⭐ PREVALENCE EFFECT

As disease prevalence rises, PPV rises and NPV falls. Sensitivity and specificity are comparatively less dependent on prevalence.

Benefit is not the same as earlier diagnosis

The lecture repeatedly contrasts early vs advanced disease to show why early detection can reduce morbidity and improve the chance of effective treatment. But screening must improve meaningful outcomes rather than merely shift the date of diagnosis.

Lead-time bias

Lecture p. 15 - earlier diagnosis makes observed survival longer even when the date of death is unchanged.

Lead-time bias = apparent improvement in survival because screening moves diagnosis earlier, without actually delaying death.

⭐ CLASSIC QUESTION STEM

A screening test increases “5-year survival from diagnosis,” but disease-specific mortality does not change. Think lead-time bias.

Length-time bias

Lecture p. 16 - slowly growing tumors remain detectable longer and are therefore disproportionately found by screening.

Length-time bias = screening preferentially detects indolent, slowly progressing tumors because they spend longer in a screen-detectable preclinical phase.

⚠️ RELATED CONCEPT: OVERDIAGNOSIS

Screening can discover indolent disease that would never have become clinically important during the patient's lifetime. That can lead to unnecessary testing, treatment, anxiety, and complications.

LO2 - Common Cancer Screening Recommendations & the Data Behind Them

Breast cancer

Lecture p. 19 - USPSTF breast-screening slide.

The lecture highlights mammography as the main population-screening test and compares recommendations across organizations. The lecture's USPSTF slide states biennial mammography ages 40-74 and emphasizes the tradeoff between mortality benefit and screening harms.

🩺 CLINICAL GUIDELINE NOTE

The lecture's USPSTF framing is biennial mammography from age 40 through 74. Screening recommendations can change over time, so use the lecture value for the course unless your instructors specify otherwise.

Breast MRI: why not screen everyone with the most sensitive test?

Lecture p. 21 - MRI is highly sensitive but less specific and more costly/time-consuming; the lecture places it primarily in high-risk populations.
⚠️ HIGH SENSITIVITY ≠ BEST POPULATION TEST

A very sensitive test can still be a poor universal screening test if specificity is low, false positives are frequent, or the test is costly, burdensome, or leads to excessive downstream procedures.

Lung cancer

Lecture p. 24 - low-dose CT (LDCT) for lung cancer screening.

The lecture criteria are:

  • Age 50-80.
  • At least 20 pack-years.
  • Current smoker or quit within the last 15 years.
  • Asymptomatic.
  • Annual low-dose CT.
🩺 CLINICAL GUIDELINE NOTE

The lecture's high-risk lung-screening criteria are age 50-80, at least 20 pack-years, current smoking or quitting within 15 years, and annual LDCT. Use these lecture values for the course.

⭐ PACK-YEARS

Packs/day × years smoked. One pack/day for 20 years = 20 pack-years; two packs/day for 10 years = 20 pack-years.

Colorectal cancer

Lecture p. 27 - stool-based tests, CT colonography, and colonoscopy.

Major strategies include:

  • FIT / high-sensitivity stool blood testing.
  • Stool DNA-FIT.
  • CT colonography.
  • Flexible sigmoidoscopy.
  • Colonoscopy, which can detect and remove adenomatous polyps.
🩺 CLINICAL GUIDELINE NOTE

For board-style context, average-risk colorectal screening generally begins in mid-adulthood and can use stool-based testing or direct visualization. For your course, prioritize the specific intervals and modalities shown in the lecture.

⭐ WHY COLONOSCOPY IS SPECIAL

Colonoscopy can be both diagnostic and preventive: removing premalignant adenomas can interrupt the adenoma-carcinoma sequence.

Cervical cancer

Lecture p. 28 - cervical screening with Pap testing and HPV-based approaches.

The lecture presents cervical cancer screening around Pap cytology and HPV testing, reflecting the long preinvasive phase of HPV-associated cervical neoplasia.

🩺 CLINICAL GUIDELINE NOTE

Cervical screening recommendations evolve, but the lecture's key concept is that Pap cytology and HPV-based testing exploit the long preinvasive phase of HPV-associated cervical neoplasia.

Prostate cancer

Lecture p. 29 - PSA and digital rectal examination are shown in the prostate screening section.
🩺 CLINICAL GUIDELINE NOTE

The lecture presents PSA-based prostate screening as a risk-benefit decision rather than a simple universal test. The key concept is balancing potential early detection against false positives, overdiagnosis, biopsy, and treatment harms.

⭐ SCREENING HARM TO REMEMBER

PSA can be elevated by cancer but also by benign conditions. Screening can therefore cause false positives, biopsy, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, and treatment-related urinary or sexual complications.

LO3 - Clinical Presentation of Breast, Lung, Colon & Prostate Cancer

Lung cancer

Lecture p. 30 - lung cancer symptom overview.
  • Persistent/worsening cough.
  • Dyspnea.
  • Chest pain.
  • Hemoptysis/bleeding.
  • Loss of appetite and weight loss.
  • Generalized weakness.
  • Possible neck mass or symptoms from metastatic disease.
⭐ STEP 1 EXTENSION

Local invasion can produce hoarseness, dysphagia, or superior vena cava syndrome. Paraneoplastic syndromes depend on histologic subtype.

Breast cancer

Lecture p. 31 - retracted nipple, peau d'orange, inflammatory breast cancer, and metastatic symptoms.
  • Breast mass or structural change.
  • Nipple retraction.
  • Peau d'orange from dermal lymphatic obstruction.
  • Inflammatory breast cancer can cause diffuse erythema/edema.
  • Metastatic disease can present with weight loss, bone pain, CNS symptoms, or abdominal symptoms.

Colon cancer

Lecture p. 32 - bowel habit change, bleeding/dark stool, abdominal discomfort/fullness, fatigue, and weight loss.
  • Change in bowel habits.
  • Rectal bleeding or dark/maroon stool.
  • Abdominal discomfort or fullness.
  • Fatigue and weight loss.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - RIGHT vs LEFT COLON

Right-sided colon cancers more often present with occult blood loss and iron-deficiency anemia. Left-sided lesions more often cause obstructive symptoms, change in stool caliber, or visible blood.

Prostate cancer

⚠️ SOURCE GAP

The official learning objective specifically includes prostate clinical presentation, but this lecture handout does not contain a dedicated prostate-presentation slide. The following is clearly separated board-style enrichment.

⭐ STEP 1 / NCI ENRICHMENT

Early prostate cancer is often asymptomatic. More advanced local disease can cause urinary symptoms such as weak stream, frequency, or difficulty emptying the bladder, although these are nonspecific and are also common in BPH. Metastatic prostate cancer classically spreads to bone and can present with persistent back, hip, or pelvic pain.

LO4 - How Cancer Is Confirmed: Pathology, Liquid Biopsy & the Future

Step 1: obtain tissue when possible

A suspicious mass or abnormal screening result usually requires a diagnostic evaluation. Depending on the site, this may involve image-guided biopsy, endoscopy/bronchoscopy, excisional biopsy, or another tissue-sampling technique.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Lecture p. 34 - diagnosis is finalized using morphology/histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC).
  • Histology establishes morphology and often identifies tumor type, differentiation, and invasion.
  • IHC detects proteins in tissue and helps classify lineage, site of origin, prognostic markers, and actionable targets.
  • Examples of clinically important IHC concepts include hormone receptors, HER2 protein expression, lineage markers, and mismatch-repair proteins.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD

A radiographic mass can strongly suggest cancer, but definitive diagnosis commonly depends on tissue pathology. Imaging answers “where and how much?”; pathology answers “what is it?”

Next-generation sequencing and AI

Lecture p. 35 - NGS can assist when histology/IHC do not conclusively establish tissue of origin and can identify molecular targets.

NGS can help:

  • Identify somatic driver mutations and actionable alterations.
  • Support classification of tumors with uncertain tissue of origin.
  • Guide targeted therapy and clinical-trial selection.

The lecture also introduces AI/deep-learning approaches such as tumor-origin assessment from histopathology. Treat this as an emerging extension of, not a replacement for, pathologist-guided diagnosis.

Liquid biopsy

Lecture p. 37 - liquid biopsy can analyze tumor-derived material such as circulating tumor DNA from blood.
Potential strengthLimitation
Minimally invasive and repeatable.May miss tumors that shed little DNA.
Can detect molecular alterations across multiple tumor sites.Does not provide full tissue architecture/morphology.
Useful for treatment selection, response monitoring, and resistance detection in some settings.A positive molecular result still requires clinical/pathologic interpretation.
⭐ FUTURE-DIRECTION CONCEPT

The “future” is increasingly integrated diagnosis: histology + IHC + molecular profiling + imaging + computational tools, rather than any single technology replacing all others.

Staging: after diagnosis, determine extent of disease

Lecture p. 38 - staging tools include CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, brain MRI, PET-CT, and selected tumor markers.

Once cancer is confirmed, staging estimates the anatomic extent of disease and directly affects prognosis and treatment planning. The lecture lists:

  • CT chest/abdomen/pelvis.
  • MRI brain when appropriate.
  • PET-CT.
  • Selected tumor markers.
⚠️ SCREENING vs DIAGNOSIS vs STAGING

Screening: asymptomatic population.
Diagnosis: establish whether cancer is present and what type it is.
Staging: determine how far a confirmed cancer has spread.

Rapid Step 1 / Course Review

Sensitivity

Disease present → how many test positive? Low sensitivity = false negatives.

Specificity

Disease absent → how many test negative? Low specificity = false positives.

PPV / NPV

Higher prevalence raises PPV and lowers NPV.

Lead-time bias

Earlier diagnosis appears to prolong survival without changing death.

Length-time bias

Screening preferentially detects slow-growing disease.

Breast

Mammography is core population screening; MRI is especially useful in selected high-risk groups.

Lung

High-risk smokers → annual low-dose CT; know pack-years.

Colon

Multiple options; colonoscopy can detect and remove premalignant polyps.

Cervix

Pap cytology and high-risk HPV testing exploit a long preinvasive phase.

Prostate

PSA screening requires weighing small potential benefit against overdiagnosis/overtreatment.

Pathology

Histology + IHC are central to definitive tumor classification.

NGS

Can identify drivers, targets, and sometimes support tissue-of-origin assessment.

Liquid biopsy

Minimally invasive molecular sampling; useful but does not replace tissue morphology in many settings.

Staging

After diagnosis, imaging determines disease extent and guides treatment.
