Individualizing Care of the Cancer Patient

Giri Raval • Wednesday Lecture 3 • Week 5: Foundations of Cancer Biology

🔥 Professor-emphasis map

This lecture is organized around the central question: why should two patients with “the same cancer” receive different treatment? I prioritized the concepts that recur across the slides and map directly to the official learning objectives.

  • Personalized medicine aims for better efficacy, appropriate dosing, fewer adverse effects, and better prognostication.
  • Histology is necessary but often not sufficient. Molecular information subdivides morphologically similar cancers into biologically different diseases.
  • Stratified medicine groups patients by biomarkers that predict response or prognosis; the lecture uses PD-L1 and immunotherapy as a major example.
  • NGS and liquid biopsy add molecular information that can guide therapy, but the lecture explicitly says tissue and liquid biopsy may both be needed.
  • Oncotype DX is the major lecture example of using gene-expression information to estimate recurrence risk and chemotherapy benefit in selected breast cancer.
  • Pharmacogenetics matters for toxicity: DPD/DPYD with fluoropyrimidines, UGT1A1 with irinotecan, and G6PD deficiency are the major lecture examples.
  • Genomic classification changes prognosis. AML morphology alone does not capture the survival differences created by cytogenetic/molecular subgroups.
  • Somatic alterations can be actionable. Targeted therapy aims at tumor-specific pathways rather than broadly damaging rapidly dividing cells.
  • Hormone receptor and inherited genetic information can alter treatment selection and risk-reduction strategies.
  • Cellular and immune therapies are presented as highly individualized modern approaches, including CAR-T, TIL therapy, checkpoint inhibitors, and allogeneic stem-cell transplantation.

Learning Objectives

  1. Explain the clonal selection hypothesis in cancer initiation and growth and explain briefly the concepts underlying individualized therapy for solid and liquid malignancies.
  2. Identify the role of histology and molecular analysis in cancer treatment planning.
  3. Recognize genetic factors that influence drug metabolism and adverse drug reactions in specific populations.
  4. Understand the clinical significance of somatic mutations in cancer for selecting targeted therapies.
  5. Explain how genetic information, such as BRCA1 mutation and hormone receptor status, influences individualized cancer treatment strategies.
  6. Briefly discuss newer cancer treatment options: targeted therapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy.

LO1 - Clonal Selection & Why Therapy Must Be Individualized

Core idea: a cancer is not a uniform collection of identical cells. Tumors evolve, contain genetically distinct subclones, and change under selective pressure from the immune system, microenvironment, and treatment.
Lecture p. 2 - “one treatment fits all” is contrasted with increasingly individualized diagnostics and treatment.

Why personalize cancer treatment?

  • Better efficacy.
  • Choose an appropriate dose.
  • Reduce adverse effects.
  • Improve prognostic estimation.

The lecture first reminds you that treatment already varies by traditional clinical variables such as histology, age, weight, body surface area, renal function, and hepatic function. Precision oncology adds tumor genotype, gene expression, inherited pharmacogenetics, and other biomarkers.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Clonal selection

A tumor begins from a clone but accumulates additional mutations over time. Subclones with a survival advantage expand. Therapy creates a powerful selection pressure: sensitive clones die, while resistant clones can survive and become dominant. This explains acquired resistance and tumor evolution after treatment.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED - Personalized vs precision vs stratified

The lecture uses these terms in an overlapping way. Conceptually, stratified medicine places patients into biomarker-defined groups; precision medicine uses molecular and clinical data to select the best treatment for an individual; “personalized medicine” is the broad patient-centered umbrella.

Stratified medicine and immunotherapy response

Lecture p. 10 - PD-L1 strata are used to illustrate how a biomarker can divide one cancer population into groups with different expected immunotherapy responses.
Lecture p. 11 - NGS is used to add another layer of prediction; STK11 and KEAP1 alterations are shown as examples of mutations associated with differential immunotherapy response.
🩺 CLINICAL CONNECTION

A biomarker may be prognostic (tells you how the cancer is likely to behave) and/or predictive (tells you whether a particular therapy is more likely to work). Precision treatment planning depends on knowing which kind of information the marker provides.

LO2 - Histology and Molecular Analysis in Treatment Planning

Core idea: histology identifies the broad disease, while molecular testing can divide that disease into biologically and therapeutically distinct subtypes.

From morphology to molecular classification

The lecture repeatedly moves from traditional classification to molecular subclassification:

  • Histology still matters: adenocarcinoma, squamous carcinoma, leukemia subtype, etc.
  • Molecular testing can identify driver alterations, immune biomarkers, gene-expression patterns, and cytogenetic risk groups.
  • Two tumors with the same morphology may therefore receive different treatment.

Tissue biopsy + liquid biopsy

Lecture p. 16 - title explicitly states “Liquid biopsy - Need both for treatment.” Tissue supplies architecture/morphology; liquid biopsy can capture circulating tumor-derived molecular information.
Tissue biopsyLiquid biopsy
Provides morphology, architecture, histology, IHC, and direct tumor sampling.Can detect circulating tumor DNA/cells and molecular alterations from blood.
May be limited by accessibility and sampling only one tumor region.Minimally invasive and repeatable; may capture heterogeneity across sites.
Often essential to establish the diagnosis.Useful for mutation detection, monitoring, and resistance assessment in selected settings.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

Liquid biopsy does not simply replace tissue pathology. The lecture's message is complementary use: molecular blood testing can add information, while tissue still provides morphologic context.

Oncotype DX: gene expression guiding breast-cancer therapy

Lecture p. 18 - Oncotype DX is presented for selected newly diagnosed early-stage invasive breast cancers, especially hormone-receptor-positive/HER2-negative disease where chemotherapy benefit is uncertain.
Lecture p. 19 - the TAILORx design illustrates how recurrence-score groups can be used to decide whether endocrine therapy alone is adequate or whether chemotherapy adds benefit.
Lecture p. 21 - a recurrence score is linked to the expected incremental benefit of chemotherapy in younger patients with early-stage HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer.
⭐ STEP 1 / BOARD CONCEPT

Oncotype DX is a multigene expression assay, not a germline hereditary-cancer test. It estimates recurrence risk and potential chemotherapy benefit in selected breast cancers. Do not confuse it with BRCA testing.

AML: morphology is not enough

Lecture p. 33 - survival differs markedly across genomic AML subgroups, demonstrating why cytogenetic/molecular classification changes prognosis and treatment planning.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Classic AML genetics

Recognize major stable board associations: APL with PML-RARA t(15;17); core-binding-factor AML with t(8;21) or inv(16)/t(16;16). The lecture uses these examples to show how molecular classification predicts outcome.

LO3 - Genetic Factors Influencing Drug Metabolism and Adverse Reactions

Core idea: inherited variation can change how quickly a patient activates, detoxifies, or clears a drug, altering both efficacy and toxicity.

5-FU / fluoropyrimidines and DPD

Lecture p. 23 - DPD is highlighted as a major route of 5-FU catabolism; impaired DPD activity can increase exposure to toxic fluoropyrimidine metabolites.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - DPYD

DPYD encodes dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD). Reduced DPD activity can cause severe fluoropyrimidine toxicity, including profound myelosuppression, mucositis, diarrhea, and other systemic toxicity.

Irinotecan and UGT1A1

Lecture p. 24 - UGT1A1 inactivates SN-38, the active metabolite of irinotecan. Reduced UGT1A1 activity increases SN-38 exposure and toxicity, especially neutropenia and diarrhea.
⭐ STEP 1 CONNECTION - UGT1A1

UGT1A1 also conjugates bilirubin. Reduced activity is the biochemical basis of Gilbert syndrome and more severe UGT1A1 deficiency states. The same enzyme becomes pharmacologically important when clearing SN-38.

G6PD deficiency

Lecture p. 25 - reduced NADPH production limits reduced glutathione regeneration, making RBCs vulnerable to oxidant stress and hemolysis.
Lecture p. 27 - skewed X-inactivation is used to explain why a heterozygous female carrier of an X-linked trait can sometimes express a clinically important phenotype.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - G6PD

G6PD deficiency is X-linked recessive. Oxidant stress can cause episodic hemolysis with Heinz bodies and bite cells. The lecture's medication slide specifically includes rasburicase among drugs that can be unsafe in moderate-to-severe G6PD deficiency.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED

The pharmacogenetic principle is broader than memorizing one gene-drug pair: host genotype can change drug exposure even when the tumor genotype is identical. Tumor genetics predicts what to target; germline pharmacogenetics can predict how the patient handles the drug.

LO4 - Somatic Mutations and Selection of Targeted Therapy

Core idea: a somatic driver can create both a growth advantage for the tumor and a therapeutic vulnerability for the clinician.
Lecture p. 38 - standard chemotherapy broadly damages rapidly dividing cells, whereas targeted therapy is designed around a specific cancer-associated molecular pathway.

Targeted therapy logic

  1. Identify a molecular alteration or dependency in the tumor.
  2. Determine whether the alteration is a true driver/actionable target.
  3. Select a drug that blocks the relevant protein or pathway.
  4. Monitor response and emergence of resistant clones.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Stable target examples
Alteration / targetClassic therapy concept
BCR-ABLABL tyrosine-kinase inhibition, e.g., imatinib.
HER2 amplificationHER2-directed therapy, e.g., trastuzumab.
EGFR activating mutationEGFR tyrosine-kinase inhibition in selected lung adenocarcinoma.
BRAF V600EBRAF-pathway inhibition in appropriate tumors.
🩺 CLINICAL CONNECTION - Resistance

Targeted therapy can produce dramatic responses, but tumors may acquire secondary mutations, activate bypass pathways, or select pre-existing resistant subclones. This is the clonal-selection hypothesis applied directly to treatment.

LO5 - BRCA1, Hormone Receptors, and Individualized Treatment

Hormone receptor status

Lecture p. 39 - breast-cancer endocrine therapy is individualized by hormone-receptor biology using receptor blockade, ovarian suppression/ablation, aromatase inhibition, or receptor downregulation.

ER blockadeTamoxifen, toremifeneBlocks estrogen-receptor signaling.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Tamoxifen

Tamoxifen is a selective estrogen-receptor modulator: antagonist in breast, but partial agonist in endometrium and bone. Classic risks include endometrial carcinoma and venous thromboembolism.

BRCA1/2 information

The official LO specifically asks how inherited information such as BRCA1 alters care. In this lecture, the broader precision-medicine framework supports that concept rather than presenting a dedicated BRCA slide.

⭐ STEP 1 / COURSE ENRICHMENT - BRCA

Germline BRCA1/2 variants can influence risk assessment, surveillance, risk-reducing surgery, family cascade testing, and treatment selection. In a tumor with homologous-recombination deficiency, PARP inhibition is a classic therapeutic application of synthetic lethality.

⚠️ GERMLINE vs SOMATIC

A germline BRCA variant is inherited and has implications for the patient and relatives. A somatic tumor alteration is acquired in the cancer. Both can matter therapeutically, but only germline findings automatically imply inherited familial risk.

LO6 - Newer Treatment Options: Targeted, Hormonal, and Immunotherapy

Monoclonal antibodies

The lecture reviews antibody-based treatment as a major targeted-therapy platform. The most useful board-level idea is to recognize that monoclonal antibodies bind extracellular or cell-surface targets, while many small-molecule kinase inhibitors act intracellularly.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Lecture p. 41 - major checkpoint classes: anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4.
Checkpoint classLecture examples
Anti-PD-1Nivolumab, pembrolizumab, cemiplimab
Anti-PD-L1Atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab
Anti-CTLA-4Ipilimumab
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - Checkpoint toxicity

Checkpoint blockade removes inhibitory signals from T cells, so toxicity often resembles autoimmunity: endocrinopathies, dermatitis/rash, colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, and nephritis.

Lecture p. 43 - cytotoxic chemotherapy toxicities are contrasted with immune checkpoint inhibitor toxicities.

CAR-T therapy

Lecture p. 35 - a patient's own lymphocytes are re-engineered to recognize and attack cancer cells.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD - CAR-T

CAR-T cells are engineered to recognize a tumor-associated surface antigen independent of conventional MHC presentation. Classic toxicities include cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity.

TIL therapy and allogeneic transplantation

The lecture also presents tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy as expansion of patient-derived tumor-reactive lymphocytes and allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation as another highly individualized cellular treatment strategy.

Lecture p. 44 - donor hematopoietic stem cells are collected, the patient receives conditioning, and donor cells are infused.
⭐ STEP 1 CONNECTION - Graft-versus-tumor

Allogeneic transplantation can treat hematologic malignancy not only through conditioning but also through a graft-versus-leukemia/tumor effect. The tradeoff is the risk of graft-versus-host disease and other transplant complications.

Rapid Step 1 / Course Review

Clonal selection

Therapy kills sensitive clones and selects resistant subclones.

Personalized medicine

Integrates patient + tumor characteristics to improve efficacy, dosing, toxicity, and prognosis.

Histology + molecular

Morphology defines the broad disease; molecular testing refines subtype, prognosis, and therapy.

Liquid biopsy

Repeatable molecular sampling from blood; complementary to tissue, not a universal replacement.

Oncotype DX

Gene-expression recurrence assay for selected HR+/HER2- early breast cancer.

DPYD / DPD

Reduced fluoropyrimidine breakdown → severe 5-FU/capecitabine toxicity.

UGT1A1

Reduced SN-38 inactivation → irinotecan-associated neutropenia/diarrhea.

G6PD

X-linked oxidant sensitivity; avoid high-risk oxidant drugs such as rasburicase in significant deficiency.

Somatic mutation

May define an actionable tumor target without implying inherited familial risk.

BRCA1/2

Homologous-recombination repair; germline findings affect family risk and can influence treatment.

Hormone receptors

ER-positive breast cancer creates an endocrine-therapy vulnerability.

Tamoxifen

ER antagonist in breast; VTE and endometrial-cancer risks.

PD-1 / PD-L1

Checkpoint blockade restores T-cell activity but can cause immune-mediated toxicity.

CAR-T

Engineered autologous T cells; CRS and neurotoxicity are classic complications.

AML genetics

PML-RARA, core-binding-factor lesions, and other molecular groups change prognosis and therapy.

Targeted therapy

Actionable tumor dependency → pathway-specific drug; resistance can emerge through clonal evolution.
