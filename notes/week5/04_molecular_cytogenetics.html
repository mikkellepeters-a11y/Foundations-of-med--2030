LO1 — Chromosome Structure Relative to Cytogenetic Analysis

Core idea: chromosome condensation, centromere position, chromosome arms, and banding patterns determine what can be seen and named cytogenetically.

Why metaphase matters

  • Chromosome compaction differs between interphase and mitosis.
  • Mitotic/metaphase chromosomes are in their most compact visible state, which makes them useful for conventional karyotyping.
  • The centromere is the chromosome region where the kinetochore forms; spindle microtubules attach to the kinetochore during mitosis.
Lecture pp. 5–8.

🔥 LECTURE EMPHASIS — Acrocentric chromosomes

13, 14, 15, 21, and 22 are the acrocentric chromosomes. This matters directly for Robertsonian translocations.

Lecture p. 14 — chromosome banding schematic, p/q arms, and the acrocentric chromosome set.

G-banding

  • Conventional karyotyping uses chromosome banding, especially G-banding with Giemsa stain.
  • The lecture describes trypsin or heat treatment to remove proteins before Giemsa staining.
  • Dark G-bands are AT-rich; the lecture emphasizes that AT-rich regions are often repetitive DNA.
  • The chromosome-specific distribution of light/dark bands creates a recognizable pattern.
  • Prometaphase/high-resolution banding uses less-condensed chromosomes when a subtle abnormality is suspected.
Lecture pp. 9–10; handout pp. 93–94.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD

The more condensed a chromosome is, the easier it is to count and identify gross changes, but the less fine detail is visible. Less-condensed prometaphase chromosomes provide more bands and therefore higher structural resolution.

LO2 — Basic Cytogenetic Nomenclature

Core idea: identify chromosome number, sex chromosomes, chromosome arm/band, and the type of abnormality from standardized notation.

Karyotype organization and band naming

  • A human karyotype contains 22 pairs of autosomes plus the sex chromosomes.
  • Chromosomes are arranged in numerical order and recognized by size, centromere position, and banding pattern.
  • The ISCN (International System for Human Cytogenomic/Cytogenetic Nomenclature) standardizes chromosome notation.
  • Band numbering proceeds from the centromere outward along p or q.

Vocabulary: chromosome number and benign variants

  • Euploid: chromosome number is an exact multiple of the haploid set.
  • Aneuploidy: chromosome number is not an exact multiple of the haploid set, e.g., monosomy or trisomy.
  • Heteroploidy: alteration in chromosome number.
  • Heteromorphism: variation in chromosome morphology or staining, usually benign and often reflecting satellite-DNA differences.
  • Trisomy: three copies of a particular chromosome.
  • Monosomy: one copy of a particular chromosome.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — Aneuploidy vs polyploidy

Aneuploidy changes the number of individual chromosomes, such as 47,+21. Polyploidy changes the number of entire haploid sets, such as triploidy. Both are chromosome-number abnormalities, but only the first is aneuploidy.

LO3 — Karyotype Analysis

Core idea: conventional cytogenetics visually examines stained metaphase chromosomes to identify chromosome number and relatively large structural changes.

How a conventional karyotype is made

  1. Obtain viable cells, commonly peripheral-blood leukocytes; fibroblasts, bone marrow, or fetal cells may also be used.
  2. Use PHA (phytohemagglutinin) / mitogenic stimulation and culture cells.
  3. Add colchicine to arrest cells in mitosis so chromosomes remain condensed.
  4. Treat with a hypotonic solution so cells swell.
  5. Fix cells and drop them onto slides so chromosomes spread out.
  6. Apply Giemsa/G-banding.
  7. Identify homologous chromosomes by band pattern, size, and centromere position to create the karyotype.
Lecture pp. 8–10 and handout pp. 93–94.

What conventional karyotyping is good at

  • Whole-chromosome numerical abnormalities such as trisomy.
  • Relatively large visible deletions, duplications, translocations, and other structural abnormalities.
  • Surveying all chromosomes at once rather than only a single targeted locus.

Major limitations

🔥 LECTURE EMPHASIS — Know these limitations
  • Cannot be performed on uncultured interphase cells.
  • Small chromosomal changes may be below its limit of detection, particularly microdeletions and microduplications.

LO4 — FISH and Comparison With Conventional Cytogenetics

Core idea: FISH uses fluorescently detected DNA probes to identify complementary chromosome sequences in either metaphase chromosomes or interphase nuclei.

FISH workflow

  1. Prepare a metaphase chromosome spread or an interphase nucleus.
  2. Denature the DNA so the strands separate.
  3. Add a complementary DNA probe with a fluorescent tag or a detectable tag such as biotin.
  4. Allow probe-target hybridization.
  5. Wash away nonspecific binding.
  6. Counterstain chromosomes/nuclei, commonly with DAPI.
  7. Visualize discrete fluorescent signals with a fluorescence microscope.
Lecture pp. 59–62.

🔥 LECTURE EMPHASIS — Deletion vs aneuploidy probe choice

Aneuploidy/counting question → chromosome-specific centromere/CEP probe is the most direct.
Known microdeletion → locus-specific/cosmid probe.
Whole-chromosome paint is useful when the question involves the identity or rearrangement of larger chromosome material.

⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — FISH is targeted

FISH can be much more sensitive than a karyotype for a known target, but the probe has to match the region you want to interrogate. A normal signal at one locus does not prove the entire genome is normal.

Trisomy overview

  • The lecture identifies trisomy 21, 18, and 13 as the major autosomal trisomies seen in live births.

  • APP is on chromosome 21 → extra gene dosage contributes to early Alzheimer-type pathology.

LO6 — How Numerical and Structural Chromosome Abnormalities Occur

Core idea: numerical abnormalities usually arise from nondisjunction, while structural abnormalities arise from chromosome breakage, abnormal recombination, or segregation of rearranged chromosomes.

Meiotic nondisjunction

🔥 EXPLICITLY LABELED “HIGH YIELD” IN THE LECTURE

Meiosis I nondisjunction: homologous chromosomes fail to separate. Abnormal gametes receive both homologs (one maternal + one paternal homolog) or neither. In the lecture’s simplified gamete model, there is no normal gamete outcome.

Meiosis II nondisjunction: sister chromatids fail to separate. Abnormal gametes receive two identical sister-chromatid copies or neither, while the other two gametes can be normal.

Lecture p. 34 — nondisjunction can yield trisomic or monosomic conceptions after fertilization.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — Meiosis I vs Meiosis II

Meiosis I error = homologs fail to separate.
Meiosis II error = sister chromatids fail to separate.

Origins of Down syndrome emphasized in the lecture

  1. Free trisomy 21 from nondisjunction — ~95%.
  2. Robertsonian translocation — <5%.
  3. 21q21q rearrangement / isochromosome mechanism.
  4. Mosaic Down syndrome due to postzygotic/mitotic nondisjunction.
  5. Partial trisomy from duplication — very rare.
Lecture pp. 33–56.

Robertsonian translocation

  • Occurs between acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21, and 22.
  • The long arms fuse near the centromere, with loss of the short p arms.
  • The lecture explains that the lost p-arm material largely contains redundant rRNA gene copies, so carriers may remain phenotypically normal.
  • A balanced carrier therefore may have 45 chromosomes yet no obvious phenotype.
  • During meiosis, segregation can generate normal, balanced-carrier, or unbalanced gametes, producing increased risk of miscarriage or an abnormal live-born child.
Lecture p. 43 — theoretical segregation outcomes from a rob(14;21) carrier.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Robertsonian Down syndrome

Translocation Down syndrome is important because a parent may be a phenotypically normal balanced carrier. Unlike ordinary age-related nondisjunction, this raises a family-specific recurrence issue and is why parental karyotyping may matter after a translocation case.

Isochromosome

  • An isochromosome has lost one arm and duplicated the other, producing two copies of the same arm.
  • In a somatic cell, this produces extra dosage of the duplicated-arm material and reduced dosage of the lost-arm material.
  • If generated during gametogenesis, fertilization may yield trisomy for duplicated-arm material and monosomy for lost-arm material.
Lecture pp. 48–51.

Somatic mosaicism

  • Occurs when nondisjunction happens after fertilization during mitotic divisions.
  • Some cell lines have the abnormal chromosome complement while others do not.
  • The lecture notes that mosaic phenotypes are often less severe because not every cell is affected.
Lecture pp. 52–53 and handout p. 99.
⚠️ COMMONLY CONFUSED — Germline vs somatic event

Constitutional/germline abnormality originates in a gamete or germ-cell lineage and can be present throughout the individual and transmitted to offspring. Somatic mosaicism arises after fertilization and affects only a subset of cells.

Duplication and partial trisomy

  • Duplication adds an extra copy of a chromosome segment, producing partial trisomy for that region.
  • Can result from unequal crossing over or abnormal meiotic segregation.
  • Duplications are often less harmful than deletions, but breakpoints can disrupt important genes.
Lecture pp. 54–55.

Chromosomal Deletions and Microdeletion Syndromes

Deletion mechanics

  • Terminal deletion: one break with loss of the distal chromosome segment.
  • Interstitial deletion: two breaks with loss of an internal segment.
  • The phenotype depends on the size of the deletion and the function of genes lost.
  • An individual with one normal homolog and one deleted homolog is hemizygous for the deleted region.
Lecture pp. 75–76.

Why recurrent microdeletions occur

The lecture emphasizes unequal crossing over between misaligned homologous/repetitive sequences. Highly similar repeats can pair incorrectly, leading to deletion in one product and duplication in the reciprocal product.

Lecture p. 77 — repeated DNA sequences can misalign and undergo unequal crossing over.

Contiguous-gene syndrome and haploinsufficiency

  • A microdeletion can remove multiple neighboring genes, producing a contiguous-gene syndrome.
  • The phenotype reflects haploinsufficiency of one or more genes: one functional copy is not enough for a normal phenotype.
  • Because these deletions can be too small for routine karyotyping, FISH with a locus-specific probe can identify the missing region.
Lecture pp. 81–83.

Cri-du-chat syndrome

  • Deletion of chromosome 5p.
  • Classic infant cry described as a mewing/cat-like cry.
  • Lecture features include hypertelorism, epicanthal folds, retrognathia, and severe intellectual disability.
  • Many cases are sporadic; the lecture also notes that a subset can result from an unbalanced product of a balanced parental translocation.
  • For a known 5p deletion, use a locus-specific/cosmid FISH probe.
Lecture pp. 78–80.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Cri-du-chat

5p deletion → high-pitched cat-like cry, microcephaly/intellectual disability, characteristic facial features.

Professor-priority microdeletion syndromes

🔥 RED PRINT = KNOW NOW

The handout explicitly says the syndromes printed in red are the ones to know now. The red items are Prader-Willi/Angelman (15q11–13) and DiGeorge/velocardiofacial syndrome (22q11). Other listed syndromes are noted for later coursework/Step review.

⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — Imprinting nuance

Paternal loss → Prader-Willi. Maternal loss → Angelman. FISH can detect a physical deletion, but not every case of these syndromes is caused by a deletion. Board questions may involve uniparental disomy or imprinting defects, which require other molecular testing.

DiGeorge / velocardiofacial syndrome

  • 22q11 deletion with variable expressivity.
  • The lecture emphasizes craniofacial anomalies and congenital heart disease, including pulmonary outflow/conotruncal abnormalities and tetralogy of Fallot.
  • Detected with a locus-specific FISH probe; a missing disease-region signal with intact control signals demonstrates the deletion.
Lecture pp. 84–86 and handout p. 101.
Lecture p. 85 — two green control signals are present, while one red 22q11.2 signal is missing.
⭐ STEP 1 HIGH YIELD — DiGeorge

Classically: 22q11.2 deletion involving pharyngeal pouch development → conotruncal cardiac defects, thymic hypoplasia/aplasia with T-cell deficiency, and hypocalcemia from parathyroid hypoplasia. A commonly used board mnemonic is CATCH-22.

⚠️ Lecture-number caution

The lecture/handout lists an approximately “3,000 Mb” deletion for DiGeorge. That numeric size is inconsistent with the concept of a 22q11 microdeletion, so this summary does not use that number as a memorization target. The tested locus is 22q11/22q11.2.

Other microdeletion/contiguous-gene syndromes listed in the lecture

The lecture also lists Cat-eye syndrome, neurofibromatosis, ichthyosis, Williams syndrome, and Smith-Magenis syndrome, but the handout explicitly separates these from the red-print “know now” group.

⭐ STEP 1 ENRICHMENT — selected later-board associations
  • Williams syndrome: 7q11.23 deletion including ELN → supravalvular aortic stenosis, hypercalcemia, characteristic social/personality phenotype.
  • Smith-Magenis syndrome: classically deletion of 17p11.2.
